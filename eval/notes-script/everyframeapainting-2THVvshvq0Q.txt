- channel: Every Frame a Painting, narrated by Tony; installment about Michael Bay and his "Bayhem" look
- opener joke: imagines viewer asking why bother with him; Tony dislikes the movies yet insists on analyzing them
- Herzog interview snippet (WrestleMania, Anna Nicole Smith, Jackass): keep watching, it's what's aimed at us; spectacle is what audiences crave
- thesis: improving filmmaking requires grasping the imagery bombarding viewers
- approach: look at imitators; test case is a botched circular hero-shot move in Battleship
- Battleship problems: nothing but blue sky behind, so no parallax; wrong lens, since Bay often uses telephoto to squash space and make backgrounds streak; actor merely swivels head rather than rising or dropping; low angle gives scale, slow motion sells it
- real Bay shot stacks camera motion, background motion, actor motion, stretched time, then ends on a still gaze offscreen, yet feels enormous
- no single technique original: depth, parallax, hero shots are standard; Bay's distinction is layer count and complexity, which isn't the same as better
- busy frames: dust, dirt, smoke, blasts between layers, plus many lamp-posts as scale cues; first Bad Boys credits sequence as proof
- weakness: can't tell when a shot suits the moment; identical move for important lines and nonsense dialogue (quote clips from Bay films)
- Jurassic Park cited as another film that handles bigness well; looking offscreen suggests more planes beyond the frame
- inspiration: West Side Story; NYT interview where Bay praises shots and cuts but only says they're "dynamic"
- quip: Howard Hawks wanted three great scenes and zero bad ones; Bay seemingly wants three thousand moving shots, none static
- Bay recycles other blockbusters (closer close-ups, broader wides, extra motion layers) and himself (camera spins one way, bomb the other, amplified)
- summary definition: motion, composition, rapid cutting for epic scale; very long or very wide lenses; edits outpace comprehension but not eye movement; just old vocabulary made grimier and busier
- related styles: late Tony Scott pushes it further; animator Glen Keane is clearer; favorite spin-off is a miniature, charming English-town version (the "Swan!" bit, probably Hot Fuzz)
- ending: audiences are visually quick yet unable to interpret; Bay is captive to his own eye, forcing dynamism even against his story's theme; closes on Fargo's Norm and Margie scene as a quiet-storytelling contrast
