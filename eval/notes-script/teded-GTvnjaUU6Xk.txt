- intro: passing gas is ordinary daily physiology; roughly 500–1500 mL total, about 10–20 times daily; creator jokes that the viewer does it too
- small share from swallowed air, e.g. during sleep; bulk is made by gut microbes working on food we can't digest ourselves
- trillions of intestinal bacteria in mutualism; we give lodging and meals
- what we get back: extra energy from food, vitamins B and K, stronger immunity, help with barrier function, motility, development of organ systems
- creator's view: clearly sensible to keep the microbes content
- the stink: volatile sulfur compounds (hydrogen sulfide, methanethiol a.k.a. methyl mercaptan), under 1% of volume
- sulfur-rich amino acids likely explain smelly gas on high-protein diets
- more gas after indigestible-carb foods: beans, lentils, dairy, plus alliums (onion, garlic, leek), radish, potato, oats, wheat, and the cruciferous group (cauliflower, broccoli, cabbage, Brussels sprouts)
- we lack the enzymes, so carb-fermenting microbes step in, giving above-usual gas
- feeling uncomfortable, bloated or visibly swollen may signal gas moving poorly through the GI tract
- caution: avoid scapegoating and eliminating foods; starved microbes may turn to sugars in the gut's mucus layer
- individual gas depends on diet and microbial makeup; same sugar yields CO2, butyrate, H2 with Clostridium but CO2, propionate, acetate with Propionibacterium
- methanogens turn other microbes' H2 and CO2 into methane, lowering overall gas volume
- tangled cross-feeding network decides amount and kind of gas; producing gas shows the microbes are active
- abnormal example: lactose intolerance; lactase absent or reduced, e.g. post-GI infection; fermentation of undigested lactose brings cramps and more gas
- closing joke: be polite about timing, and go easy on your microbes, since helping is their intent
