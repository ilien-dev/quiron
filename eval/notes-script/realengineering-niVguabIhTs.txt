- Frank Lloyd Wright, 1956: "The Illinois", 1.7 km; possible in theory, impractical (elevators not ready, sway discomfort)
- physics setup: slender tall structures vibrate in wind; shaking lamp posts as familiar case; cylinder in wind tunnel as the model
- low speed: force just follows wind direction; higher speed: flow detaches, two symmetric eddies form; Galway river eddy, kayakers rest in it
- critical point: eddies lose symmetry and oscillate, vortex shedding starts, sideways force alternates
- danger when shedding frequency equals structure's natural frequency; sway amplitude builds each cycle; natural damping (materials, joint friction) can fall short, so engineers add mechanical dampers
- Taipei 101, once world's tallest, displays a 730-tonne tuned mass damper publicly
- 8 August 2015: category 5 typhoon on Taiwan, damper moving, caught by webcam
- mechanism: damper lags the tower, tuned to building's frequency so moves opposite; pistons carry opposing force, dissipating kinetic energy
- creator finds it amazing Burj has no damper; shape stops vortices organising
- constant cross-section lets poles shed vortices in step along full height; fix = helical strakes on chimneys and offshore platforms (liquids shed vortices too)
- Burj footprint modelled on a desert flower (hymenocallis); Y-shaped wings with concrete core for torsion, buttresses for bending like I-beam; promised future video
- setbacks spiral upward like strakes, so vortices can't line up; design relied on computer analysis and wind tunnel testing
- outlook: supertalls getting easier to build, record will keep changing hands; urban population passed 50% in 2007, 33% twenty years earlier, ~80% projected by 2050; supertalls may become urban necessity rather than showy status symbols
