- Veritasium street experiment: host hands passersby a coin-flip wager, each risking $10 of their own cash
- framing: the wager works as a stand-in for how we handle risk in life, money is beside the point
- most decline a fair 50/50 offer; one objects to gambling as a principle, another says they lack ten dollars on them
- host raises the prize step by step: $12, $15, then $20 against the $10 loss, which he calls his ceiling (2:1)
- people still say no; one would need $50 before agreeing, another says nothing under $100
- host's math: the $20-vs-$10 wager averages +$5 per flip, yet interviewees dwell on what they could lose
- named effect: loss aversion; a loss stings about double a gain on average, individuals differ
- twist: same bet repeated 10 times, then 100 times; interviewees hold firm, call it intuition with no rational basis
- host's reply: across 100 tosses expect roughly 45–55 wins each way; average profit $500; losing anything is about a 1/2300 shot
- even if losses count double, the 100-round package is plainly a good deal, so refusing the single flip looks inconsistent
- caveat: not a pitch for casinos, where the odds run against you
- lesson: see each small gamble as one in a long sequence; accepting them all tends to leave you ahead overall
- on-camera flips: one person wins $12, another takes $20; host quips about his bad luck that day
