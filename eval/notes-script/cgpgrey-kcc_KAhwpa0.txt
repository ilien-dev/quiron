- topic: 2012 Mayan doomsday myth, set against a long record of apocalypse predictions that all flopped
- creator scoffs at the notion that the 2012 one is finally correct
- Mayan calendars counted days and years plus enormous cycles of roughly 5,000 years, so they could project thousands of years ahead
- the calendars stop around 2012; modern new-age people took that as proof the Mayans foresaw the end of everything
- new-agers imagined a spiritual rebirth or planetary reset; creator labels them happy but science-illiterate, calls it hippy consciousness-raising nonsense
- joke: a culture that drew thorny strings through tongues probably wasn't imagining that
- logic complaint: a calendar finishing doesn't mean the world finishes; time is endless, stone supply isn't, so carvers had to quit eventually
- fringe idea ballooned into mass hysteria thanks to the 2012 disaster movie (creator claims it dominated the Oscars) and Emmy-nominated doomsday documentaries
- sighing jab at The History Channel, asking what became of them
- NASA swamped with questions, pulled away from robots and exploration to post a webpage rebutting it
- sarcastic tone toward NASA's busy schedule; Mayans framed as a stone-age society with human sacrifice, no wheels for carts, no glass for telescopes
- escalating predictions: magnetic pole flip (takes ages, not one day), collision with Planet X (no astronomer has spotted it), nearby star exploding (no candidates exist)
- alien invasion: creator calls it absurd, then hints "or is it?"
- galactic alignment beam: creator admits to having no idea what it is
- closing argument: how could they predict cosmic anomalies across vast distances and millennia but not see the Spanish crossing the Atlantic?
- conclusion: Mayans never forecast any apocalypse; the claim came only from non-Mayans
