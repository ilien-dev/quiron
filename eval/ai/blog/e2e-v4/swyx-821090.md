# September 11th from Singapore

I was a teenager in Singapore when the planes hit the towers. It was evening there (we're twelve hours ahead of New York) and I don't remember it as one clear memory, just fragments: the TV on in the living room, my parents unusually quiet, the same footage looping again and again until it stopped looking real.

Every year around this date I end up thinking about how something on the other side of the world reshaped so much of the one I was growing up in.

## Watching from far away

A lot of Americans remember September 11th with total clarity: where they were, who they called, what the sky looked like. For me it came through a screen. No smoke in the air, nobody I knew in the buildings. And yet it felt enormous.

Singapore is a small, tightly connected country, and we learned about the world through the news. That night the news was one story. At school the next day the teachers tried to explain what had happened, and I don't think any of them really knew how. We held a moment of silence. I remember it as the quietest my school had ever been.

In the months and years after, it showed up in small ways around me. Airport security got noticeably stricter. The local news talked more about terrorism, and there was more attention on national security at home. Travel had always felt easy, and now it came with more questions, more lines and more forms.

As I got older I started to get that the world I was walking into as a young adult was shaped heavily by that day. The wars, the politics, the way we think about privacy and surveillance - so much of it goes back to those few hours.

## So why is this on a dev blog?

Fair question! I've come to see a connection.

A lot of what we build today carries the fingerprints of a post-9/11 world: identity verification, data retention policies, content moderation, access control. The tradeoff between security and privacy is not some abstract debate for engineers. We make calls on it every day, sometimes without noticing: what we log, what we store, who can see it, and for how long.

I think about that more now. The tools we build can protect people, and they can also be used to watch them. Remembering how fast the norms shifted after that day makes me want to be more deliberate about the defaults I set.

Nearly three thousand people died that day, and many more have died since from illnesses linked to the recovery effort. Behind every one of those numbers is a family who still feels it.

I didn't lose anyone. I watched from far away. But I think it's worth remembering even from a distance, because in some way the day belongs to all of us. It changed the world my generation grew up in, wherever we happened to be.

If you have your own memory of that day, from wherever you were, I'd be grateful if you shared it in the comments. It helps to remember together.
