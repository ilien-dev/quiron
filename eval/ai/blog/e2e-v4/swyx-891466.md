# Community Heat, or Why You Should Get Good at Events

There's a concept I keep coming back to when I think about careers and community building, and about how ideas spread in tech: **heat**. Not literal heat, obviously. I mean the energy and momentum that builds up around a person, a project or an idea once it starts getting attention. And one of the most reliable ways to make that heat is events: talks, meetups, conferences, livestreams, panels, whatever form they take.

So this is my case for getting good at events (speaking at them, organizing them, or just showing up and taking part). I think it's one of the highest-leverage things you can do for your career and your community, even if public speaking terrifies you right now.

## What is community heat?

It's the buzz that builds around something when enough people pay attention to it at the same time. A blog post that gets a handful of quiet reads vs one that gets shared and argued about on every platform at once. A project that picks up a few GitHub stars over months vs one that is suddenly trending, with people tweeting about it, writing follow-up posts and building on top of it within days.

Heat isn't only about quality, though quality helps. It's about being visible at the right moment, amplified by the right people, in front of the right audience. Events are one of the best ways I know to make that happen.

## Why events do this better than almost anything else

**They create a shared moment.** You can read a blog post anytime, at your own pace, alone. A conference talk happens at one specific time, in front of a room (physical or virtual) full of people who all go through it together. When a talk lands, you can feel the room shift, and everyone in it feels the shift with you. People tweet about it during and talk about it after, and it keeps coming up in conversations for months. Async content can't do that.

**They compress time.** Building an audience slowly through regular blogging or open source work does work! But it takes a long time. One good conference talk can do more for your visibility in twenty minutes than months of quietly shipping code or writing posts that don't quite catch fire. The slow work still matters (it's usually what gives you something worth talking about in the first place), but the event is often what takes all that quiet value and puts it in front of many more people at once.

**They spread.** When you speak at an event you aren't only reaching the people in the room. You reach everyone they talk to afterward, everyone who watches the recording, everyone who reads the tweetstorm summary, and whoever stumbles on a blog post about your talk months later. And it adds up over time. Every talk makes you a bit more present in that community, so you're more likely to get invited to the next event, show up in the next roundup post, or come to mind when someone needs an expert on your topic.

## You don't have to be the one on stage

When people hear "get good at events" they tend to assume it means becoming a polished public speaker. That's one path. There are others.

- **Organizing** meetups, conferences or panels puts you at the center of a community's energy without you being on stage. Organizers build relationships with speakers, attendees and sponsors, and they often have outsized say in where a community goes, because they decide what gets amplified.
- **Showing up** as an engaged attendee counts for more than people think. Ask good questions after talks, introduce people to each other, be a visible presence at the events. That builds real social capital even if you never give a talk.
- **Documenting** events (live-tweeting talks, writing recap posts, putting together highlight threads) is underrated. You become the person who captures and passes on the community's best ideas, which is a role of its own, and it builds heat around the event and around you.

## But I'm scared of public speaking

I get it. Public speaking is one of the most common fears there is, and standing in front of a room (or a camera, for virtual events) can feel paralyzing. A few things that have helped people I know:

- **Start small.** A five-minute lightning talk at a local meetup is a much lower-stakes way to learn than jumping straight to a keynote.
- **Talk about what you've actually built or learned.** Real experience beats polish. People connect with what you've done, not with performed expertise.
- **Practice out loud, a lot.** Reading your slides in your head is not the same as saying the words out loud several times before the real thing.
- **Call the nerves excitement.** Your body does nearly the same thing either way; the story you tell yourself about it changes how it feels.

The people you see generating heat around their work aren't necessarily more talented than everyone else. Often they're simply visible more often, and events are one of the most efficient ways to become visible. Every talk you give, every meetup you organize, every event you show up to and engage with, adds to how the community sees you. Over years that turns into reputation and trust, and into opportunities that async work alone rarely brings.

If you're doing good work and nobody seems to notice, it might not be a quality problem. It might be a visibility problem. You don't need to be a world-class keynote speaker overnight. Start small and keep showing up. Let yourself learn this gradually. The heat around your work can matter as much as the work itself, and events are where a lot of it gets made.
