# How to find consulting clients

Finding consulting clients can feel like a chicken-and-egg problem: companies want experienced consultants, but you can't get experience until somebody hires you.

The good news is that there's nothing mysterious about getting clients. It's a process you can repeat, and it comes down to four things:

1. Pick a specific problem
2. Show that you can solve it
3. Start conversations with the right people
4. Keep following up

You don't need a huge audience, a fancy website or a perfect personal brand. You need a clear offer and a reliable way to reach the people who might need it.

## Start with a problem, not a job title

"Software consultant" is too broad. It tells people what you are, but not why they should hire you.

A better pitch connects a particular kind of customer to an outcome they care about:

> I help growing SaaS companies reduce AWS costs without slowing down their engineering teams.

Some other examples:

- Helping startups prepare their infrastructure for enterprise security reviews
- Migrating legacy React applications to modern frameworks
- Improving slow PostgreSQL queries for high-traffic products
- Building internal tools that replace spreadsheet-heavy workflows
- Helping engineering teams establish reliable CI/CD pipelines

Being specific makes marketing easier, because you know what to write about, who to contact and which problems to talk about. It makes referrals easier too. Nobody remembers that you're a "general technology consultant" - but they do remember the person who fixes expensive cloud infrastructure problems.

And your focus doesn't have to be permanent! It only has to be specific enough that someone knows when to call you.

## Make a small, concrete offer

A lot of new consultants try to sell an open-ended engagement right away. That's risky for the client, because they can't tell what the scope, the price or the result will be.

Start with a small offer that's easy to understand and easy to buy instead. For example:

> A two-week performance audit covering database queries, application bottlenecks, and infrastructure configuration, followed by a prioritized remediation plan.

A good starter offer has:

- A clear target customer
- A recognizable problem
- A defined scope
- A limited timeline
- A useful deliverable
- A visible business outcome

An assessment, an audit, a workshop, a prototype or a focused implementation can all turn into bigger work later. The client gets to try you out without signing up for a six-month project, and you get the context you need to spot more opportunities.

Don't give the whole engagement away as a "free consultation" though. A short intro call can be free. Diagnosis and recommendations are real professional work, and you should get paid for them.

## Begin with people who already trust you

Your first clients will probably come from people you already know, not from strangers on the internet.

So make a list:

- Former coworkers and managers
- Founders you know
- Previous customers or vendors
- People from technical communities
- Friends working at relevant companies
- Agencies offering adjacent services

Then message them one at a time. Don't announce that you're "available for anything." Tell them what you focus on, and make your ask easy to act on:

> I’ve started helping SaaS teams diagnose and reduce cloud infrastructure costs. I’m looking for companies whose AWS bill has grown faster than their usage. If someone comes to mind, would you be comfortable introducing us?

That isn't aggressive selling. You're giving people enough information to recognize a possible match.

Don't push your contacts to invent work for you, either. Ask for advice, some context or an introduction when it makes sense. A conversation today might turn into a referral three months from now.

## Look for signals, not just companies

Cold outreach works better when it's based on evidence.

Instead of putting together a random list of businesses, look for signs that a company might have the problem you solve. Maybe they're hiring multiple engineers or they announced a new funding round. Maybe they're migrating to a new platform, or they had a public outage, or they're launching an enterprise product. Watch for companies posting about rising infrastructure costs, opening roles related to your specialty, or talking about their technical challenges in public.

A good outreach message ties what you know to what's going on with them:

> I saw that your team is hiring several platform engineers as you expand the enterprise product. Teams at that stage often discover that deployment permissions and audit trails become blockers during customer reviews. I help SaaS companies address those gaps. Is that something your team is encountering?

You're not trying to close a deal in one message. You're trying to find out whether the problem is even there.

Keep it short. Don't attach a proposal, don't drop a calendar link with no context, and don't list every service you offer.

## Publish proof that you know your stuff

Clients can't look inside your head, so they look for evidence. That could be technical articles, case studies, open-source contributions or conference talks. It could be architecture reviews, before-and-after performance measurements, testimonials, or detailed explanations of work you've done before.

Write about the problems you want to get hired for. If you want database optimization projects, an article about CSS architecture might show you're smart, but it won't help people see you as the database person.

Good content is concrete. Explain how you figured out what was wrong, what options you looked at, which tradeoffs mattered and what changed after the fix went in.

If the client details are confidential, anonymize it:

> A subscription company was processing billing jobs sequentially. We redesigned the workflow around idempotent parallel workers, reducing processing time from hours to minutes.

One strong case study is usually worth more than ten generic posts.

Content rarely brings in leads immediately on its own. Where it pays off is when someone gets your message, searches for your name, and finds real evidence that you understand their problem.

## Get to know other service providers

Other consultants and agencies can be great sources of work. They might need specialized expertise they don't offer themselves, or extra capacity during a busy period. They might need someone to maintain a system after launch, or someone they trust to hand the leads that are too small or outside their focus.

Look for people whose services complement yours instead of competing with them directly. A designer might partner with a frontend consultant. A product agency might need a security specialist. A fractional CTO might need implementation partners they can count on.

Go into it trying to be useful to them. Find out what a good referral looks like for them, pass along opportunities that fit, and be someone they can depend on when you work together.

A small group of partners you trust can give you a steadier pipeline than a big social media following.

## Treat discovery like a diagnosis

When a prospect agrees to talk, fight the urge to start pitching. Ask questions instead:

- What prompted you to address this now?
- What happens if the problem is never fixed?
- Who is affected by it?
- What have you already tried?
- What constraints must a solution respect?
- Who is involved in approving the project?
- What would a successful outcome look like?

You're figuring out whether the problem matters, whether you can solve it, and whether the client is ready to do something about it.

Before you suggest an approach, summarize what you heard. It shows you understand the situation, and it gives them a chance to correct your assumptions.

And if there's no real problem, say so! Saving a prospect from work they don't need builds more trust than steering every conversation toward a sale.

## Follow up without being annoying

Consulting deals stall all the time. Priorities change, budgets need approval, people get busy. Silence doesn't always mean no.

When you follow up, have a reason:

- Share a relevant article
- Clarify a recommendation
- Provide a concise project outline
- Mention a new observation
- Ask whether the priority has changed

Keep simple notes on each conversation and pick a date for the next step. A spreadsheet is fine for this.

Skip the messages that only say "Just checking in." Add some context or something useful, and make it easy for them to give you an honest answer - even if the answer is "not now."

## Do business development every week

If you only look for clients when your current project ends, you're putting unnecessary pressure on yourself. Keep doing business development while you're busy.

A weekly routine you can actually keep up might look like:

- Contacting five well-matched prospects
- Following up with five existing conversations
- Reconnecting with two people in your network
- Publishing one useful technical insight
- Speaking with one potential referral partner

Track activities you control, not just signed contracts. Revenue shows up late and you can't predict it, but sending a thoughtful message or having a conversation is something you can do today.

Over time, pay attention to which channels bring in real opportunities. Put more effort there, and cut back on the stuff that gets attention but doesn't lead to conversations.

## Finally

Most consultants don't struggle because they don't know enough. They struggle because their offer is vague, they only reach out every now and then, or they give up on following up too early. What works is doing the whole routine again every week.

Finding clients gets less intimidating once it stops being an emergency that happens every so often and becomes part of the job. You don't need everyone to know who you are. You need a few of the right people to know exactly how you can help.
