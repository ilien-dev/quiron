# Community Heat, or Why You Should Get Good at Events

One piece of advice I keep repeating to founders: get good at events. This started as a braindump for my newsletter so it's rougher than usual and I'm not claiming to be an expert on any of it.

The clearest example is Apple. When Apple teases a "Special Event", journalists clear their schedules and rivals delay their own launches, so Apple gets millions in earned attention from one event.

What got me writing this down was watching Next.js Conf and then [a chat I had with Lee Robinson from Vercel](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mlsTlFW7BSo).

## Community Heat

I borrow the idea of hot and cold media from McLuhan and apply it to communities:

- **async is cold**: forums, mailing lists
- **chat is mixed**
- **IRL is white-hot**

The cold channels are bigger, but people in them are less engaged. (Chat being "mixed" is being generous, because I am so tired of waiting on a typing indicator only to get "haha".)

My thesis is that a community should alternate between hot and cold, like [annealing](https://www.swyx.io/blogpost-annealing/). The Slack vs Discord question that everyone argues about matters very little if you don't have events.

As a rough rule I'd aim for an online:offline ratio of 90/10 or 80/20. Pick 5 to 10 IRL events and make them memorable, then book the IRL events 3 to 6 months out.

## Tools

For online events, the options I know about:

- Hopin
- the Vercel Event Starter Kit, which is what React Conf used
- just YouTube, which is all Svelte Summit used
- Gather.town, though I suspect the novelty may fade
- Zoom for under 100 people, and Zoom Webinars above 100

## Cadence

Some companies whose rhythm you can copy:

- **Yearly**: [Cloudflare's Birthday Week](https://www.swyx.io/cloudflare-go/). The downside is that it rushes launches to hit the date, and AWS does something similar with its pile of announcements before re:Invent.
- **Quarterly**: WorkOS.
- **Monthly**: Temporal runs a [monthly meetup](https://temporal.io/meetup), and each one builds the mailing list (the "GitNation strategy").
- **Weekly**: Learn with Jason, Containers from the Couch.

## Naming

I hate the word "webinar" and don't want to attend a webinar, but I'd attend a "workshop". "Teach-ins" and "office hours" are other names that work.

## Stop Copying the 8-Hour Conference

When Covid hit, conferences copied their 8-hour days straight onto the internet, but nobody watches 8 hours of a conference online. It's the Windows mobile port all over again, and that took about 10 years to go native.

Svelte Summit in 2020 copied the old format too. The better format for an online conference: accept every talk, curate the finished videos, run a 4-hour main stage, and release the rest through the year.

Related opinion: blind CFPs miss great speakers who only ever do invite-only talks, so if you're curating a conference you should reach out to people.

## Marketing

Push registrations above everything else. People sign up because other people signed up ([reflexivity](https://www.swyx.io/mimicry-reflexivity/)), so the number feeds on itself.

Then measure async views against live views: about 0.5:1 is bad, 10:1 is good and 1000:1 is legendary. The example I have in mind got 70,000 signups.

Post-production is worth the effort, and if you have tips on making trailers I'd love to hear them.
