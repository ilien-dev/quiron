# If you don't hire juniors, you don't deserve seniors

Every senior developer was once a junior developer who needed someone to take a chance on them.

They wrote code they later regretted. They misunderstood requirements. They needed help debugging problems that now take them five minutes to recognize. Someone gave them work they weren’t fully prepared for, reviewed their pull requests, answered their questions, and kept trusting them as they learned.

Yet many companies want to hire senior developers while refusing to hire juniors. They want people who can navigate ambiguity, make sound technical decisions, and help a team grow. They just don’t want to participate in creating those people.

That’s a convenient strategy while someone else pays for the training. It’s also a bad bet for the industry.

## Seniors don’t appear fully formed

A job description can make seniority look like a shopping list: eight years of experience, expertise in a particular stack, system design skills, communication skills, and a record of shipping reliable software.

But those abilities aren’t downloaded with a title. They develop through exposure and feedback.

A developer learns to estimate work by making estimates and seeing where they were wrong. They learn to design maintainable systems by living with systems that were hard to change. They learn to handle incidents by being included in incident response, first with support and eventually with more responsibility.

Most of all, they learn judgment by making decisions and seeing the consequences.

You can’t get all of that from tutorials, bootcamps, personal projects, or interviews. Those can provide valuable foundations, but professional software development involves constraints that are hard to reproduce alone: changing requirements, production failures, security concerns, accessibility needs, budgets, deadlines, and other people.

A junior needs access to that environment to grow. If every employer insists that someone arrive with experience, the door closes before many capable people can get through it.

## “We don’t have time to train juniors”

This objection is understandable. Teams are busy. Deadlines are real. A junior developer can require more attention than they initially contribute, and a company with a fragile product or a team in crisis may not be able to support one well.

But *“we don’t have time”* can also become a permanent policy disguised as a temporary constraint.

If your team has been too busy to teach for three years, the problem may not be the availability of junior-friendly work. It may be that the organization has built a system with no room for learning, documentation, or knowledge sharing.

That has costs beyond hiring. When only a few people understand critical parts of the codebase, those people become bottlenecks. When every task requires a senior developer, experienced engineers spend their days answering the same questions and rescuing work that could have been made more approachable.

Teaching a junior forces a team to make assumptions visible. Why is deployment done this way? Where does this service get its data? What makes a change risky? If the answers exist only in someone’s head, the team has a problem whether or not it hires a junior.

Of course, hiring a junior won’t automatically fix poor documentation or chaotic processes. It can, however, make those weaknesses harder to ignore.

## Hiring juniors is an investment, not charity

Some discussions frame junior hiring as a moral favor: companies should give people a chance because it’s the nice thing to do.

Giving people a chance matters. But that framing undersells the value juniors bring.

Junior developers ask questions that expose confusing code and undocumented decisions. They bring perspectives shaped by different backgrounds and recent learning. They can contribute to meaningful work while building the context needed for larger responsibilities.

They also give senior developers opportunities to practice mentorship, delegation, and communication. Those are not secondary skills. A senior who can make the whole team more effective often has more impact than one who can solve difficult problems alone.

None of this means a junior should be hired as cheap labor or expected to be grateful for any conditions they’re offered. It means a well-supported junior can become a valuable teammate—and the process of supporting them can strengthen the team around them.

The return isn’t guaranteed, and it isn’t immediate. That’s what makes it an investment.

## The entry-level job that requires experience

Many aspiring developers run into a familiar contradiction:

> Entry-level position. Two to three years of professional experience required.

Sometimes that requirement is copied from an old template. Sometimes a hiring manager wants a mid-level developer at an entry-level salary. Sometimes the company simply doesn’t know which skills are essential on day one and which can be learned.

Whatever the reason, the effect is the same: people who could grow into the role are encouraged to rule themselves out.

A better job description separates genuine requirements from preferences. If someone needs to understand basic programming concepts and be able to work through an unfamiliar problem, say that. If knowledge of a specific framework would be useful but can be learned on the job, say that too.

Then design the interview around the role you’re actually filling. A junior candidate doesn’t need to have designed a globally distributed system. They should have a chance to show how they think, how they respond to feedback, and how they approach problems they haven’t seen before.

That doesn’t require lowering standards. It requires choosing standards that match the opportunity.

## What supporting a junior actually looks like

Hiring someone and telling them to “ask questions whenever” is a start, but it isn’t a development plan. New developers may not know whom to ask, what information to provide, or whether their question is worth interrupting someone.

Support needs to be part of how the team works.

**Give them a clear onboarding path.** Help them get the application running, understand the product, meet the people they’ll work with, and make a small change. Keep the instructions somewhere the next person can improve them.

**Assign meaningful, scoped work.** A junior doesn’t need to spend six months changing button colors. Give them tasks with a clear outcome and manageable risk, then gradually increase the ambiguity and ownership.

**Make feedback timely and specific.** “This code is bad” teaches nothing. “This function is doing three things; separating the validation from the database call will make each part easier to test” gives someone a useful idea to apply again.

**Pair without taking over.** Let the junior drive sometimes. Ask what they think is happening before reaching for the keyboard. A solution they understand is worth more than one they watched someone else produce in thirty seconds.

**Protect time for mentorship.** If mentoring matters, it needs to be reflected in workload and expectations. A senior developer cannot be accountable for training someone while also being expected to maintain the same delivery pace indefinitely.

**Create a path to independence.** Support should evolve. The goal is not to keep a junior dependent on a mentor, but to help them build confidence, judgment, and ownership.

None of these practices is useful only for juniors. Clear onboarding, good feedback, and manageable work help experienced hires, too.

## Yes, juniors might leave

A common concern is that a company will invest in training a developer only for them to take those skills somewhere else.

That can happen. People change jobs for many reasons: compensation, growth, management, location, family, or simply the desire for a different challenge.

The answer is to build a place where people have reasons to stay. Pay fairly as their responsibilities grow. Give them opportunities to work on interesting problems. Recognize the people who mentor them. Don’t expect loyalty as repayment for ordinary professional development.

Some will still leave. That doesn’t make their growth a failure. Former employees carry what they learned into the wider community, just as your senior hires brought experience they gained elsewhere.

Every company benefits from work done by previous employers, teachers, open source maintainers, and colleagues. Participating in that exchange is part of sustaining the profession.

## Not every company needs a junior right now

The title of this post is deliberately sharp. It doesn’t mean every team must hire a junior immediately.

A two-person startup trying to find product-market fit may need someone who can work independently from day one. A team recovering from a major incident may need to stabilize before it can offer proper support. Hiring a junior into an environment where nobody has the time or inclination to help them is unfair to everyone involved.

But those constraints should lead to an honest question: **What would need to change for us to support a junior?**

Perhaps the team needs a better onboarding guide. Perhaps it needs more capacity, a less frantic roadmap, or a manager who treats mentoring as real work. Perhaps it can start with an internship, an apprenticeship, or one carefully supported hire.

And if hiring isn’t possible, there are other ways to contribute: mentor through a community program, review open source contributions, speak with students about the work, or help someone prepare for interviews. Those efforts don’t replace paid opportunities, but they can make the path into the industry less opaque.

## Build the team you want to hire from

It’s easy to say there aren’t enough senior developers. It’s harder to create an environment where developers can become senior.

That work takes patience. It means accepting that someone will need explanations, make mistakes, and occasionally take longer than an experienced colleague would. It also means giving them increasingly meaningful responsibilities and trusting them to grow.

If your company wants experienced people, ask how it helps create them. If the answer is “we wait for someone else to do it,” that strategy may work for a while. It leaves the future of your team—and the profession—to somebody else.

Hire juniors when you can support them. Give them real work, good feedback, and room to grow.

That’s how the next generation of seniors gets here.
