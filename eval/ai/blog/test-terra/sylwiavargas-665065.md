# Talk Notes: "Self-Care on Rails" (RailsConf 2021)

*Tags: #rails #ruby #mentalhealth #techtalks*

What can a Rails developer do when the people they love are having a hard time?

In [“Self-Care on Rails,”](https://www.rubyevents.org/topics/mental-health?back_to=%2Ftalks%2Fbuilding-workplaces-for-caregivers-supporting-parents-in-tech%3Fback_to_title%3DWork-Life%2BBalance&back_to_title=Building+Workplaces+for+Caregivers%3A+Supporting+Parents+in+Tech) a RailsConf 2021 talk, Ben Greenberg explores that question through projects he built for his family during the pandemic. His answer is warm and practical: pay attention to the needs around you, and consider whether the skills you already have can make a difficult moment a little easier.

The talk also asks a broader question about our responsibility as developers. Software affects real people, whether we’re building a widely used product or a small app for someone we know. That impact gives us reason to think carefully about what we make—and for whom.

## Building connection when people are apart

The pandemic disrupted routines, celebrations, and relationships for adults and children alike. A birthday party, for example, suddenly became a problem with no easy substitute: how do you celebrate together when friends can’t gather in person?

Greenberg responded by building a video-sharing app for his son’s birthday. It’s an example of using familiar tools to address an immediate, personal need. The goal wasn’t to create the next big platform. It was to help children spend time together and make a birthday feel special.

The project also touches on **WebRTC**, a web technology that enables real-time communication, including video, audio, and data exchange. For Rails developers, it’s a reminder that a server-side framework can be part of an experience that feels immediate and human.

I like this example because the value is easy to understand without a product roadmap or a growth chart. The people using the app mattered to its creator, and the problem mattered to them.

## Automating a little friction

Another project Greenberg discussed was a Ruby gem that used web scraping to automate school form submission. If you’ve ever had to repeat the same administrative task, you can appreciate the appeal.

Automation is often presented as a way to make businesses more efficient. Here, it also has a personal dimension: reducing a recurring task can give a family back time and attention.

That doesn’t mean every inconvenience needs an app. A useful question is: *What keeps creating friction for someone I care about?* Sometimes the answer will be a small script. Sometimes it will be a conversation, a shared responsibility, or a break from the screen.

## Self-interest and community care

One theme that stood out in the talk is that caring for yourself and caring for others can support each other. Your own needs matter, and recognizing them doesn’t make you selfish. At the same time, the people around you have needs that deserve attention too.

Greenberg connects this idea to the responsibility that comes with creating software. Conversations about ethical licensing, for instance, ask developers to consider how their work might be used and whom it might affect. Those questions apply at different scales: from an open source project used across the world to a tool made for one family. [These notes on the talk](https://dev.to/sylwiavargas/tech-talk-notes-self-care-on-rails-railsconf-2021-49ma) offer more context on the examples and ideas he discusses.

For me, the takeaway is an invitation to notice the relationship between technical work and everyday life. A useful project doesn’t need to be impressive on GitHub. It can help a child celebrate, spare a parent some repetitive work, or make it easier for friends to stay connected.

And sometimes the caring choice is to close the laptop. The point is to start with people, then decide whether code can help.
