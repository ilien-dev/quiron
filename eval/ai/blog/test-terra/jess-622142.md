# The 7 Most Popular DEV Posts from the Past Week

From a browser game inspired by childhood memories to an experiment in catching AI agents colluding, this week’s reading list has plenty to explore. There’s practical advice for open source contributors, a surprising bit of geometry, and a reminder that the most interesting questions about technology often involve people.

These seven posts appeared in [DEV’s November 10 featured roundup](https://dev.to/devteam/top-7-featured-dev-posts-of-the-week-432g). The list is selected by DEV’s editors rather than presented as a traffic-based ranking.

## 1. What should we learn to do without AI?

**[My reflections on raising a daughter in the age of AI](https://dev.to/p0rt/i-use-ai-what-should-i-teach-my-daughter-3efp)** by Sergei Parfenov

If an AI assistant can explain a concept or finish an assignment, what does learning it yourself look like? Sergei approaches that question as both a developer who uses AI and a parent thinking about his daughter’s future.

His suggestions are useful beyond parenting: check claims against their sources, be willing to say “I don’t know,” and try solving a problem again after putting the assistant away. It’s a thoughtful read for anyone wondering how to use helpful tools while continuing to develop their own skills.

## 2. A childhood memory becomes a 3D game

**[I built a 3D game about my Nigerian childhood (and I'm not a game developer)](https://dev.to/deborahemeni1/i-built-a-3d-game-about-my-nigerian-childhood-and-im-not-a-game-developer-281g)** by Deborah Emeni

A familiar childhood errand—and the responsibility of counting the change—became the inspiration for Deborah’s free 3D browser game. The result is a personal story told through something people can play.

Deborah also shares lessons from building with an AI coding agent, testing in in-app browsers, and paying attention when players quietly drop off. It’s a lovely example of how a specific memory can become an inviting creative project.

## 3. See the Fibonacci spiral unfold

**[The Fibonacci Spiral Is Secretly a Straight Line](https://dev.to/highflyer910/the-fibonacci-spiral-is-secretly-a-straight-line-4b4p)** by Thea

What happens when you hinge the squares of a Fibonacci spiral together and unfold them? Thea explores that question with an animation built in three.js, taking readers through the geometry and code behind it.

The post also examines some popular Fibonacci myths. Come for the visual trick; stay for a reminder that playing with an idea can make the math behind it much easier to appreciate.

## 4. When AI testing exposes a team problem

**[An AI Testing Presentation Became the Final Push for Organizational Change at My Company](https://dev.to/xulingfeng/an-ai-testing-presentation-became-the-final-push-for-organizational-change-at-my-company-12ag)** by xulingfeng

A demonstration of a multi-agent testing system led to a bigger conversation than expected. Among the surprises: some AI-generated bug reports that initially appeared invalid exposed gaps between documented requirements and what the team had agreed to build.

The post offers a window into how testing can surface communication problems as well as software defects. If your team has ever debated whether a report is “really” a bug, this one may feel familiar.

## 5. Test the limits of your agent limits

**[The Limits That Don't Limit](https://dev.to/aws-builders/the-limits-that-dont-limit-2o4j)** by Pin Xiong

A timeout sounds straightforward—until an experiment shows a five-second deadline taking 21.9 seconds. Pin investigates four limits intended to constrain AI agents and shows why their names alone don’t tell you everything about their behavior.

The practical takeaway: test safeguards under the conditions that matter to your application. A limit is only useful if you understand what it actually controls and when it takes effect.

## 6. Make your open source contribution count

**[A Maintainer's Advice on Contributing to Open Source in 2026](https://dev.to/wasp/a-maintainers-advice-on-contributing-to-open-source-in-2026-4ea1)** by Martin Šošić

Submitting a pull request is one part of contributing. Helping maintainers understand your intent and building trust are just as valuable.

Drawing on experiences with the Wasp project, Martin discusses how AI-generated contributions have changed the maintainer’s inbox and what helps a newcomer stand out. If you’ve been looking for a way to get involved in open source, this is a useful perspective from the other side of the review.

## 7. Give a simple baseline a chance

**[A Two-Line Function Caught AI Collusion Better Than the AI Did](https://dev.to/ayush_singh_9b0d83152be5b/a-two-line-function-caught-ai-collusion-better-than-the-ai-did-3nmd)** by Ayush Singh

Can an AI guard spot two agents secretly coordinating in a game? In this experiment, a simple check on their actions performed better at the task.

Ayush explains the experiment and its limits, making the post a useful prompt for anyone evaluating AI systems: before reaching for a sophisticated monitor, find out how well a straightforward baseline works.
