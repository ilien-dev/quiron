# How I Am Getting Out From Under My Student Debt

For a long time, my student debt felt like a problem I could manage by not looking at it too closely. I made payments when they were due, checked the balance occasionally, and told myself I would get serious about it when I earned more money.

The trouble was that “when I earn more” kept moving further away. There was always another expense, another month that felt unusually expensive, or another reason to put off making a plan.

I’m changing that. I don’t have a dramatic story about paying off six figures in a year, and I’m not going to pretend that a budgeting app can solve every financial problem. What I do have is a more honest view of what I owe and a plan I can follow without putting the rest of my life on hold.

Here’s how I’m approaching my student debt, one step at a time.

## I started by finding the real number

The first step was uncomfortable: I gathered the details of every loan in one place.

I wrote down each balance, interest rate, minimum payment, loan servicer, and due date. I also noted whether the loan was federal or private, because that affects which repayment options might be available.

Until I did this, I was thinking about my debt as one large, intimidating number. Breaking it into individual loans made it easier to understand. I could see which loans were costing me the most in interest and which balances were closest to being paid off.

I used a simple spreadsheet with columns like these:

```text
Loan | Balance | Interest rate | Minimum payment | Due date
```

Nothing fancy. The important part was having a reliable starting point. I update the numbers periodically so I can see progress, even when it feels slow.

## I made a budget I can actually live with

My first instinct was to cut everything optional and throw every spare dollar at my loans. That sounded motivating for about five minutes. Then I thought about how often an overly strict plan had fallen apart for me before.

Instead, I looked at my take-home pay and the expenses I need to cover: housing, food, utilities, transportation, insurance, and minimum debt payments. Then I looked for spending I could reduce without making the plan miserable.

For me, the goal is to create a consistent amount for extra payments, not to win a contest for the smallest grocery bill. Some months that amount may be modest. Some months it may be zero. A plan I can return to after a difficult month is more useful than a perfect plan I abandon after two weeks.

I also leave room for things I enjoy. Paying off debt matters to me, but so does having a life while I do it.

## I’m keeping a small emergency fund

It might seem strange to save money while paying interest on debt. But without any cash set aside, an unexpected expense can send me straight back to a credit card or force me to miss a loan payment.

I started by choosing a small emergency savings target that felt achievable. The exact amount depends on personal circumstances; the point is to build a buffer for expenses that would otherwise disrupt the plan.

Once that buffer is in place, I can make extra loan payments with more confidence. If I need to use it, replenishing it becomes part of the plan again.

This is one of the biggest changes in my thinking: progress is not just reducing the loan balance. It’s also making my finances stable enough that I can keep reducing it.

## I chose a repayment strategy

After covering the minimum payment on every loan, I direct any extra money toward one loan at a time.

There are two common ways to choose that loan:

- **Debt avalanche:** Put extra money toward the loan with the highest interest rate. This generally saves the most interest if I stick with it.
- **Debt snowball:** Put extra money toward the loan with the smallest balance. Paying off a loan sooner can provide a useful motivational boost.

I’m using the avalanche approach because reducing interest matters to me. But I understand the appeal of the snowball. If seeing a balance disappear helps someone stay consistent, that can be a meaningful advantage.

Before making extra payments, I check how my loan servicer applies them. I want the extra amount directed to the intended loan and applied in a way that reduces my balance, rather than assuming the payment will be handled the way I expect. I keep a record and check the account afterward.

## I’m reviewing the repayment options available to me

Making extra payments isn’t the only decision. The repayment plan itself matters, especially if the required monthly payment is putting pressure on the rest of the budget.

I’m reviewing my loan documents and checking the current options with my servicer. Depending on the type of loan and individual circumstances, there may be different repayment plans, hardship options, or other programs worth considering.

The details matter. A lower monthly payment can make a budget more manageable, but it may also mean paying for longer or paying more interest over time. Eligibility and program rules can change, so I’m checking current official information before making a decision.

I’m also cautious about refinancing. A different interest rate might look appealing, but refinancing can change the terms of a loan and, in some cases, mean giving up benefits attached to federal loans. I want to understand the tradeoffs before signing anything.

## I automated the basics

Willpower is useful, but I don’t want my entire plan to depend on remembering a payment date.

I set up a system for making at least the required payments on time, with reminders to check that the payments go through and that enough money is in the account. If automatic payments are available, I review their terms and whether they make sense for my cash flow.

For extra payments, I use a separate step. That gives me a chance to look at the month’s expenses before sending additional money. Some people may prefer to automate the extra payment too; I like having a little flexibility while I build the habit.

Automation helps with consistency, but I still check my statements. A few minutes of review can catch a failed payment, an unexpected change, or an extra payment that didn’t go where I intended.

## I’m making extra money part of the plan

There’s a limit to how much I can cut from my budget. Increasing income can make a bigger difference, although that’s often easier said than done.

I’m looking for realistic opportunities: asking about growth at work, improving skills that support my career, and considering occasional side work when I have the time and energy. I’m trying to avoid treating every free hour as an opportunity to earn money. Burnout can derail a financial plan just as quickly as an unexpected bill.

When extra money does come in, I decide what it needs to do. Some might replenish savings, cover an upcoming expense, or pay down a loan. I don’t have to send every windfall to debt for it to make a difference.

The key is making that decision deliberately instead of letting the money disappear without noticing.

## I track progress without obsessing over it

Student debt can take years to repay. If I check the balance every day, the changes are too small to feel satisfying, and the interest can make the whole effort feel discouraging.

A monthly check-in works better for me. I record the balances, confirm that payments were applied, and compare the total with earlier months. I also note milestones: an emergency fund target reached, a loan paid off, or several months of consistent payments.

I try not to compare my timeline with someone else’s. People start with different balances, incomes, family responsibilities, and opportunities. Someone else’s payoff story can offer ideas, but it doesn’t define what my progress should look like.

## The plan is allowed to change

I used to think a good debt plan was one I could follow perfectly. Now I think a good plan is one I can adjust.

If my income changes, I’ll revisit the payment amount. If an emergency comes up, I’ll deal with it and rebuild. If I learn that a different repayment option fits my circumstances better, I’ll consider it carefully.

Getting out from under student debt is a financial goal, but it’s also about reclaiming some attention. I want to spend less time feeling vaguely anxious about what I owe and more time making informed decisions about it.

I’m not at the finish line yet. But I know my numbers, I have a direction, and I’m taking steps I can repeat. For now, that feels like a solid place to start.
