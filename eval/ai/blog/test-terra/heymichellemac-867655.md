# 10 Ways I Use Figma As A Creator

When I first opened Figma, I thought of it as a tool for designing websites and apps. I expected frames, buttons, and carefully organized components. What I didn’t expect was how often I’d reach for it when I wasn’t designing an interface at all.

These days, Figma is one of my favorite places to turn rough ideas into things I can share. I use it to plan content, make graphics, organize inspiration, and give my projects a consistent look. Some files become polished assets; others are glorified digital scratch pads.

Here are ten ways I use Figma as a creator, along with a few practical ideas you can try in your own workflow.

## 1. Brainstorming ideas before I start creating

A blank document can make me feel like I need to write complete sentences immediately. A blank Figma canvas feels more forgiving.

When I’m exploring an idea, I’ll add a few text boxes, screenshots, color swatches, and rough shapes. I move things around until I can see connections between them. Sometimes an idea that sounded vague in my head starts to make sense once it sits next to a reference image or a possible headline.

For example, if I’m planning a series about building a personal website, I might create a cluster for each topic: choosing a domain, writing an about page, selecting projects, and deploying the site. Under each cluster, I’ll add questions a beginner might have.

I don’t worry about making this canvas pretty. The goal is to think visually and leave enough room for ideas to change.

**Try it:** Start with one sentence in the middle of a frame. Add five related ideas around it, then group the ones that could become a post, video, or project.

## 2. Creating graphics for social media

A good social graphic doesn’t have to be complicated. Often, I just need a clear headline, a strong background, and enough visual consistency that people recognize the post as mine.

I keep a small collection of reusable layouts for announcements, quotes, tutorials, and project updates. When I want to share something new, I duplicate a layout and replace the content. That saves me from making the same decisions about spacing and typography every time.

I also create separate frames for the places where I plan to share the graphic. A square image might work well in one feed, while a taller layout gives a carousel more room to breathe. Instead of stretching one design into every format, I adjust the composition for each frame.

The most useful habit I’ve developed is previewing graphics at a small size. If I can’t read the headline without zooming in, I simplify it.

## 3. Making cover images for blog posts

A cover image gives a post a visual identity before anyone reads the first paragraph. I use Figma to create covers that feel connected without making every article look identical.

My usual starting point is a simple template: a background, a title, and one visual element related to the topic. For a coding article, that might be a short code snippet or a diagram. For a more personal post, it could be a photo, illustration, or abstract shape.

I try to keep the title short enough to read at a glance. If the article has a long headline, I’ll use a shorter phrase on the image and leave the full title for the post itself.

Once I’m happy with the design, I export the frame as an image and check how it looks in the publishing platform’s preview. Cropping can surprise you, so I keep important text away from the edges.

## 4. Building a visual identity

A visual identity doesn’t need to start with a 40-page brand guide. Mine started with a few decisions I wanted to stop making over and over again.

I created a Figma page with my main colors, type choices, logo variations, and examples of how I use them. When I make a new graphic or update my portfolio, I can refer back to that page and keep things consistent.

I also include examples of what *not* to do, such as placing light text on a low-contrast background or using too many typefaces in one graphic. Those reminders are useful when I’m moving quickly.

Over time, the page has changed as my work has changed. That’s the benefit of keeping it practical: it’s a reference I actually use, not a document I feel obligated to follow forever.

**Try it:** Pick one heading font, one body font, and three or four colors. Create a sample post with them. You can always refine the choices later.

## 5. Turning explanations into diagrams

Some ideas are easier to understand when people can see the relationships between the parts. When a paragraph starts filling up with phrases like “then,” “after that,” and “connected to,” I consider making a diagram.

Figma works well for simple flows, comparison charts, timelines, and annotated screenshots. I’ve used boxes and arrows to explain how a request moves through an application, how a content workflow fits together, and what happens between an initial idea and a published project.

I start with the fewest shapes needed to explain the concept. Then I add labels in plain language and check whether the direction of the flow is obvious. Color can help distinguish categories, but I don’t rely on color alone; labels and shapes should carry the meaning too.

The result doesn’t need to be elaborate. A diagram that makes one confusing step clear can be more useful than a beautiful one that tries to explain everything.

## 6. Planning carousels and slide decks

A carousel or slide deck is a sequence, so I like seeing all the frames next to one another. Figma makes it easy to spot where the pacing feels uneven or where I’ve repeated the same point.

I usually begin by writing one idea per frame:

1. A title that tells people what they’ll learn.
2. A short explanation of the problem.
3. A few practical steps or examples.
4. A final takeaway or question.

Only after the sequence makes sense do I refine the design. This keeps me from spending too much time polishing a slide that doesn’t need to exist.

I also try to give each frame enough breathing room. If I’m shrinking the text to fit, that’s usually a sign I should split the idea across two slides or cut a sentence. The audience shouldn’t have to work hard to find the point.

## 7. Designing reusable components

Repetition is part of being a creator: the same profile image, callout box, footer, or title treatment can appear across dozens of assets. Figma components help me manage those repeated elements.

For example, I might turn a small footer containing my name and website into a component. I can then use instances of it in multiple graphics. If I update the main component, those instances can reflect the change without me editing every frame individually.

Components are also useful for things like video thumbnail labels, numbered steps, and common callouts. I keep them simple enough that I can still adapt a design when a particular piece of content needs something different.

The trick is to wait until a pattern actually repeats. I don’t need a component for every rectangle on the canvas. A little structure saves time; too much structure can become another project to maintain.

## 8. Mocking up portfolio and website changes

Before I change my portfolio, I like to sketch the idea in Figma. It gives me a place to experiment with the order of sections, the size of a project image, or the wording of a call to action before I start editing code.

I might make a rough version of a new homepage and compare it with the current one. Can a visitor tell what I do? Are my best projects easy to find? Does the page give someone a clear next step?

The mockup doesn’t have to cover every screen or interaction. Sometimes one frame is enough to answer the question I’m exploring. If the layout raises more questions, I can add a mobile version or map out another state.

For me, the value is in making decisions visible. It’s much easier to compare two page structures when I can see them side by side.

## 9. Collecting and organizing inspiration

I save examples of things that catch my attention: a clear diagram, an interesting color palette, a thoughtful portfolio layout, or a headline treatment that makes a complex topic feel approachable.

In Figma, I can put those references next to notes about *why* they work. That distinction matters. “I like this” is pleasant to revisit, but “the short labels make this diagram easy to scan” gives me something I can apply to my own work.

I organize references by purpose rather than collecting everything on one endless canvas. A page for article covers, another for portfolio layouts, and another for presentation ideas makes it easier to find something when I need it.

Inspiration is a starting point, not a shortcut to copying. I look for principles I can adapt, then make choices that fit my own content and audience.

## 10. Sharing work and gathering feedback

It’s easy to become attached to a design when I’ve been staring at it for hours. Sharing a Figma file gives me a chance to hear how someone else sees it.

I try to ask specific questions. Instead of “What do you think?” I might ask, “Can you tell what this graphic is explaining?” or “Which project would you click first?” Focused questions tend to produce feedback I can act on.

I also give reviewers enough context: who the work is for, where it will appear, and what I want it to accomplish. A thumbnail intended to be seen on a phone has different constraints from a slide shown on a large screen.

Afterward, I look for patterns rather than treating every suggestion as an instruction. If several people misunderstand the same label, that’s a useful signal. If one person prefers a different shade of blue, I’ll weigh that against the goal of the design.

## Make Figma fit your process

The reason I keep coming back to Figma is its flexibility. It can be a sketchbook in the morning, a diagramming tool in the afternoon, and an export station when I’m ready to publish.

You don’t need a perfect file structure or a polished brand to get started. Pick one recurring part of your creative process and make it a little easier: save a cover template, diagram an explanation, or collect references for your next project.

The best setup is the one that helps you spend less time rebuilding the same things and more time making work you’re excited to share.
