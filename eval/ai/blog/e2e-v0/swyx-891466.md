# Community Heat, or Why You Should Get Good at Events

There's an idea I keep coming back to when I think about careers, building communities and how ideas spread in tech. I call it heat. I mean the energy and momentum that builds up around a person, a project or an idea when people start paying attention to it. And one of the most reliable ways I know to make heat is events: talks, meetups, conferences, livestreams, panels, whatever form they take.

In this post I want to make the case that getting good at events is one of the best things you can do for your career and your community. That covers speaking at them, organizing them, or just showing up and taking part. It's true even if public speaking terrifies you right now.

## What is community heat?

Community heat is the buzz that builds around something when enough people pay attention to it at the same time. Think of two blog posts. One gets a handful of quiet reads. The other gets shared, discussed and argued about on several platforms at once. Or think of two projects. One slowly collects a few GitHub stars over months. The other is suddenly trending, and a few days later people are tweeting about it, writing follow-up posts and building on top of it.

Heat isn't just a matter of quality, though quality helps. It comes from being *visible at the right moment*, with the right people passing your work on to the right audience. Events are one of the most reliable ways I know to get that kind of focused attention.

## Why events make heat better than almost anything else

### They create a shared moment

You can read a blog post whenever you want, at your own pace, on your own. A conference talk happens at one moment, in front of a room (physical or virtual) full of people who are all watching it together. That shared experience gives the room an energy you just can't get from something people read on their own time.

When a talk lands, you can feel the room change as it happens, and everyone in that room feels it at the same time. People talk about it afterward, tweet about it during, and bring it up in conversations for months. It gives a whole group of people one point in time to rally around.

### They compress time

You can build an audience or a reputation slowly, by blogging steadily or contributing to open source. That works. It also takes a long time. Events cut that time down a lot. One good conference talk can do more for how visible you are in twenty minutes than months of quietly shipping code or writing posts that never quite catch on.

The slow, steady work still matters. It's often what gives you something worth talking about at the event in the first place. But the event is often what takes the value you've been quietly building up and shows it to a much bigger audience all at once.

### They spread further than the room

When you speak at an event, you reach more than the people in the room. You reach everyone they talk to afterward, everyone who watches the recording later, everyone who reads the tweetstorm summary, and everyone who finds a blog post about your talk months later. Events send out ripples well past the first audience, especially when the talk is good enough that people want to pass it on.

That effect grows when you speak at events again and again. Each talk makes you more present in a particular community. You get more likely to be invited to the next event, mentioned in the next roundup post, or thought of when someone needs an expert on a topic.

## Getting good at events doesn't mean you have to speak

When people hear "get good at events," they often assume it means becoming a polished public speaker. That's one path. It's far from the only one.

Organizing events (meetups, conferences, panels) puts you at the center of a community's energy without putting you on stage. Organizers build relationships with speakers, attendees and sponsors. They often have a lot of influence over where a community goes, because they're the ones deciding what gets amplified.

Showing up again and again as an engaged attendee matters more than people think, too. Ask good questions after talks. Introduce people to each other. Be someone people see and talk to at a community's events. Over time that builds real social capital, even if you never step on stage.

Then there's documenting events: live-tweeting talks, writing recap posts, putting together highlight threads. I think it's an underrated way to build heat around the event and around yourself. You become the person who captures the community's best ideas and passes them around, which is a useful role in its own right.

## Getting over the fear

I get it. Public speaking is one of the most common fears there is, and standing in front of a room (or a camera, for virtual events) can feel paralyzing. A few things have helped people I know get past it.

- Start small. A five-minute lightning talk at a local meetup is a much lower-stakes way to build the skill than jumping straight to a keynote.
- Talk about what you've actually built or learned. Real experience lands far better than polish, and people connect with it more than with a show of expertise.
- Practice out loud, over and over. Reading your slides silently in your head is not the same as saying the words out loud several times before the real thing.
- Treat nervousness as excitement. Your body does almost the same thing in both cases, and the story you tell yourself about it changes how it feels.

## It compounds

The people you keep seeing with heat around their work, their projects or their communities often aren't more talented than everyone else. They're just visible more often, and events are one of the best tools I know for being visible over time.

Every talk you give, every meetup you organize and every event you really show up to adds another data point to how the community sees you. Over years that adds up to reputation, trust and opportunity, in a way that's hard to match with work people only read on their own time.

## Final thoughts

If you're doing good work and it feels like nobody's noticing, you might not have a quality problem. You might have a visibility problem. Events are one of the best ways I know to fix that, because they create shared moments that people remember. They compress time, and they spread your work further than almost anything else can.

You don't need to become a world-class keynote speaker overnight. Start small, keep showing up, and let yourself build this skill slowly. The heat around your work often matters as much as the work, and events are where that heat gets made.
