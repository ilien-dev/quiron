# How to find consulting clients

Finding your first consulting clients can feel like a catch-22. Clients want consultants with experience, but you can't get experience until a client hires you.

The good news is that there's no secret to getting clients. I think of it as a process you can repeat, and for me it comes down to four things:

1. Pick a specific problem
2. Show that you can solve it
3. Start the right conversations
4. Follow up, every time

You don't need a huge audience, a fancy website, or a perfectly polished personal brand. You need a clear offer and a reliable way to reach the people who might need that offer.

## Start with a problem, not a job title

"Software consultant" is too broad. It tells a client what you are, but it doesn't tell the client why they should hire you.

A better pitch ties a certain kind of customer to an outcome they care about:

> I help growing SaaS companies reduce AWS costs without slowing down their engineering teams.

A few more I like: helping startups get their infrastructure ready for enterprise security reviews, moving legacy React apps to modern frameworks, speeding up slow PostgreSQL queries for high-traffic products, building internal tools to replace work that lives in spreadsheets, or helping engineering teams set up CI/CD pipelines they can rely on.

Being specific makes marketing easier. You know what to write about, who to talk to, and which problems to talk about.

It makes referrals easier too. People rarely remember that someone is a "general technology consultant." They do remember the person who fixes expensive cloud infrastructure problems.

Your focus doesn't have to be permanent. Your focus only has to be specific enough that people know when to call you.

## Make a small, concrete offer

A lot of new consultants try to sell an open-ended engagement right away. That's risky for the client, because the scope, the price and the result aren't defined.

So start with a small offer that's easy to understand and easy to buy. For example:

> A two-week performance audit covering database queries, application bottlenecks, and infrastructure configuration, followed by a prioritized remediation plan.

A good starter offer has a clear target customer and a problem they'll know when they see it. The scope is defined and the timeline is short, and it ends with something useful in their hands and a business outcome they can see.

An assessment, an audit, a workshop, a prototype or a focused bit of build work can all be a way in to bigger work. The client gets to try you out without signing up for a six-month project, and you get to know the client well enough to spot more work you could do for the client.

Don't give the whole engagement away as a "free consultation." A short intro call can be free. In my view diagnosis and recommendations are real work, and they should be paid for.

## Begin with people who already trust you

My bet is that your first clients will come from people you already know, not from strangers on the internet.

Make a list of the people you know. Put down former coworkers and managers, founders you know, past customers and vendors, people you know from technical communities, friends who work at relevant companies, and agencies that offer services next to yours.

Then write to each of them one at a time. I wouldn't send one email to all of them. Don't tell them you're "available for anything." Tell them what you focus on and make the ask easy to act on. Something like:

> I've started helping SaaS teams diagnose and reduce cloud infrastructure costs. I'm looking for companies whose AWS bill has grown faster than their usage. If someone comes to mind, would you be comfortable introducing us?

This isn't pushy selling. You're giving people enough to see a possible match when they see one.

Don't push the people you know to invent work for you. Ask them for advice, context or an introduction when it fits. A conversation today might turn into a referral three months from now.

## Look for signs, not just companies

Cold outreach works better when you have a reason to reach out, some sign the company might need you.

Instead of building a random list of companies, look for signs that a company might have the problem you solve. Maybe the company is hiring several engineers, or the company just announced a funding round. The company might be moving to a new platform, might have had a public outage, or might be launching an enterprise product. Or the team is posting about rising infrastructure costs, opening roles in your area of focus, or talking about their technical problems in public.

A good cold message ties what you know to that context:

> I saw that your team is hiring several platform engineers as you expand the enterprise product. Teams at that stage often discover that deployment permissions and audit trails become blockers during customer reviews. I help SaaS companies address those gaps. Is that something your team is encountering?

You aren't trying to close a deal in one message. You're trying to find out if the problem is there at all.

Keep the message short. Don't attach a proposal, don't send a calendar link with no context, and don't list every service you offer.

## Publish proof that you know your stuff

Clients can't look inside your head, so they look for proof that you know what you're doing. The proof could be technical articles, case studies, open-source contributions, conference talks, architecture reviews, before-and-after performance numbers, testimonials, or detailed write-ups of past work.

Write about the problems you want to be hired to solve. If you want database optimization projects, a post about CSS architecture might show you're smart, but it won't back up your pitch.

Good content is concrete. Explain how you found the problem, what options you looked at, which tradeoffs mattered, and what changed after you shipped the fix.

If the client details are confidential, anonymize the case:

> A subscription company was processing billing jobs sequentially. We redesigned the workflow around idempotent parallel workers, reducing processing time from hours to minutes.

One strong case study is usually worth more than ten generic posts.

Content rarely brings in clients right away by itself. Where I think it pays off is when a client gets your message, searches your name, and finds proof that you understand the problem.

## Build relationships with other service providers

Other consultants and agencies can send you a lot of work. They might need skills they don't have, or extra hands when they're busy. They might need someone to maintain a system after launch, or someone they trust to hand leads that are too small or outside their focus.

Look for people whose services fit next to your services instead of competing with them. A designer might team up with a frontend consultant. A product agency might need a security specialist. A fractional CTO might need people they can count on to build things.

I'd go into these relationships looking to help. Find out what a good referral looks like for them, pass along work that fits, and be someone they can depend on when you work together.

A small group of partners you trust can give you steadier work than a big social media following.

## Treat discovery like a diagnosis

When a client agrees to talk, don't jump into a pitch. Ask questions:

- What made you want to deal with this now?
- What happens if the problem doesn't get fixed?
- Who does it affect?
- What have you tried already?
- What constraints does a solution have to respect?
- Who needs to approve the project?
- What would a good outcome look like?

You're trying to work out three things: whether the problem matters, whether you can solve it, and whether the client is ready to act.

I'd sum up what you heard before you suggest an approach. It shows you understand the situation, and it gives the client a chance to correct you.

If no real problem is found, say so. Telling a client they don't need the work builds more trust than pushing every conversation toward a sale.

## Follow up without being a pest

Consulting deals stall all the time. Priorities change, budgets have to be approved, people get busy. When a client goes quiet, it doesn't always mean no.

When you follow up, have a reason to follow up. Share an article that's relevant to them, clear up a recommendation, send a short outline of the project, tell them something new you noticed, or ask if the priority has changed.

Keep simple notes on each conversation and pick a date for the next step. A spreadsheet is really all I'd use.

I'd skip the messages that only say "Just checking in." Add some context or something useful, and make it easy for the client to give you an honest answer, even if that answer is "not now."

## Make business development a weekly habit

If you only look for clients when your current project ends, you put yourself under pressure you don't need. Looking for clients should be kept going while you're busy with clients.

A weekly routine you can actually keep might look like this:

- Reach out to five prospects who are a good fit
- Follow up on five conversations you already have going
- Reconnect with two people in your network
- Publish one useful technical insight
- Talk to one possible referral partner

Track the things you control. Signed contracts shouldn't be the only thing that gets counted. Money shows up late and unpredictably. Sending good messages and having conversations are things you can do today.

Over time, I'd watch which channels bring in real work. Put more effort into those channels, and cut back on the things that get attention but don't lead to conversations.

## Consistency is what wins

Most consultants who struggle don't lack skills. Their offer is vague, they reach out in bursts, or they stop following up too early.

So pick a problem worth solving and package a small first engagement. Reach out to people who have a plausible reason to care. Publish proof, build referral relationships, and do it all again every week.

Finding clients gets a lot less scary once finding clients stops being an emergency you deal with now and then and becomes part of the job. You don't need everyone to know who you are. You need a few of the right people to understand exactly how you can help.
