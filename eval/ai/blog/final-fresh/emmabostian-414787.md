# 5 Tips On Landing Your First Developer Job

This week Honeypot.io published a video of me giving my ["5 Tips For Junior Developers"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n18iJYxWy3w&t=41s). A commenter named Stefan pointed out that the title promised tips on breaking into tech, but the content was all about what to do once you'd been hired.

He was right, I skipped the part about getting the first job entirely, so this post is my attempt to fix that.

Before I start, I have to be upfront about something: my path was privileged. My internship at IBM turned into a full-time role inside IBM, so I never had to mass-apply for my first job. That changed later: when I applied for my second job (at LogMeIn) and then for Spotify, I applied everywhere, and roughly 9 out of 10 companies never replied at all. So I do know what that silence feels like, just not from the very first search.

---

# 1. Make your resume easy to scan

Recruiters spend about [7.4 seconds looking at a resume](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/). That's not a lot of time to make an impression, so every line has to earn its spot.

What I recommend:

- A fun design is fine, but put the key information and your contact details at the top
- Leave the photo off
- In your bullet points, write about the impact you had and use numbers wherever you can

And don't send the same generic resume to every posting. If a job emphasizes React, put your React projects front and center.

I actually made a [LinkedIn Learning course on writing a tech resume](https://www.linkedin.com/learning/writing-a-tech-resume/color?autoplay=true), where I show my own resume, if you want to go deeper.

# 2. Build two or three projects

Having a relevant computer science degree is a privilege, and it opens doors. If you don't have one, you need something else to point to, and that something is projects. Aim for two or three projects on GitHub. If you did a bootcamp you'll usually come out with one or two, so you may only need to build one more.

Don't stop at tutorials, though. Tutorials teach you syntax, but they don't show that you can solve a problem on your own, and that's what the person interviewing you wants to see. Build something that made you make decisions and debug things yourself, so you have real stories to tell in the interview.

# 3. Get some certificates

If you're learning on your own, pick courses that give you a certificate at the end. [FreeCodeCamp](http://freecodecamp.org/), LinkedIn Learning and [Coursera](https://www.coursera.org/) all offer them. A certificate gives you something concrete to put on your resume when you don't have a job to list yet.

# 4. Learn how to solve problems (out loud)

This is the one that cost me the most. I never really learned how to problem solve, and it was a huge handicap in technical interviews. I'm not an expert here (I'm actually working on a full course on technical interviews right now), but this is the process I'd follow:

1. Ask clarifying questions
2. Nail down the requirements
3. Talk through a few options and their tradeoffs
4. Think out loud the whole time instead of typing in silence
5. Admit what you don't know
6. Optimize your solution
7. Test it
8. Talk about what you'd improve with more time

Practice this with friends or mentors before the real thing. Interviewing is a skill of its own, separate from how well you code.

And if the job asks for a technology you've never touched, say so. "I haven't used X yet, but I'm eager to learn it" is a perfectly good answer, and it's far better than bluffing.

# 5. Keep your ego in check

This one is personal. After college, with an IBM internship under my belt, my ego was huge, and honestly it hurt my growth for several years. Stay curious and stay humble; you'll learn a lot faster.

---

Here's the last thing, and it matters more than any of the tips above: you will get discouraged, probably more than once, and when that happens you should take a break. Your mental health comes first, and the job search will still be there when you come back.
