# If You Don't Hire Juniors, You Don't Deserve Seniors

_There's a revised 2023 version of this post on my blog:_ https://isaaclyman.com/blog/posts/junior-developers/

-----

Some companies don't hire junior developers at all, and Netflix is the famous example. Asked [why there are no interns at Netflix](https://www.quora.com/Why-aren%E2%80%99t-there-interns-at-Netflix), the answer compared juniors to puppies: they make messes, and somebody has to clean them up.

I find this absurd. For one thing, puppies are great.

For another, the reasoning doesn't hold up once you look at it. The excuses I hear most often go something like this:

- "We move too fast."
- "We can afford seniors, so why wouldn't we?"
- "The stakes are too high for beginners."
- "Our engineers have too much autonomy for hand-holding."
- "We have to focus on the product first."

There are more than [100,000 tech companies in the US](https://www.selectusa.gov/software-and-information-technology-services-industry-united-states). Not one of them will tell you their mistakes don't matter, so every one of those excuses applies to every one of those companies, and hiring only seniors doesn't give you an edge. If anything, it's a signal that something is wrong with how the place is managed.

## About those messes

Let's go back to the puppy. "We can't have messes" really means "we can't have mistakes," and a culture where nobody is allowed to make a mistake is a culture run on fear.

Seniors make mistakes too. The real answer to mistakes isn't hiring people who supposedly don't make them; it's good process: tests, QA, failovers, access control and changes you can roll back. If a junior can break production, a senior can break production, and the only difference is that the junior will probably find the gap in your process sooner. That's a favor, not a liability.

## Let's talk money

According to Indeed, the average [junior software engineer](https://www.indeed.com/salaries/Junior-Software-Engineer-Salaries) makes $55,394 a year. The average [senior software engineer](https://www.indeed.com/salaries/Senior-Software-Engineer-Salaries) makes $117,374. That's more than twice as much.

Now think about what most of the work on a software team actually is. A lot of it is glue code: wiring one thing to another, moving data from here to there. Add the code that just describes your business domain, and you're looking at half the work or more. A junior can write that code. When a junior writes it, it costs you $28 an hour; when a senior writes it, it costs you $59 an hour, and the result is the same.

And "junior" doesn't stay junior for long on a given team. A junior who has been on your team for six months will often beat a brand-new senior, because the junior knows your code and the senior doesn't. Seniors need onboarding too. They just ask different questions.

So here's what I'd propose: one junior plus one senior gets you roughly the output of two seniors, for less than 75% of the cost. That pair should be the basic unit of a development team.

There's another cost that doesn't show up on a salary survey. Put enough seniors in a room and they'll spend a surprising number of payroll hours arguing about trivia: which algorithm, which micro-optimization, which code style. Juniors rarely do.

## Where do you think seniors come from?

Most developers want to grow into something: they want to become an architect, a team lead, a CTO, a founder. A company that won't hire juniors is also a company with no career path, and a company with no career path is a company where people stagnate.

It's rare, and impressive, to meet someone who's been a team lead at a company for eight years and started there as an intern. Eight years is about a tenth of a lifespan. That company got eight years of someone's growth and got to keep all of it. More companies should want that.

## What juniors bring

Juniors aren't just cheaper seniors. They bring things seniors often don't: optimism, a willingness to follow, and no baggage about how things were done at their last job.

When I was a junior, I was the one who got handed the prototypes and the trials of new tools. Some of it turned into order-of-magnitude improvements in page load times. Some of it was merging pages together, which saved weeks of maintenance. And some of it was proving that a technology we were considering wasn't good enough, which is just as useful to know.

Juniors also keep senior code honest. Seniors, left alone, tend to overengineer. When a junior has to read and work with that code, the overengineering gets noticed and trimmed. A team with a mix of experience levels ends up with a simpler codebase.

## The payoff

Hire juniors and you get lower turnover, more diversity and less overhead. It's not the only thing that matters in building a team, and I'm not pretending it is.

But if you won't hire juniors, you're counting on someone else to train the seniors you want to hire. If every company thought that way, there wouldn't be any seniors left to hire.
