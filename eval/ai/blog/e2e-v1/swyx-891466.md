# Community Heat, or Why You Should Get Good at Events

There's an idea I keep coming back to when I think about careers, community building and how ideas spread in tech. I call it heat. I don't mean literal heat. I mean the energy that builds up around a person or a project (sometimes just an idea) when people start paying attention to it. One of the most reliable ways to build that heat is events: talks, meetups, conferences, livestreams, panels, whatever form they take.

I want to make the case that getting good at events is one of the highest-leverage things you can do for your career and your community. That could mean speaking at them, organizing them, or just showing up and taking part. It holds even if public speaking terrifies you right now.

## What is "community heat"?

Community heat is the buzz around something when enough people pay attention to it at the same time. Think of a blog post that gets a handful of quiet reads, and then one that people share and quote and argue about on several platforms at once. Or a project that picks up a few GitHub stars over months, next to one that suddenly shows up on the trending page, with people tweeting about it and writing follow-up posts and building on top of it within days.

Quality helps, but I don't think heat is only about quality. It's about being visible at the right moment, to the right audience, with the right people passing it on. And events are one of the most reliable ways I know to find exactly that kind of concentrated attention.

## Why events generate heat better than almost anything else

### They create a shared moment

Anyone can read a blog post at any time, on their own, at their own pace. A conference talk happens at one moment, in front of a room (physical or virtual) full of people who are all watching it together. That gives it a kind of group energy that content people consume on their own schedule just can't match.

When a talk lands, you can feel the room change, and everyone in the room feels it together. People bring that moment up afterward, tweet about it while it's happening and mention it in conversations for months. It's one point in time that a whole group of people can point back to.

### They compress time

You can build an audience or a reputation slowly through steady blogging or open source work. That works. It just takes a long time. Events compress that timeline: one well-received conference talk can do more for your visibility in twenty minutes than months of quietly shipping code or writing posts that never quite catch fire.

The slow work still matters, and it's often what gives you anything worth presenting at the event in the first place. But the event is often what takes value you've been quietly building up and suddenly puts it in front of a far bigger audience.

### They pass your ideas along for you

When you speak at an event, you reach more than the people in the room. You also reach everyone they talk to afterward, the people who watch the recording later, the ones who read somebody's tweetstorm summary, and whoever finds a blog post about your talk months later. The ripples go well past the room, especially when people like the talk enough to pass it along.

This adds up when you speak at more events over time. Each talk makes you a bit more present in a community. You get invited to the next event, mentioned in the next roundup post, and remembered when someone needs an expert on your topic.

## Getting good at events doesn't only mean speaking

When people hear "get good at events," they often assume it means becoming a polished public speaker. That's one path, but it's far from the only one.

**Organizing** events (meetups, conferences, panels) puts you in the middle of a community's energy without putting you on stage. Organizers build relationships with speakers, attendees and sponsors. They often have outsized influence on where a community goes, because they're the ones deciding what gets attention.

**Showing up regularly** as an engaged attendee matters more than people think. Ask good questions after talks. Introduce people to each other. Over time, being a familiar, active face at a community's events builds real social capital, even if you never get on stage yourself.

**Documenting events** is another underrated way to build heat around the event and around you. That means live-tweeting talks, writing recap posts or putting together highlight threads. People start to see you as the person who captures the community's best ideas and passes them on, and that's a valuable role in its own right.

## Getting past the fear

I get it. Public speaking is one of the most common fears there is, and standing in front of a room (or a camera, for virtual events) can feel paralyzing. A few things have helped people I know get past it:

- **Start small.** A five-minute lightning talk at a local meetup is a much lower-stakes way to build the skill than going straight to a keynote.
- **Talk about what you've actually built or learned.** Being genuine matters far more than polish. People connect with real experience, not with someone performing expertise.
- **Practice out loud, more than once.** Reading your slides silently in your head is not the same as saying the words out loud several times before the real thing.
- **Call the nerves excitement.** Your body reacts almost the same way to both, and the story you tell yourself about it changes how it feels.

## It compounds

The people you see generating heat around their work, their projects or their communities aren't necessarily more talented than everyone else. Often they're just more visible, more often. Events are one of the most efficient ways to build that kind of visibility.

Every talk, every meetup you organize and every event you really show up to adds another data point to how the community sees you. Over the years that turns into reputation, trust and opportunity, and work people only ever read on their own time has a hard time matching that.

So if you're doing good work and it feels like nobody's noticing, it might not be a quality problem. It might be a visibility problem. You don't need to become a world-class keynote speaker overnight. Start small and keep showing up. Give yourself permission to get better at this slowly. The heat you build around your work often matters as much as the work, and events are where a lot of it gets made.
