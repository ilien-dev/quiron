# Community Heat, or Why You Should Get Good at Events

There's a quiet asymmetry in developer careers. Two engineers with similar skills, similar résumés, and similar GitHub graphs end up with wildly different options five years later. One gets pinged about jobs, invited to speak, and has a name that makes people say "oh, I've heard of them." The other is equally competent and nearly invisible.

The usual explanations are luck, privilege, or "personal brand," a phrase that makes most of us wince. I'd offer a better one: **community heat**.

## What I mean by community heat

Heat is what friction produces. Rub two things together and you get warmth. Communities work the same way. A Slack workspace with 20,000 members and no interaction is cold storage. A meetup of 25 people who argue about database migrations over pizza is warm.

Community heat is the accumulated warmth around you and your work: people who know your name, have talked to you in person, and associate you with something good. It's built from small interactions, and it has some properties worth knowing:

- **It decays.** If you stop showing up, the warmth dissipates.
- **It compounds.** Warm people introduce you to other warm people.
- **It's local before it's global.** Almost nobody becomes known to thousands without first being known by dozens.
- **It's hard to fake.** You can buy followers, but you can't buy the feeling of "oh, *you're* the person who helped me debug that thing."

And the best generator of heat I know of is events.

## Why events beat almost everything else

Online communities are great. I'm writing this on one. But text-based interaction has a low heat ceiling. Replies are asynchronous, tone is ambiguous, and most people lurk. You can be in a Discord for two years and never have a single memorable exchange.

Events collapse all of that:

1. **Bandwidth.** Voice, face, body language, and shared context. Ten minutes in a hallway can do what ten weeks of thread replies can't.
2. **Shared experience.** You all watched the same talk, or the same demo failed. That gives you a built-in conversation starter and a memory in common.
3. **Commitment signal.** Showing up costs time, and often money. That filters the crowd toward people who care.
4. **Serendipity density.** More unplanned, useful collisions happen per hour at a good event than in a month of online browsing.

If you're early in your career, events are the cheapest way to compress years of networking into months. If you're senior, they're how you stay connected to what people actually struggle with, rather than what Hacker News says they struggle with.

## "But I'm an introvert"

Most of us are, to some degree. Here's the reframe: **getting good at events is a skill, not a personality trait.** Nobody is born knowing how to work a conference. People who look natural have usually built a set of habits that make it easy.

Here are the ones that matter.

## Getting good as an attendee

**Have a goal that isn't "network."** "Network" is too vague to act on. Better goals: talk to three people who use the tool I'm evaluating, find one person who has solved a problem like mine, or meet the maintainer of a library I depend on. Specific goals give you a reason to start conversations.

**Arrive early.** The first 30 minutes are the easiest time to talk to people. Everyone's standing around, nobody's in a clique yet, and there's an obvious opener: "Have you been to this one before?"

**Learn a few open-ended questions.** My reliable ones:
- "What are you working on these days?"
- "What brought you to this talk?"
- "What's the most annoying part of your stack right now?"

That last one is a heat generator. People love to complain constructively, and it surfaces real problems fast.

**Ask the speaker one good question afterward.** Not in the Q&A, where it's performative, but in person. Something specific that shows you listened: "You mentioned that you moved off the queue for X. What did you do about ordering guarantees?" Speakers remember this.

**Follow up within 48 hours.** This is where most people fail. A short message works: "Great talking about flaky integration tests. Here's that article I mentioned." Include something useful, and keep it short. The follow-up turns a pleasant moment into a durable connection.

## Getting good as a speaker

You don't need to be an expert to give a talk. You need to have done something, learned something, and be able to explain it honestly.

Some of the best talks are structured like this: *here's a problem I hit, here's what I tried, here's what failed, here's what worked.* That's it. A talk titled "How We Migrated 40 Services Off a Deprecated SDK and What Broke" will outperform a polished survey of "The Future of Cloud" almost every time.

A few practical notes:

- **Start at small venues.** Meetups, lunch-and-learns, and lightning talks. Five-minute slots are a gift because the stakes are low.
- **Rehearse out loud.** Reading slides in your head doesn't count. Do it standing up, with a timer.
- **Cut half your content.** Everyone overpacks. One idea, explained well, beats five rushed.
- **Share the slides and a short write-up afterward.** This extends the life of the talk and creates something searchable. A dev.to post is perfect for this.
- **Stay for the rest of the event.** After a talk, people want to approach you. Don't vanish to your hotel room.

A talk is one of the most concentrated sources of heat there is. In 20 minutes, you tell a room of strangers who you are and what you care about, and they come to you with questions. That's inverted networking: instead of seeking out conversations, you become the person conversations find.

## Getting good as an organizer

This is the underrated one. If attending builds heat and speaking concentrates it, **organizing is the thing that generates it for other people**, and you're the one holding the match.

Organizers get disproportionate returns:

- You meet every speaker and sponsor.
- You become the default node in the local graph. People introduce *you* to others.
- You build skills most engineers never practice: logistics, communication, budgeting, negotiation, and people management.

And you don't need a 500-person conference. Start small:

1. **A monthly meetup of 10 to 20 people.** A borrowed room and a loose topic is enough.
2. **A recurring format.** "Show and tell," "bring a bug," or "lightning talks." Consistency matters more than novelty.
3. **A code of conduct from day one.** It's cheap, it signals safety, and it makes people more willing to show up.
4. **A co-organizer.** Burnout is the main killer of community events. Share the load before you need to.
5. **A way to follow up.** Post a recap, share photos if people are fine with it, and announce the next date before the current one ends.

The failure mode of community events is almost never "nobody came to the first one." It's "the organizer got tired and stopped." Build for sustainability, not spectacle.

## Making events work online too

Virtual events can generate heat too, but they require more deliberate design, because the default is passive viewing. If you run or attend one, look for formats that force interaction: small breakout rooms, live debugging sessions, collaborative workshops, or "ask me anything" calls with real back-and-forth. Cameras on, even if just for part of it, help a lot.

Hybrid is hardest. If you run a hybrid event, give the remote audience a dedicated host and a real channel, or they'll be second-class participants.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating events as transactions.** If you show up only to pitch or recruit, people feel it. Give first. Answer questions, make introductions, and share resources.
- **Collecting contacts instead of making connections.** Twenty LinkedIn requests mean little. Two real conversations mean a lot.
- **Going once.** One event is a lottery ticket. Showing up repeatedly to the same community is how familiarity and trust form.
- **Only attending big conferences.** They're fun but diffuse. Local and niche events typically offer better conversations per hour.
- **Ignoring the hallway.** Often the best content is outside the session rooms.

## A simple 90-day plan

If you want to start from zero:

- **Month 1:** Attend two events. At each, have three real conversations and follow up with each person.
- **Month 2:** Attend two more, including one in a community adjacent to your usual one. Volunteer for something small, such as greeting people or helping with setup.
- **Month 3:** Give a five-minute lightning talk, or co-host an event. Write a short post about what you learned.

At the end of 90 days, you'll know dozens of people by name, you'll have a talk and an article to your credit, and you'll have the beginnings of a reputation that no amount of solo grinding could produce.

## The long game

Skills get you hired. Heat gets you found. Over a long career, the people who thrive are rarely the ones with the single best technical chops. They're the ones who are skilled *and* connected, whom others trust, recommend, and want to work with.

Events are the most reliable machine for producing that connection. They're imperfect, sometimes awkward, and occasionally expensive, but nothing else delivers the same concentration of human warmth for a developer.

So pick one event this month. Show up early, ask one good question, and follow up afterward.

Then do it again. That's how the heat builds.
