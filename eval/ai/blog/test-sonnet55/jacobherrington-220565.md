Every developer I know has a folder of unsent things. A half-written blog post. A pull request sitting in draft. A job application with the cover letter finished and the submit button untouched. Mine used to overflow.

Fear of rejection rarely announces itself as fear. It shows up as "I'll polish it a bit more," or "I'm not ready yet," or "someone more qualified has probably already done this." I haven't gotten rid of it, but I've found ways to make it smaller and less in charge. Here is what works for me.

## 1. Figure out what the fear is protecting

When I catch myself stalling, I ask: *what exactly am I afraid will happen?* The answer is usually vague, something like "they'll think I'm not good enough." Vague fears are powerful because they can't be checked.

So I make it specific. If I submit this talk proposal and it's rejected, what changes? I get an email. Maybe a polite template. My skills are the same as yesterday, and my career isn't over. Written down, the fear usually turns out to be protecting my ego from a few minutes of discomfort. That's a bad trade for skipping an opportunity.

## 2. Shrink the ask

Big, high-stakes asks make rejection feel enormous. I try to break them into smaller asks that are cheaper to lose.

Instead of "get my open source contribution merged into a major project," the first step is "open an issue asking if the maintainers would want this." Instead of "land a senior role at my dream company," it's "ask one engineer there for 15 minutes to talk about their work."

Small asks have two advantages. A "no" stings less, and a "yes" gives me information and momentum. Often the small ask changes what the big ask should be.

## 3. Separate the work from the verdict

A rejection is a decision made by someone with limited time, incomplete information, and their own constraints. It is not a precise measurement of my worth or even my skill.

I've been on the other side of this. When I helped review applications, I rejected strong candidates because we needed someone with a different stack, or because the budget changed, or because I was reading my fortieth résumé of the day. None of that was a judgment of the person.

Now when I get a "no," I try to sort it into one of three buckets:

- **Fit:** it wasn't right for them, and that's fine.
- **Timing:** it might have been right six months ago or six months from now.
- **Quality:** there's something concrete I can improve.

Only the third bucket requires action, and it's the only one that gives me something useful.

## 4. Collect rejections on purpose

This sounds strange, but it works. I set a target for "no"s rather than "yes"es. If I want one conference talk accepted, I plan to submit to ten. If I want a few good freelance clients, I count how many people I've pitched, not how many replied.

Once rejection is the expected cost of the process, it stops feeling like a personal failure. It becomes a metric. A hundred small tests that fail teach me more than one test I was too scared to run. The same logic applies when debugging: a failing test isn't an insult, it's information.

## 5. Write down the worst case and the best case

Before I send something scary, I write two lines:

- **Worst realistic case:** They say no, or they don't reply.
- **Best realistic case:** They say yes, and something good starts.

I specify *realistic* because anxiety loves catastrophes like "everyone will see it and laugh." That almost never happens. People are busy and mostly thinking about their own work.

Seeing the two lines side by side usually makes the decision obvious. The worst case is mild and recoverable, and the best case is worth a lot.

## 6. Treat code review as practice

Code review is the most common form of rejection in a developer's life. Someone looks at your work and says "change this." For years, every comment felt like a verdict on me.

What helped was changing how I read feedback. A comment on my pull request is about the code, and it's usually offered to make the shared codebase better. Now I try to respond to the technical point first, and I leave the emotional reaction for later. If a comment stings, I wait an hour before replying. Almost every time, it looks more reasonable afterward.

Taking code review this way gave me a low-stakes place to practice hearing "not like this" without falling apart. That carries over to bigger situations.

## 7. Be kind to the part of you that's afraid

I used to think the answer was to be tougher: just push through, stop being a baby about it. That never lasted. What worked better was treating the fear as a reasonable instinct, since humans evolved to care about belonging, and then acting anyway.

When a rejection does hurt, I let it. I take a walk, tell a friend, and give myself an evening. Then I look at whether there's anything to learn, and I send the next thing. Letting myself feel disappointed, without making it mean something about me, is what keeps me going.

## What I've noticed

The people who seem fearless about rejection usually aren't. They've just had more of it, and they've learned it's survivable. Every "no" I've gotten has been less damaging than I predicted, and every unsent thing in that folder has been more costly than any rejection.

So here's the small step I'd suggest: open your folder of unsent things, pick the smallest one, and send it today. Expect that it might get a "no," and send it anyway.
