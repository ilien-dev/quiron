# First impressions of Polywork as a software engineer

I have a LinkedIn profile I touch twice a year, a GitHub profile that says a lot about my code and nothing about my work, and a personal site that is permanently "almost updated." So when I came across Polywork, a professional network that promises to show what you actually do rather than only your job titles, I was curious. Here are my first impressions after spending some time with it as a working software engineer.

## What Polywork is trying to be

Polywork's pitch is that a career is not a linear list of employers. Most of us do many things at once: a day job, an open source library, a talk at a meetup, a side project, a few blog posts, some mentoring. A traditional résumé flattens all of that into a column of company names.

Polywork replaces the résumé with a timeline of "highlights." Each highlight is a short post about something you did, tagged with an activity type such as shipped a project, wrote an article, gave a talk, or got a new role. Your profile becomes a feed of accomplishments rather than a static document.

For developers, that framing is appealing, because so much of our best work never fits in a job description.

## Onboarding and setting up a profile

Getting started was quick. The profile builder walks you through the basics, and the design is clean and noticeably less cluttered than most professional networks. It feels closer to a personal site than a database record.

The first thing I noticed was how little pressure there was to fill in every field. Older networks nag you toward a "100% complete" profile. Here, a profile is mostly whatever highlights you choose to add, so the empty state doesn't feel like a failure.

I did stall at the blank page when adding my first highlight. Deciding what counts as worth posting takes some thought. Is fixing a nasty race condition a highlight? What about a small internal tool nobody outside my team will ever see? I ended up writing about a few things I'd normally dismiss as "just my job," and that turned out to be a useful exercise in itself.

## Highlights suit engineering work

This is the part I liked most. Engineering work is full of small wins that never make it onto a résumé: a migration that cut build times, a refactor that deleted two thousand lines, a debugging session that uncovered a subtle bug in a dependency.

Posting these as short highlights is a low-friction way to keep a running record of your work. It reminded me of keeping a "brag document," which many senior engineers recommend for performance reviews and promotion cases. Polywork makes that habit public and a bit more social.

A few things worked well:

- **Short form is fine.** A highlight can be a couple of sentences and a link. You don't need to write a full blog post.
- **Links and media attach easily.** Pointing to a pull request, a repo, a demo, or an article takes little effort.
- **The timeline gives context.** Scrolling through it shows a trajectory, which a list of job titles can't.

For anyone who dreads writing "self-promotional" content, the small-and-frequent format is a gentler way in.

## Badges and signaling what you're open to

Another idea I liked is the ability to signal what you're open to: hiring, mentoring, collaborating on a project, speaking, advising, or just chatting. It's a small feature, but it changes how a profile reads.

On most networks, the implicit message is "I'm either employed or job hunting." Real careers are more varied. I might not want a new job, but I'd happily pair with someone on an open source project or answer questions from a junior developer. Being able to say that explicitly makes the network feel more like a place to meet people than a place to be recruited.

It also helps the other direction. If you're looking for a collaborator on a side project, a contributor for a library, or a speaker for an event, you have a better chance of finding someone who has said they're interested.

## Discovery and collaboration

Here my impressions are more mixed, mostly because the value of a network depends on who is on it. Collaboration features are only useful if the people you'd want to collaborate with are active there. In my first stretch of use, I saw plenty of designers, product people, creators, and founders, and a thinner slice of engineers than I'm used to seeing on developer-focused platforms.

That isn't necessarily a downside. Some of the most interesting connections for an engineer come from outside engineering: a designer who wants to prototype something, a founder who needs a technical cofounder, a writer who covers developer tools. But if you expect a dense community of backend or systems engineers, you may need to bring your own network.

I also found the follow-and-discover experience a little less polished than the profile side. Finding relevant people took more manual effort than I'd like, and I'd love better ways to filter by skills and interests.

## What I didn't love

No first-impressions post is honest without some complaints.

**It's another place to maintain.** I already have GitHub, a blog, a LinkedIn profile, and a social account or two. Adding one more place to post is a real cost. If highlights could be pulled in automatically from sources like GitHub activity or my dev.to posts, I'd use it far more consistently.

**Recruiters still live elsewhere.** If your goal is purely to get hired, the places where recruiters search are still the older, larger networks. Polywork can complement those but doesn't replace them yet.

**Developer-specific signals are light.** Code is the best evidence of what an engineer can do, and the platform doesn't do much to surface it beyond links. Deeper integration with repositories or technical writing would make it more compelling for our field.

**The "personal brand" energy.** Any platform built around highlighting your accomplishments risks becoming a stream of polished self-congratulation. Mostly it avoided that, but I could see it drifting that way as it grows.

## Who I think it's for

After some time with it, I'd say Polywork suits engineers who:

- Do a lot of things outside their day job and want one place to show them
- Are early in their career and don't yet have a long list of impressive employers
- Freelance, consult, or work on side projects
- Want a lightweight way to log and share their progress

It's probably less compelling if you want a large, active developer community, or if you just want recruiters to find you quickly.

## Final thoughts

My first impression is positive but cautious. The idea that your work should be the center of your professional identity, rather than your job titles, is the right one, and it matches how engineers actually build careers. The highlight format is easy to use, the design is pleasant, and the "open to" signals are a smart touch.

Whether it becomes part of my regular routine depends on two things: how much of the posting can be automated, and whether the engineers I want to connect with end up there. For now, I'll keep adding highlights and see whether it earns a permanent spot next to my other profiles.

If you've tried Polywork, I'd like to hear how it went for you. Did it lead to any real collaborations, or did it just become another profile gathering dust? Let me know in the comments.
