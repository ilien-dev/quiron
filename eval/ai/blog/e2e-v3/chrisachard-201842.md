# How to find consulting clients

Finding consulting clients can feel like a paradox: companies want experienced consultants, but you can't become experienced until someone hires you.

The good news is that there's nothing mysterious about getting clients. It's a repeatable process, and it comes down to four things:

1. Choosing a specific problem
2. Showing that you can solve it
3. Starting the right conversations
4. Following up, every time

You don't need a huge audience or a fancy website or a polished personal brand. You need a clear offer and a reliable way to reach the people who might need it.

## Start with a problem, not a job title

"Software consultant" is too broad, since it tells a client what you are but not why they should hire you.

A better pitch ties a particular kind of customer to an outcome they'd pay for:

> I help growing SaaS companies reduce AWS costs without slowing down their engineering teams.

Some other examples:

- Helping startups get their infrastructure ready for enterprise security reviews
- Migrating legacy React applications to modern frameworks
- Fixing slow PostgreSQL queries for high-traffic products
- Building internal tools that replace spreadsheet-heavy workflows
- Helping engineering teams set up reliable CI/CD pipelines

Being specific makes marketing easier, because you know what to write about and who to contact, and it makes referrals easier too. Nobody remembers that someone is a "general technology consultant." They do remember the person who fixes expensive cloud infrastructure problems.

And you don't have to pick your focus permanently. It only has to be specific enough that someone understands when to call you.

## Sell something small first

A lot of new consultants try to sell an open-ended engagement right away. That's risky for the client, because they don't know the scope, the price or what they'll get.

So start with a small offer that's easy to understand and easy to buy. For example:

> A two-week performance audit covering database queries, application bottlenecks, and infrastructure configuration, followed by a prioritized remediation plan.

A good starter offer has a clear target customer and a problem they'll recognize. It has a defined scope, a limited timeline, something useful they get at the end, and a business outcome they can see.

An assessment, an audit, a workshop, a prototype or a focused piece of implementation can all lead to bigger work. The client gets to try you out without signing up for a six-month project, and you get the context you need to spot what else they might need from you.

Don't give the whole engagement away as a "free consultation," though. A short intro call can be free, but diagnosis and recommendations are valuable professional work.

## Your first clients probably already know you

Your first clients are more likely to come from people you already know than from strangers on the internet. Make a list: former coworkers and managers, founders you know, past customers or vendors, people from technical communities, friends at companies that fit, and agencies that offer services next to yours.

Then write to them one at a time. Don't announce that you're "available for anything." Say what you focus on and make the ask easy to act on:

> I've started helping SaaS teams diagnose and reduce cloud infrastructure costs. I'm looking for companies whose AWS bill has grown faster than their usage. If someone comes to mind, would you be comfortable introducing us?

That isn't aggressive selling. You're simply giving people enough information to recognize a possible match.

Don't pressure anyone to invent work for you; ask for advice, context or an introduction when it's appropriate. A conversation today might turn into a referral three months from now.

## Cold outreach: look for signals

Cold outreach works better when it's based on evidence, so don't build a random list of businesses. Look for signals that a company might have the problem you solve. For instance, they might be:

- Hiring multiple engineers
- Announcing a new funding round
- Migrating to a new platform
- Having a public outage
- Launching an enterprise product
- Posting about rising infrastructure costs
- Opening roles related to your specialty
- Talking about technical challenges in public

Then tie what you know to what they're doing:

> I saw that your team is hiring several platform engineers as you expand the enterprise product. Teams at that stage often discover that deployment permissions and audit trails become blockers during customer reviews. I help SaaS companies address those gaps. Is that something your team is encountering?

You won't close a deal in one message, and you shouldn't try: all you want to find out is whether a relevant problem really exists. Keep it short. Don't attach a proposal, don't drop in a calendar link with no context, and don't list every service you offer.

## Show your work

Clients can't look inside your head, so they look for evidence. That can be technical articles, case studies, open-source contributions or conference talks. It can also be architecture reviews, before-and-after performance numbers, testimonials, or detailed write-ups of past work.

Write about the problems you want to get hired for. If you want database optimization work, an article about CSS architecture might show you're smart, but it won't help your positioning.

Good content is concrete. Say how you diagnosed the issue, what options you looked at, which tradeoffs mattered and what changed afterwards. If the client details are confidential, anonymize them:

> A subscription company was processing billing jobs sequentially. We redesigned the workflow around idempotent parallel workers, reducing processing time from hours to minutes.

One good case study is usually worth more than ten generic posts.

Content rarely brings in leads on its own, though. Where it really pays off is when someone gets your message, searches your name and finds proof that you understand their problem.

## Other consultants and agencies send work too

Other consultants and agencies can be a great source of work. Sometimes they need expertise they don't have, or extra hands during a busy stretch. Sometimes they need someone to maintain a system after launch, or someone they trust to hand leads to that are too small or outside their focus.

Look for people whose work fits next to yours without competing with it. A designer might team up with a frontend consultant, a product agency might need a security specialist, and a fractional CTO might need people they can rely on to build things.

Go into it by offering something. Find out what a good referral looks like for them, pass along leads that fit, and be someone they can count on when you work together. A few partners you trust can give you a steadier pipeline than a big social media following.

## When they agree to talk

When a prospect agrees to a call, resist the urge to start pitching. Ask questions instead:

- What prompted you to address this now?
- What happens if the problem stays unresolved?
- Who is affected by it?
- What have you already tried?
- What constraints must a solution respect?
- Who is involved in approving the project?
- What would a successful outcome look like?

You're trying to find out whether the problem matters, whether you can solve it, and whether they're ready to do something about it.

Summarize what you heard before you suggest an approach, because it shows you actually understood and it gives them a chance to correct your assumptions.

If there's no real problem, tell them honestly. Saving a prospect from work they don't need builds more trust than pushing every conversation toward a sale.

## Following up

Consulting deals often stall because priorities change, budgets need approval or people get busy, so silence doesn't always mean no.

When you follow up, have a reason. Share an article that's relevant, clarify a recommendation, send a short project outline, mention something new you noticed, or ask whether the priority has changed. Don't send messages that just say "Just checking in." And make it easy for them to give you an honest answer, even if that answer is "not now."

Keep simple notes on each conversation and pick a date for the next step. A spreadsheet is enough.

Don't wait until your current project ends to look for the next one, either. That's where the pressure comes from. Keep doing business development while you're busy. A weekly routine you can manage might look like this:

- Contacting five well-matched prospects
- Following up on five existing conversations
- Reconnecting with two people in your network
- Publishing one useful technical insight
- Talking to one potential referral partner

Track the things you control, not only signed contracts. Revenue shows up late and unpredictably, while sending thoughtful messages and having conversations are things you can do today. Over time you'll see which channels bring in real opportunities. Put more effort there, and do less of whatever gets attention but no conversations.

Most consultants don't struggle because they lack expertise. They struggle because the offer is vague, or they only reach out now and then, or they stop following up too soon. You don't need everyone to know who you are, just a small number of the right people who understand exactly how you can help.
