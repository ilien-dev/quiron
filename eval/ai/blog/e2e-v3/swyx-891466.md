# Community Heat, or Why You Should Get Good at Events

I keep coming back to one idea when I think about careers and community building, and about how ideas spread in tech: heat. I mean the energy that builds up around a person or a project once people start paying attention. And one of the most reliable ways to make heat is events. Talks, meetups, conferences, livestreams, panels, whatever form they take.

So I want to argue that getting good at events is one of the highest-leverage things you can do for your career and your community. That means speaking at them, organizing them, or just showing up and taking part, and it holds even if public speaking terrifies you right now.

## What I mean by heat

Heat is the buzz that builds when enough people look at one thing at once. Think of a blog post that gets a handful of quiet reads next to one that gets shared and argued over on several sites at the same time. Or a project that picks up a few GitHub stars over months, next to one that suddenly shows up in trending, with people tweeting about it, writing follow-up posts and building on it within days.

Quality helps, but heat isn't only quality. It comes from being visible at the right moment, in front of the right audience, with the right people passing it on. Events are one of the most reliable ways I know to get exactly that kind of concentrated attention.

## Why events work so well

A blog post can be read by anyone, any time, alone. A conference talk happens at one moment, in front of a room (physical or virtual) full of people who are all watching it together. Nothing people read on their own schedule does that. When a talk lands the whole room shifts, and everybody in it feels the shift together. People tweet during the talk and discuss it afterward, and it comes up in conversations for months.

Events also compress time. You can build an audience or a reputation slowly, by blogging steadily or contributing to open source, and it works, but it takes a long while. One good conference talk can do more for your visibility in twenty minutes than months of quietly shipping code or writing posts that never catch on. The slow work still matters. It's usually what gives you something worth talking about in the first place. The event is what takes that quiet work and puts it in front of a lot more people at once.

And when you speak, you don't only reach the people in the room. You reach everyone they talk to afterward, everyone who watches the recording, everyone who reads the tweetstorm summary, and whoever finds a blog post about your talk months later. The ripples go well past the audience, especially when people like the talk enough to pass it along. Over several events this compounds. Each talk makes you more present in a particular community, and so more likely to be invited to the next event, mentioned in the next roundup post, or thought of when someone needs an expert on your topic.

## You don't have to be on stage

When people hear "get good at events" they tend to assume it means becoming a polished speaker. That's one way. There are others.

Organizing meetups, conferences or panels puts you in the middle of a community's energy without putting you on the stage. Organizers get to know speakers, attendees and sponsors, and they often have a lot of say in where a community goes, because they're the ones who choose what gets amplified.

Showing up again and again as an engaged attendee matters more than people think. Ask good questions after talks, introduce people to each other, be someone others see around, and over time you build real social capital without ever stepping on stage.

You can also document events: live-tweet the talks, write recap posts, make highlight threads. That builds heat around the event and around you. People start to link you with catching and sharing the community's best ideas, and that's a role worth having in its own right.

## The fear

I get it. Public speaking is one of the most common fears, and standing in front of a room (or a camera, for virtual events) can be paralyzing. A few things have helped people I know get past it:

- **Start small.** A five-minute lightning talk at a local meetup is a much lower-stakes way to learn than jumping straight to a keynote.
- **Talk about what you've actually built or learned.** People connect with real experience far more than with polish or a show of expertise.
- **Practice out loud, more than once.** Reading your slides silently in your head is not the same as saying the words out loud several times before the real thing.
- **Call the nerves excitement.** Your body does nearly the same thing either way, and the story you tell yourself about it changes how it feels.

## It adds up

The people making heat around their work often aren't more talented than everyone else. A lot of the time they're just visible more often, and events are one of the most efficient ways to build that. Every event you show up to properly, on stage or off, adds a bit to how the community sees you. Over years that turns into reputation, trust and opportunity, and it's hard to get the same from writing and code alone.

If you're doing good work and nobody seems to notice, the problem might be visibility, not quality. You don't need to become a world-class keynote speaker overnight. Start small and keep showing up. It's fine to get better at this slowly. The heat around your work often matters as much as the work, and events are where a lot of that heat gets made.
