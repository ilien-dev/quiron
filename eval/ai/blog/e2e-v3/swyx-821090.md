# September 11th from Singapore

I was a teenager in Singapore when the planes hit the towers. It was evening there, twelve hours ahead of New York, and I don't remember it as one clear memory so much as pieces: the television on in the living room, my parents unusually quiet, the same footage looping until it stopped looking real.

Every year around this date I end up thinking about how something on the other side of the world reshaped so much of the one I was growing up in.

## Watching from far away

A lot of Americans remember September 11th with total clarity. Where they were, who they called, what the sky looked like. For me it came through a screen. There was no smoke in the air and nobody I knew was in the buildings, and it still felt enormous.

Singapore is a small and tightly connected country. We learned about the world through the news, and that night the news was one story. At school the next day the teachers tried to explain what had happened, and I don't think any of them really knew how. We held a moment of silence, and I remember it as the quietest my school had ever been.

## What changed

In the months and years after, the effects turned up in small ways around me. Airport security got noticeably stricter. The local news talked more about terrorism and paid more attention to national security at home. Travel had always felt easy and now it came with more questions, more lines and more forms.

As I got older I started to see that the world I was walking into as a young adult had been shaped heavily by that day. The wars, the politics, the way we think about privacy and surveillance. So much of it goes back to those few hours.

## What it has to do with tech

It might seem strange to write about this on a developer blog, but I've come to see a connection.

A lot of what we build today carries the fingerprints of a post-9/11 world: identity verification, data retention policies, content moderation, access control. For engineers the balance between security and privacy isn't an abstract debate. We make decisions about it every day, sometimes without noticing. What we log, what we store, who can see it and for how long.

I think about that more now. The tools we build can protect people and they can also be used to watch them. When I remember how fast the norms shifted after that day, I want to think harder about the defaults I set.

## Remembering

Nearly three thousand people died that day, and many more have died since from illnesses connected to the recovery effort. Behind every one of those numbers is a family that still feels it.

I didn't lose anyone. I watched from far away. But I think it's worth remembering even from a distance, because in some way the event belongs to all of us. It changed the world my generation grew up in, wherever we happened to be.

If you have your own memory of that day, from wherever you were, I'd be grateful if you shared it in the comments. It helps to remember together.
