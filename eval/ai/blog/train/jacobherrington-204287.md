# How to Find Mentors, Grow a Network, and Make Friends

When I started out as a developer, I thought my career would be built entirely on technical skill. Learn the right languages, ship good code, and everything else would take care of itself.

It took me a while to realize how wrong that was. Almost every major opportunity I've had, from my first real job to promotions to side projects, came through people. Someone recommended me. Someone answered a question I was stuck on. Someone gave me honest feedback when I needed it.

The problem is that "network" and "mentor" can sound intimidating, even a little transactional. Nobody wants to be the person handing out business cards at a meetup. The good news is that the best relationships don't come from networking in that sense. They come from being curious, being helpful, and showing up consistently.

Here's what's worked for me and for a lot of developers I know.

## Rethink what a mentor is

A lot of people imagine a mentor as a single wise senior engineer who takes you under their wing, meets with you every week, and guides your entire career. That does happen, but it's rare, and waiting for it can leave you stuck.

In practice, most mentorship is smaller and more scattered:

- A coworker who reviews your pull requests thoughtfully
- Someone in a Discord server who answers your questions about Rust
- A blog author whose posts you read every week
- A friend who's two years ahead of you in their career and remembers what it was like

You can have lots of mentors, and most of them won't even know they're mentoring you. That's okay. Think of it as a personal board of advisors rather than a single guru.

## How to find mentors

### Start where you already are

The easiest place to find mentors is your current job or school. Look around for people whose work you admire, and ask them specific questions.

"Will you be my mentor?" is a big ask. It's vague and it asks for an open-ended commitment. Compare that to:

> "I really liked how you structured the caching layer in that PR. Would you have 20 minutes this week to walk me through how you approached it?"

That's specific, it's flattering in an honest way, and it has a clear time limit. Most people are happy to say yes. And if the conversation goes well, it's natural to follow up with another question later. That's how mentorship usually starts: not with a formal agreement, but with a series of good conversations.

### Make it easy to help you

When you ask for help, do the work upfront. Explain what you've tried, what you expected, and where you're stuck. Share code snippets or links. Respect their time.

People are much more willing to mentor someone who clearly puts in effort. It also makes the conversation more productive for both of you.

### Close the loop

This is the most underrated tip I know. When someone gives you advice, go back later and tell them what happened.

> "Hey, I tried the approach you suggested for the migration and it worked. Thanks again, that saved me a ton of time."

It takes thirty seconds, and it makes the other person feel that their help mattered. It also keeps you on their radar. People remember the folks who follow up.

### Look outside your company

If your workplace doesn't have many senior people, or you're self-taught and don't have a workplace yet, look to communities:

- Online communities like DEV, Discord servers, Slack groups, and subreddits for your stack
- Open source projects, where maintainers often guide new contributors
- Local meetups and conferences
- Formal mentorship programs, which many communities and bootcamps offer

## How to grow a network (without feeling gross)

"Networking" gets a bad reputation because people picture it as collecting contacts for personal gain. But a healthy network is really just a group of people who know you, trust you, and would be happy to hear from you.

Here's how to build one naturally.

### Give before you ask

The fastest way to build relationships is to be useful. You don't need to be an expert to help people:

- Answer questions in forums or community chats, even beginner ones
- Share articles or tools you found helpful
- Write about what you're learning. Your "I finally understood closures" post will help someone who's where you were last month
- Leave thoughtful comments on other people's work
- Contribute to open source, even if it's just docs or small fixes

When you consistently help others, people start to recognize your name. Relationships form on their own.

### Show up consistently

One-off interactions rarely build lasting connections. What works is showing up in the same places over time.

Pick one or two communities and actually participate. Go to the same monthly meetup. Comment regularly in the same online spaces. After a few months, you'll recognize people and they'll recognize you. That's when real conversations start happening.

### Be genuinely curious

At events or in DMs, you don't need an elevator pitch. Just ask people about their work:

- "What are you working on right now?"
- "What got you into this field?"
- "What's something you've learned recently that surprised you?"

Then listen. Most people love talking about their work, and they'll remember you as someone who was interested.

### Share your work publicly

Writing blog posts, giving talks, streaming, or posting projects on GitHub all create what some people call "surface area for luck." When your work is visible, people can find you even when you're not actively looking for them.

You don't need a big audience. Even a handful of readers can lead to great connections.

## How to actually make friends

Mentors and networks are great, but let's be honest: a lot of us also just want friends in this industry. People to vent to after a rough deploy, celebrate wins with, and nerd out about new tools.

Friendship at work (and in tech communities) is built the same way as anywhere else: repeated, low-stakes interactions and a bit of vulnerability.

### Move from public to private

A lot of friendships start in public spaces, like a comment thread or a group chat, and then move to DMs. If you've had a few good exchanges with someone, send a direct message:

> "Loved your take on the testing thread. I've been wrestling with the same thing on my team. How are you handling flaky integration tests?"

That small step from public to one-on-one is often what turns an acquaintance into a friend.

### Suggest something small

A virtual coffee, a pairing session, or grabbing lunch after a meetup. Keep it casual and low-commitment. Not every invitation will turn into a friendship, but some will.

### Be open about struggles

Tech can feel like everyone else has it all figured out. Being honest about imposter syndrome, burnout, or a bug that ate your entire afternoon makes you more relatable. Often, the other person will share their own story, and that's where real connection happens.

### Celebrate other people

When a friend ships something, gets a new job, or publishes a post, cheer for them publicly. Share it. Leave a comment. People remember who was in their corner.

## Common worries

**"I'm too junior. I don't have anything to offer."**
You do. Beginners bring fresh perspective, energy, and questions that help others clarify their thinking. Plus, you can always offer gratitude, which is more valuable than you'd expect.

**"I'm an introvert."**
So are many developers. Online communities, writing, and one-on-one conversations are all introvert-friendly ways to connect. You don't have to work the room at a conference.

**"I don't have time."**
You don't need hours. A few thoughtful comments a week, one coffee chat a month, and following up with people who help you add up surprisingly fast.

**"What if I get ignored?"**
You will, sometimes. People are busy. It's not personal. Keep reaching out to others, and don't let a few non-responses discourage you.

## Wrapping up

Mentors, networks, and friendships aren't separate things. They overlap and grow out of the same habits:

- Ask specific questions and respect people's time
- Help others generously
- Show up consistently in the same places
- Follow up and close the loop
- Be curious, honest, and kind

None of this happens overnight. But if you start small and keep at it, a year from now you'll look around and realize you're surrounded by people who've got your back. And before long, you'll be the mentor someone else is looking for.
