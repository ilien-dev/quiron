# News: Call to Remove RMS from FSF

If you've been anywhere near free software circles this week, you've probably seen the growing call for Richard Stallman to be removed from every leadership position he holds, including his seat on the board of the Free Software Foundation. This isn't a fringe complaint from a handful of people online. It's an open letter signed by a large and growing number of developers, maintainers, and organizations across the free and open source software world, and it deserves a clear explanation of what's happening and why it matters.

## What Triggered This

The immediate spark was a set of comments Stallman made on an MIT mailing list regarding the Jeffrey Epstein case, in which he made remarks widely seen as defending or minimizing the severity of the situation involving Epstein's associates. Those comments were the tipping point, but they weren't happening in a vacuum. Stallman has a long history of statements and behavior, documented over decades, that many in the community have found alienating, inappropriate, or outright harmful, particularly toward women in tech spaces.

The open letter calling for his removal lays out a pattern rather than a single incident, framing this as the latest and most public example of a problem that has existed for a long time.

## Why This Matters Beyond One Person

It would be easy to frame this purely as a story about one controversial figure, but the implications reach further than that. Stallman isn't just a random contributor. He is the founder of the GNU Project, the original author of the GNU General Public License, and one of the most recognizable faces of the free software movement itself. His conduct, and the FSF's response to it, sends a signal about what kind of community free software wants to be.

For years, critics have pointed out that tolerating this kind of behavior from prominent figures makes free software spaces less welcoming, particularly for people who are already underrepresented in the field. The call for his removal isn't just about accountability for one person's words. It's about whether organizations like the FSF are willing to prioritize the health and inclusivity of their community over the reputation of a single founder.

## The Response So Far

As of this writing, Stallman has resigned from his position at MIT following the backlash, and pressure continues to mount on the FSF board specifically. The FSF has not yet announced a permanent removal, and reactions within the community remain split. Some see this as a long overdue reckoning, while others worry about erasing the contributions of someone who, whatever his personal failings, played an undeniably foundational role in the free software movement as we know it.

This tension, between separating someone's ideas from their conduct and acknowledging that conduct has consequences, is playing out publicly and in real time across mailing lists, GitHub threads, and social media.

## What Happens Next

Organizations navigating this kind of controversy often move slower than the public wants, partly out of caution and partly out of genuine difficulty in figuring out governance structures that were never designed for this kind of situation. The FSF board will likely face continued pressure until it makes a clear, public decision one way or another. Silence at this point is itself a kind of answer, and the community seems to know it.

## Closing Thoughts

Whatever happens with the FSF board's decision, this moment is a reminder that free software, like every other community, has to reckon with the people who built it and how they treated others along the way. Ideals like software freedom don't exist in a vacuum separate from the people advocating for them. How this plays out will say a lot about what the free software movement is willing to stand for going forward, and it's worth paying attention to, even if you've never contributed a line of GNU code in your life.
