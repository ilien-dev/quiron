# I'm building a new home outside of DEV. Here's why (and where to find me!)

DEV has been an important part of my journey.

It gave me a place to publish before I felt like I had anything worth publishing. It introduced me to thoughtful developers, generous teachers, curious beginners, and people willing to share what they learned the hard way. Some of my most rewarding conversations started in the comments beneath a post I nearly didn’t publish.

I’m grateful for all of that.

But lately, I’ve been thinking more seriously about where my work lives—and who ultimately controls that place.

So I’m building a new home outside of DEV.

This isn’t a dramatic exit, a criticism of the community, or a “platforms are bad” manifesto. I may still share articles here from time to time. DEV remains a valuable community, and I’m not interested in pretending otherwise.

This is simply the next step for me: publishing from a place I own.

## A profile is not quite a home

Publishing platforms make it wonderfully easy to begin. You create an account, open an editor, write something, and press a button. Hosting, distribution, comments, discovery, and formatting are handled for you.

That convenience matters. It removes enough friction for people to find their voice.

But there’s a difference between having a profile somewhere and having a home of your own.

A profile exists inside someone else’s product. Its appearance, reach, organization, and capabilities depend on decisions you don’t make. Navigation can change. Features can disappear. Algorithms can shift. Terms can be updated. A platform can be acquired, decline, or simply move in a direction that no longer fits you.

None of this requires bad intentions. Products evolve.

The problem is that my writing doesn’t feel temporary to me. Articles are part of my work, my memory, and my relationship with readers. I want their primary location to be somewhere durable—somewhere whose purpose isn’t subject to another company’s roadmap.

That means a domain I control, files I can back up, and a publishing system I can move if I need to.

## I want more room to experiment

A developer’s website can be more than a reverse-chronological list of articles.

I want to publish short notes that don’t need to become polished tutorials. I want living documents I can update as my understanding changes. I want project logs, small tools, reading notes, code experiments, and collections of useful links.

I also want to connect related ideas properly.

On a typical publishing platform, every post is an island. There may be tags and recommendation widgets, but the platform decides much of the surrounding experience. On my own site, I can build trails between ideas: a tutorial can link to the project that inspired it, the mistakes I made while building it, and a later article that revises my original conclusion.

That kind of structure feels closer to how learning actually works. Knowledge is rarely a feed. It’s a web.

Building the site is also an experiment in itself. I get to make choices about accessibility, performance, typography, feeds, search, analytics, and all the tiny details that disappear when someone else operates the publishing stack.

Some choices will be imperfect. That’s part of the appeal.

## I want a direct relationship with readers

Social platforms are excellent at creating reach, but reach and connection aren’t the same thing.

If you follow me on a platform, an algorithm still decides whether you see what I publish. A post can reach thousands of people one week and almost nobody the next. Authors and readers are technically connected, but the platform remains in the middle.

My new site will include an RSS feed and an email newsletter. Neither is as fashionable as a recommendation algorithm, but both are refreshingly straightforward.

If you subscribe, you’ll receive what I publish. If you unsubscribe, you won’t. There’s no engagement puzzle to solve and no pressure to manufacture urgency just to remain visible.

I’m especially excited about RSS. It is quiet, open, and reader-controlled. You choose the application. You choose the subscriptions. You choose when to read. No infinite-scroll slot machine is required.

The web could use more relationships like that.

## I want to write differently

Publishing on a community platform creates subtle incentives.

Titles become optimized for clicks. Topics drift toward what is already popular. Posts become easier to skim and harder to remember. There’s pressure to publish frequently, arrive at a confident conclusion, and package every learning experience into a neat list.

I’ve participated in all of that. Sometimes it’s useful! Clear titles and readable structure aren’t sins.

But I want space for work that doesn’t fit the feed particularly well.

I want to write essays that take their time. I want to document unfinished thinking. I want to revisit old posts and openly annotate what I got wrong. I want to publish niche technical investigations even when only twelve people will care—because those twelve people may care a lot.

Owning the publication gives me permission to optimize for usefulness and longevity instead of immediate performance.

## This isn’t goodbye

I don’t believe independent websites and communities like DEV are opponents.

The healthiest version of the web includes both.

Personal sites give creators ownership, flexibility, and permanence. Communities provide discussion, discovery, and the wonderful experience of encountering people outside your existing circle. We lose something when everything is locked inside large platforms, but we also lose something when everyone publishes alone and waits to be discovered.

My plan is to publish on my own site first. Some articles may then appear here in full; others may be adapted, summarized, or linked. When I cross-post, I’ll make the original location clear.

I’ll still read posts here. I’ll still join conversations when I have something useful to contribute. I’m changing where my work begins—not rejecting the people I met along the way.
