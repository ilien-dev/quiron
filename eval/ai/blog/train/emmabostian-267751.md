# DISCUSS: Technical Interview Horror Stories

Technical interviews can be challenging, insightful, and even enjoyable. They can also feel like an escape room designed by someone who forgot to include an exit.

Most developers have at least one interview story that still makes them wince: the impossible whiteboard problem, the interviewer who never looked up from their laptop, the surprise eight-hour assignment, or the rejection email that arrived before the interview had technically ended.

Let’s talk about those experiences.

## The algorithm ambush

You apply for a front-end role focused on building accessible interfaces. The interview begins, and suddenly you’re asked to implement a graph traversal algorithm from memory—on a whiteboard, without documentation, while three people silently watch.

Algorithms can be useful for evaluating problem-solving skills. But when the exercise has little connection to the actual job, it often measures interview preparation more than engineering ability.

Bonus horror points if the interviewer says, “This should be easy.”

## The never-ending take-home project

The recruiter describes the assignment as “a small task that should take about two hours.”

You open the instructions and discover that “small” means:

- Build a full-stack application
- Add authentication and authorization
- Write unit and integration tests
- Make it responsive
- Deploy it
- Document every architectural decision
- Achieve “production-quality” code

Three evenings later, you submit it. The company responds with a template rejection—or disappears completely.

Take-home projects can give candidates room to demonstrate their skills without the pressure of live coding. However, companies should define a realistic time limit, evaluate only what fits within it, and provide meaningful feedback when they ask for substantial work.

## The hostile interviewer

Some interviewers treat the conversation like a contest they must win.

They interrupt every answer, reject valid alternatives without explanation, and ask increasingly obscure questions until they find something you don’t know. Instead of exploring how you think, they seem determined to prove that they are the smartest person in the room.

An interview should not be an interrogation. It is a collaborative evaluation—and candidates are evaluating the company too. If disrespect appears before you are hired, it probably won’t disappear afterward.

## The mystery evaluation

You solve the task, explain your reasoning, discuss trade-offs, and ask thoughtful questions. The conversation seems positive.

Then comes the rejection:

> “We decided to move forward with candidates whose experience more closely matches our needs.”

Which experience? Which needs? What could you improve?

Companies may have legal or logistical reasons for limiting feedback, but completely opaque processes leave candidates guessing. Even one specific observation can make the experience more useful.

## The interview for a different job

The job description says senior developer. During the interview, you learn that the position includes product management, customer support, infrastructure ownership, weekend availability, and mentoring the entire engineering team.

The compensation, however, remains firmly attached to the original role.

Sometimes roles evolve. Sometimes job descriptions are vague. But major responsibilities should not be revealed halfway through the hiring process.

## What makes an interview fair?

A good technical interview does not have to be easy. It should be relevant, respectful, and transparent.

Candidates should know the format in advance. Exercises should resemble the work they would actually perform. Interviewers should permit questions, acknowledge multiple valid solutions, and evaluate communication and judgment—not just whether someone remembers a particular trick under pressure.

And yes, candidates should be compensated when an assignment produces work the company could realistically use.
