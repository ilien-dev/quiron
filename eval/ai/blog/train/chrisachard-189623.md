# Music to Listen to While Coding

Everyone has an opinion about what to listen to while writing code, and honestly, there's no single right answer. What works for deep focus on a gnarly bug might be completely wrong for banging out boilerplate on a Friday afternoon. Still, over the years I've settled into a handful of go-to categories that consistently help me get into a flow state. Here's what's currently in my rotation, broken down by the kind of work I'm doing.

## Lo-Fi Hip Hop

This is the obvious one, and it's popular for a reason. Lo-fi beats are repetitive, low on lyrics, and don't demand much attention, which makes them perfect background noise for tasks that require sustained concentration. The steady tempo also seems to help with typing rhythm, if that's a real thing. There are endless 24/7 livestreams on YouTube if you want something that never ends and never requires you to hit next.

## Video Game Soundtracks

Video game music is composed specifically to keep you engaged without being distracting, which makes it a surprisingly good fit for coding. Composers know that players need to stay focused for hours at a time, so the music is built to loop, to build tension without becoming overwhelming, and to avoid lyrics that would pull attention away from what's happening on screen. Soundtracks from games with exploration or puzzle mechanics tend to work especially well, since they're designed to accompany quiet, thoughtful moments.

## Ambient and Drone

When I need to think through a hard architectural problem, or I'm debugging something gnarly that requires holding a lot of context in my head, I switch to ambient or drone music. There's often no discernible melody, no rhythm to tap along to, just a wash of sound that fills the silence without competing for brain cycles. It sounds strange if you've never tried it, but it's remarkably effective for deep, uninterrupted thinking.

## Classical and Instrumental

For tasks that require a bit more energy but still no lyrics, classical music is a classic choice for a reason. Baroque pieces in particular have a steady, predictable structure that some people find helps with concentration. It's not for everyone, but if lo-fi starts to feel too repetitive, switching to a Bach concerto or a Chopin nocturne can be a nice change of pace.

## Electronic and Synthwave

When I'm doing something more mechanical, like refactoring, writing tests, or working through a big list of small tasks, I like something with a bit more energy. Synthwave and electronic music without vocals hits that sweet spot of keeping me moving without derailing my train of thought. It's the musical equivalent of a caffeine boost.

## Silence

Sometimes the best music to code to is no music at all. When I'm trying to solve a genuinely hard problem, even background music can be one too many inputs competing for attention. Noise-canceling headphones with nothing playing, or just a quiet room, can be more productive than any playlist.

## Building Your Own System

The real trick isn't finding the perfect genre, it's matching the music to the task. Repetitive, low-effort work pairs well with more energetic music, because it helps keep you moving. Hard problems that require deep thought usually pair better with something quieter, or nothing at all. Pay attention to what you naturally reach for during different kinds of work, and you'll probably notice your own patterns emerging.

## Wrapping Up

There's no universal playlist that works for everyone, and what works for me might completely fail for you. The best approach is to experiment, notice what actually helps you focus versus what just feels good in the moment, and build a small rotation of go-to options for different kinds of coding sessions. And if none of it works, don't feel bad about coding in silence. Sometimes that's exactly what the problem in front of you needs.
