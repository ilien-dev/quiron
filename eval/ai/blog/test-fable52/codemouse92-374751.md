## What went well

**Starting small.** The first version did exactly one thing, badly. But it ran end to end, and that changed everything. Once there's a working path through the system, every improvement is a change to something real rather than a guess about something imaginary.

**Writing things down.** I kept a plain text file of decisions: what I chose, and why. It felt like overhead in week one. By week three, when I couldn't remember why I'd picked [TECHNICAL CHOICE] over [ALTERNATIVE], it had already paid for itself.

**Working in short sessions.** Most of this project was built in [TIME BLOCKS, e.g. "an hour before work"]. Short sessions force you to leave things in a working state, and that discipline kept the project from ever getting stuck in a half-broken refactor.

## What went wrong

**I rewrote too early.** In week two I threw out [COMPONENT] and rebuilt it "properly." The new version was cleaner and solved problems I didn't have yet. I lost [NUMBER] days and gained an abstraction I've since partly undone.

**I underestimated the boring parts.** [BORING THING: error handling, auth, deployment, data migration] took roughly as long as the feature it supports. It always does, and I still didn't budget for it.

**I avoided the hard problem.** There's one piece of this project, [THE HARD PART], that I'm not sure how to solve. I spent a suspicious amount of time polishing everything around it. Productive procrastination is still procrastination.

## Lessons I'm taking into month two

1. **Do the scariest thing first.** If one unknown could sink the project, find out early, not after the easy 80% is built.
2. **Wait for the pain before abstracting.** Duplicated code is cheap to fix later. The wrong abstraction is not.
3. **Ship something a person can touch.** Feedback on a rough working version beats feedback on a polished description every time.
4. **Measure progress by what works, not by what's written.** Lines of code and commit counts feel good but say very little.

## One month in

The biggest surprise wasn't technical. It was how much momentum matters. The days I did twenty minutes of work were worth more than they looked, because they kept the project loaded in my head. The weeks with gaps cost far more than the hours I skipped.
