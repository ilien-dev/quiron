At 8:46 in the morning in New York, American Airlines Flight 11 struck the North Tower of the World Trade Center. In Singapore, it was 8:46 at night.

That twelve-hour gap shaped how a whole region experienced September 11th, 2001. For most Americans, it was a Tuesday morning: commutes, coffee, the start of a workday interrupted. For Singapore, it was the end of the day. People were having dinner, watching the evening news, or getting ready for bed. Then the television showed one burning tower, then a second plane.

By the time the South Tower collapsed, it was 9:59 PM in Singapore. The North Tower fell at 10:28 PM. Many people across Asia went to sleep, or tried to, knowing the world had changed. They woke on September 12th to newspapers trying to explain what had happened while the day was already over.

## The same moment, a different date

I've been thinking about this as a developer, because it's one of the clearest examples of something we get wrong in software all the time: **an event happens at an instant, but people remember it on a date, and the date depends on where they were.**

Here's the first impact rendered across a few time zones:

```python
from datetime import datetime
from zoneinfo import ZoneInfo

impact = datetime(2001, 9, 11, 8, 46, tzinfo=ZoneInfo("America/New_York"))

for tz in ["America/New_York", "UTC", "Asia/Singapore", "Asia/Tokyo"]:
    local = impact.astimezone(ZoneInfo(tz))
    print(f"{tz:20} {local:%Y-%m-%d %H:%M}")
```

```
America/New_York     2001-09-11 08:46
UTC                  2001-09-11 12:46
Asia/Singapore       2001-09-11 20:46
Asia/Tokyo           2001-09-11 21:46
```

Singapore still lands on September 11th, but only just. Go a little further east and the later events of that morning fall on September 12th. In Sydney, the towers fell after midnight. For Australians, the shock of "9/11" largely belongs to the morning of the 12th.

Nobody disputes the name. "September 11th" is a global label now. But the lived experience, the hour of the day, the meal you were eating, whether you saw it live or woke up to it, varies by longitude.

## What this means for the code we write

Most of us will never build systems that record history. But we build systems that record *moments*: transactions, log entries, medical events, alerts, birthdays, deadlines. And the same lessons apply:

1. **Store instants, not wall-clock strings.** Save timestamps in UTC (or with an explicit offset). `2001-09-11 08:46` with no zone is ambiguous. `2001-09-11T12:46:00Z` is not.

2. **Keep the original zone when it matters.** "What time was it *for the person who experienced this*?" is often a real business question. Converting everything to UTC and throwing away the zone loses that.

3. **"What day did it happen?" is a question with an implicit location.** Daily reports, anniversaries, "events on this date" features: they all need to answer *whose* day.

4. **Use real time zone data.** Singapore has been UTC+8 since 1982, but it's had six different offsets in the twentieth century. Hardcoding offsets will eventually betray you. Use the IANA database.

## Remembering across time zones

Every year, the anniversary arrives in Singapore first, twelve hours before New York. Memorials and moments of silence observed at 8:46 AM Eastern happen in the evening here, just as the events did.

There's something fitting in that. Some places live through a moment at sunrise and some at nightfall, but it's the same moment, shared by everyone on the planet at once. Our calendars and clocks split it into different numbers. The instant itself doesn't split.

Nearly 3,000 people died that day. However we write the timestamp, that's what the date stands for.
