# Community Heat, or Why You Should Get Good at Events

There's a concept I keep coming back to when I think about career growth, community building, and how ideas actually spread in tech: heat. Not literal heat, obviously, but the kind of energy and momentum that builds around a person, a project, or an idea when it starts gaining attention. And one of the most reliable ways to generate that heat is through events — talks, meetups, conferences, livestreams, panels, whatever form they take.

In this post, I want to make the case for why getting good at events — speaking at them, organizing them, or just showing up meaningfully — is one of the highest-leverage things you can do for your career and your community, even if public speaking terrifies you right now.

## What Is "Community Heat"?

Community heat is the buzz that builds around something when enough people start paying attention to it at the same time. It's the difference between a blog post that gets a handful of quiet reads and one that gets shared, discussed, referenced, and debated across multiple platforms simultaneously. It's the difference between a project that slowly accumulates a few GitHub stars over months and one that suddenly finds itself trending, with people tweeting about it, writing follow-up posts, and building on top of it within days.

Heat isn't purely a function of quality, though quality certainly helps. It's a function of *visibility at the right moment*, amplified by the right people, in front of the right audience. And events are one of the most reliable mechanisms for generating exactly that kind of concentrated visibility.

## Why Events Generate Heat Better Than Almost Anything Else

### They Create a Shared Moment

A blog post can be read anytime, by anyone, at their own pace, in isolation. A talk at a conference happens at a specific moment, in front of a room (physical or virtual) full of people experiencing it simultaneously. That shared experience creates a kind of collective energy that asynchronous content simply can't replicate.

When a talk lands well, you can feel the room's energy shift in real time — and crucially, everyone in that room experienced that shift together. That shared moment becomes something people talk about afterward, tweet about during, and reference in conversations for months. It's a single point in time that a whole group of people can rally around.

### They Compress Time

Building an audience or reputation slowly through consistent blogging or open source contributions absolutely works, but it takes a long time. Events compress that timeline dramatically. A single well-received conference talk can do more for your visibility in twenty minutes than months of quietly shipping code or writing posts that don't quite catch fire.

This isn't to say the slow, consistent work doesn't matter — it absolutely does, and it's often what gives you something substantive to actually talk about at that event in the first place. But the event itself is often the catalyst that takes existing, quietly-accumulated value and suddenly makes it visible to a much wider audience all at once.

### They Create Natural Amplification Networks

When you speak at an event, you're not just reaching the people in the room. You're reaching everyone those people talk to afterward, everyone who watches the recording later, everyone who reads the tweetstorm summary, and everyone who stumbles on a blog post referencing your talk months down the line. Events create ripples that extend far beyond the immediate audience, especially when the content resonates enough that people want to pass it along.

This amplification effect compounds when you speak at multiple events over time. Each talk reinforces your presence in a particular community, making you more likely to be invited to the next event, referenced in the next roundup post, or thought of when someone needs an expert on a particular topic.

## Getting Good at Events Doesn't Mean Just Speaking

When people hear "get good at events," they often assume this means becoming a polished public speaker. That's one path, certainly, but it's far from the only one.

**Organizing** events — meetups, conferences, panels — puts you at the center of a community's energy without requiring you to be the one on stage. Organizers build relationships with speakers, attendees, and sponsors, and they often have outsized influence on a community's direction precisely because they're the ones shaping what gets amplified.

**Showing up consistently** as an engaged attendee also matters more than people give it credit for. Asking thoughtful questions after talks, introducing people to each other, and being a visible, active presence in a community's event circuit builds real social capital over time, even without ever stepping on stage yourself.

**Documenting events** — live-tweeting talks, writing recap posts, creating highlight threads — is another underrated way to build heat around both the event and yourself. You become associated with capturing and distributing the community's best ideas, which is its own valuable role.

## Overcoming the Fear

I get it — public speaking is one of the most commonly cited fears, and the idea of standing in front of a room (or a camera, for virtual events) can feel genuinely paralyzing. A few things that have helped people I know get past this:

- **Start small.** A five-minute lightning talk at a local meetup is a much lower-stakes way to build the skill than jumping straight to a keynote.
- **Talk about what you've actually built or learned.** Authenticity resonates far more than polish. People connect with genuine experience, not performative expertise.
- **Practice out loud, repeatedly.** Reading your slides silently in your head is not the same as saying the words out loud multiple times before the real thing.
- **Reframe nervousness as excitement.** The physiological response is nearly identical — the story you tell yourself about it makes a real difference in how it feels.

## The Compounding Effect

The people you see consistently generating heat around their work, their projects, or their communities aren't necessarily more talented than everyone else — they're often just more consistently visible, and events are one of the most efficient tools for building that visibility over time.

Each talk, each meetup you organize, each event you show up meaningfully to, adds another data point to how the community perceives you. Over years, this compounds into reputation, trust, and opportunity in a way that's hard to replicate through purely asynchronous work alone.

## Final Thoughts

If you're doing good work but feel like nobody's noticing, it might not be a quality problem — it might be a visibility problem. Events are one of the most powerful tools available for solving that, because they create shared, memorable moments that compress time and generate amplification in a way that few other formats can match.

You don't need to become a world-class keynote speaker overnight. Start small, show up consistently, and give yourself permission to build this skill gradually. The heat you generate around your work often matters just as much as the work itself — and events are where that heat gets made.
