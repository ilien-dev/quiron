# If You Don’t Hire Juniors, You Don’t Deserve Seniors

Every company wants senior engineers.

They want people who can navigate ambiguity, design reliable systems, mentor teammates, communicate with stakeholders, review code thoughtfully, prevent incidents, and ship without constant supervision.

What many companies do not want is junior engineers.

Juniors need onboarding. They ask questions. Their estimates are imperfect. Their pull requests require careful review. They may solve the wrong problem before learning how to identify the right one. Supporting them consumes time that could have been spent delivering features.

So companies compete for seniors while refusing to participate in creating them.

That is not merely shortsighted hiring. It is an attempt to benefit from an ecosystem without contributing to it.

If you do not hire juniors, you do not deserve seniors.

## Senior engineers do not appear fully formed

Every senior engineer was once a beginner who needed somebody to take a chance on them.

Someone reviewed their early code. Someone explained why the database query was slow, why the abstraction was premature, or why the clever solution would become a maintenance problem. Someone let them join an incident call even though they could not yet help much. Someone trusted them with a task slightly beyond their current ability.

Those experiences created the engineer companies now describe as “senior.”

Tutorials can teach syntax. Books can teach principles. Personal projects can build confidence. None of them completely reproduces working inside a real system with real users, deadlines, trade-offs, legacy decisions, and consequences.

Professional judgment develops through participation.

When a company says it only hires senior engineers, it is effectively saying:

> We want the result of years of investment, but we would prefer somebody else to make that investment.

A single company can get away with this. An entire industry cannot.

## “We need someone who can hit the ground running”

This phrase appears in countless job descriptions and hiring discussions.

Sometimes it is reasonable. A small startup facing an existential deadline may genuinely need experienced people. A team rebuilding a critical payments platform may not be the right environment for its first junior hire.

But “we need someone productive immediately” has become less of an exceptional constraint and more of a permanent operating philosophy.

The uncomfortable truth is that senior engineers rarely hit the ground running either.

They may understand distributed systems, React, Kubernetes, or PostgreSQL. They do not understand your undocumented deployment process, your historical architecture, your customers, your organizational incentives, or why a suspicious function named `processDataFinal2` must never be called on Fridays.

They still need context. They simply ask different questions.

A healthy team does not eliminate onboarding. It gets good at onboarding people with different levels of experience.

If your organization cannot tolerate a new engineer needing support, that may reveal less about the candidate and more about the organization. Perhaps the documentation is missing. Perhaps ownership is unclear. Perhaps every experienced engineer is overloaded. Perhaps the system is too fragile for safe experimentation.

Those problems also hurt senior hires. Seniors may conceal the damage better, but they cannot make it disappear.

## Juniors are not discounted seniors

Hiring juniors only makes sense if you understand what they are.

A junior engineer is not a senior engineer available at a lower salary. They should not be given the same expectations, less authority, and twice the scrutiny. They are not there to absorb abandoned tickets nobody else wants.

They are developing professionals who can contribute while learning.

That requires appropriately scoped work, regular feedback, accessible teammates, and increasing responsibility. It also requires patience with mistakes—within reasonable, safe boundaries.

The goal is not to protect juniors from every failure. The goal is to create an environment where failures are small, visible, recoverable, and educational.

Give them a feature with clear boundaries. Let them investigate a bug. Ask them to improve documentation after onboarding. Pair on a migration. Include them in design discussions, even if they mostly listen at first. Have them explain their reasoning during reviews.

Then gradually increase the size and ambiguity of the problems they own.

That is how judgment is built.

## Mentorship is engineering work

Companies often claim to value mentorship while treating it as something engineers should perform invisibly between “real” tasks.

A senior spends two hours pairing with a junior, and the roadmap records two hours of lost productivity. Nobody records the junior’s improved understanding, the defect avoided next month, or the knowledge no longer concentrated in one person.

This accounting is convenient and wrong.

Mentorship is not separate from engineering. It is part of building an engineering organization.

A system is healthier when more people understand it. A team is more resilient when knowledge can move. A company is less fragile when senior engineers can develop others instead of becoming permanent bottlenecks.

If mentorship matters, plan for it.

Reduce the mentor’s delivery load. Recognize teaching in performance reviews. Reward engineers who write useful documentation, conduct careful reviews, and create safe opportunities for others. Do not assign one senior five mentees and call it a growth culture.

Mentorship without time is just another form of unpaid organizational debt.

## Juniors make teams better

Supporting juniors is not charity.

Junior engineers bring benefits that experienced teams routinely underestimate.

They ask questions insiders stopped asking years ago. Why does deployment require twelve manual steps? Why do these services share a database? Why is this value nullable? Why does every new developer need to message the same person for credentials?

Sometimes the answer is a legitimate constraint. Sometimes the answer is, “Nobody has questioned it recently.”

Both answers are useful.

Juniors are also excellent detectors of accidental complexity. If an intelligent new teammate cannot understand a subsystem after a reasonable introduction, the subsystem may be too difficult to understand. Their confusion is information.

Teaching improves the teacher, too. Explaining a decision forces an experienced engineer to separate genuine principles from habits and preferences. “That’s just how it’s done” tends to collapse under sincere questions.

A strong junior can also become productive surprisingly quickly. Many arrive with current knowledge, curiosity, and fewer assumptions about what cannot be changed. Given structure and trust, they often contribute far beyond the tiny tasks organizations initially reserve for them.

## The experience trap

Entry-level job listings have become a running joke:

- Junior title
- Junior salary
- Three years of professional experience required
- Production experience with an exact stack
- Evidence of leadership
- Ability to work independently from day one

This is not an entry-level role. It is an experienced hire with a discounted title.

When nearly every “junior” position requires previous employment, the industry creates a closed loop: you need experience to get experience.

Candidates are advised to solve the problem through unpaid work, open source contributions, endless portfolio projects, certifications, and increasingly elaborate interview preparation. These can all demonstrate initiative, but they should not become an alternative employment system applicants must complete before being considered employable.

Not everyone has equal time for unpaid preparation. People with caregiving responsibilities, demanding jobs, disabilities, or limited financial support are filtered out long before an interview.

Then companies complain that the talent pipeline lacks diversity.

The pipeline is not a mysterious natural phenomenon. It is shaped by the doors employers choose to open.

## What sustainable hiring looks like

A company does not need to fill half its team with new graduates. Sustainable hiring is about balance.

A healthy organization hires across experience levels and gives people room to progress. It knows which teams can support juniors and ensures those teams have the capacity to do so. It defines what growth looks like instead of treating promotion as a reward for heroic overwork.

Its junior roles are actually junior roles. Interviews test potential, foundational knowledge, communication, and learning ability—not familiarity with obscure puzzles or one exact toolchain.

Once hired, juniors receive:

- A structured onboarding plan
- A named mentor with protected time
- Work that matters but has manageable risk
- Clear expectations for their level
- Frequent, specific feedback
- Access to technical discussions
- Increasing ownership as their skills grow

None of this is revolutionary. It is simply management.

The company also accepts that some people it trains will eventually leave. That possibility is often used to argue against investing in juniors, as if senior engineers never resign.

People leave companies that hire them at every level. The answer is not to stop developing employees. The answer is to build a workplace where growth, compensation, trust, and interesting work give them reasons to stay.

And when they do leave, they strengthen the same professional ecosystem your next senior hire will come from.

## Seniority should include creating successors

There is another reason companies that reject juniors should reconsider their obsession with seniors: developing other engineers is part of senior-level impact.

Seniority is not just the ability to solve harder problems alone. It is the ability to improve the effectiveness of a team.

A senior engineer who writes excellent code but cannot share context, delegate, review constructively, or help others grow may be highly skilled, but their impact has a ceiling.

Junior engineers provide opportunities for seniors to practice leadership without forcing everyone into management. They learn how to communicate decisions, calibrate feedback, define safe boundaries, and recognize when to guide versus when to step back.

Without less-experienced teammates, “mentorship” remains an interview story instead of an organizational capability.

## You cannot optimize only for today

Hiring only seniors may look efficient in a quarterly plan. Over time, it produces an expensive, brittle market where every company fights for the same limited group.

It also creates teams with strange shapes: many people qualified to make architectural decisions, fewer people available to execute well-scoped work, and little internal succession. Highly experienced engineers become buried in tasks that could have been valuable learning opportunities for someone earlier in their career.

Meanwhile, promising candidates remain outside the industry because nobody wants to be their first employer.

Software companies love to talk about long-term thinking. They design systems for scale, build multi-year roadmaps, and debate technical debt.

The same thinking should apply to people.

You cannot demand an abundant supply of senior engineers while refusing to create conditions in which junior engineers can become senior. You cannot celebrate mentorship while allocating no time for it. You cannot complain about a talent shortage while defining talent as “someone another company already trained.”

Hire juniors.

Pay the real cost of supporting them. Let experienced engineers teach. Build systems where mistakes are contained and learning is normal. Promote people when they grow, and compensate them so they are not forced to leave to have that growth recognized.

The senior engineers you want are not waiting in an infinite external pool.

They are juniors someone chose to hire.
