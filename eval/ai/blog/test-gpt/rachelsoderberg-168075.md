# How I Am Getting Out From Under My Student Debt

For a long time, I treated my student debt like bad weather: unpleasant, persistent, and mostly outside my control.

I made the minimum payments. I avoided looking too closely at the total. Whenever an email arrived from my loan servicer, I opened it with the same enthusiasm I reserve for software license agreements and dentist reminders.

The debt was always there, but I kept it at the edge of my attention.

That strategy worked until it didn’t. Even when I wasn’t actively thinking about the loans, they influenced my decisions. They affected how much risk I could take, how I thought about changing jobs, and whether an unexpected expense felt manageable or catastrophic.

Eventually, I realized I didn’t need a perfect financial strategy. I needed an honest one.

This is the system I’m now using to get out from under my student debt.

A quick disclaimer: this is my personal approach, not financial advice. Student loans differ by country, program, interest rate, and borrower protections. Before changing repayment plans, refinancing, or making large extra payments, check how the decision affects your specific loans.

## I Stopped Treating the Balance as One Giant Number

My first real step was simple: I listed every loan separately.

For each one, I recorded:

- The current balance
- The interest rate
- The minimum monthly payment
- Whether the rate was fixed or variable
- Whether the loan was federal, private, or otherwise protected
- Any repayment, forgiveness, or hardship options attached to it

Before doing this, I thought of my student debt as one enormous, discouraging number. Breaking it into individual loans turned it into a collection of smaller problems.

That distinction mattered.

One loan had a noticeably higher interest rate than the others. Another had protections I would lose if I refinanced it. A third was small enough that eliminating it could free up part of my monthly cash flow.

The spreadsheet didn’t make the debt disappear, but it gave the debt edges. I could finally see what I was dealing with.

## I Built a Budget I Could Actually Live With

I have created ambitious budgets before. They were beautiful documents describing a person who apparently never ordered food, bought books, attended birthdays, replaced broken electronics, or experienced joy.

Those budgets rarely survived the month.

This time, I started with several months of actual spending. I grouped expenses into broad categories and looked for patterns without trying to justify every purchase.

The goal was not to prove that I had been irresponsible. It was to understand where my money was already going.

I divided my monthly spending into four buckets:

1. Essential expenses
2. Minimum debt payments
3. Flexible spending
4. Savings and additional debt payments

Then I set a realistic baseline for each category.

I still budget for entertainment, eating out, and hobbies. If my plan requires me to behave like a machine for several years, it is not a plan I will follow. The point is to reduce spending deliberately, not remove every enjoyable part of life.

The most useful change was giving myself a fixed amount of guilt-free spending. Once that money is allocated, I can use it without turning every coffee into a moral crisis.

## I Chose a Repayment Strategy

Two common approaches kept appearing in my research.

The debt avalanche method targets the highest-interest loan first while maintaining minimum payments on everything else. Mathematically, this usually reduces the total interest paid.

The debt snowball method targets the smallest balance first. It may cost more in interest, but it can produce quicker psychological wins.

I chose a mostly avalanche-based strategy. I make the required payment on every loan, then direct extra money toward the loan with the highest interest rate.

“Mostly” is important.

If a small loan gets close enough to elimination that paying it off would free meaningful monthly cash flow, I may temporarily prioritize it. Personal finance is partly mathematics and partly behavior. The theoretically optimal plan is useless if I abandon it.

My current system is:

- Automate every minimum payment
- Select one target loan
- Send all routine extra payments to that loan
- Reassess only after the target is paid off or my circumstances change

This prevents me from scattering additional payments across several balances and feeling as though nothing is moving.

## I Automated the Boring Parts

Motivation is unreliable, especially after a long day.

Automation removes a decision from every month. My minimum payments leave automatically, and my planned extra payment is scheduled shortly after I receive income.

That timing is intentional. When I waited until the end of the month, “extra payment money” had a mysterious tendency to become “apparently I needed several small things from the internet money.”

Now the debt payment happens before lifestyle expansion gets a vote.

I still check every transaction and statement. Automation should reduce repetitive work, not replace attention. I also keep a small buffer in the payment account so an unusual billing date does not create an overdraft.

## I Created an Emergency Fund Before Going All In

At first, I wanted to put every available dollar toward the debt. Emotionally, that felt like progress.

In practice, it would have made the plan fragile.

Without cash reserves, the first car repair, medical bill, or broken laptop could force me to borrow again. I would be paying down one balance while creating another, potentially at a much higher interest rate.

So before becoming aggressive with extra payments, I built a starter emergency fund. It is not enormous, but it can absorb common surprises.

I am continuing to grow it gradually while paying down the loans. This slows my payoff date slightly, but it makes the entire system more durable.

Debt reduction is not only about sending money to lenders. It is also about reducing the chance that an ordinary emergency becomes new debt.

## I Looked at Income, Not Just Expenses

There is a limit to how much I can cut.

Housing, food, transportation, healthcare, and utilities do not disappear because I have a spreadsheet. After removing obvious waste, further cuts produce smaller savings and a much less enjoyable life.

Income has more room to grow.

As a developer, I’m focusing on changes that can improve my primary income over time:

- Strengthening skills that are valuable in the job market
- Documenting accomplishments instead of hoping someone notices them
- Preparing for compensation conversations
- Keeping my résumé and portfolio current
- Evaluating job opportunities carefully
- Taking occasional side work when the pay justifies the time

I’m cautious about side projects. A second job that destroys my health or harms my main career is not automatically a good financial decision.

The highest-leverage move may be earning more in my regular working hours, not filling every evening with freelance work.

When additional income arrives, I decide in advance how to use it. Part goes toward current life or savings, and a significant portion goes to the target loan. That balance helps me make progress without feeling that every raise has vanished into the past.

## I Am Avoiding Lifestyle Inflation

Lifestyle inflation rarely arrives as one dramatic purchase. It appears as a series of reasonable upgrades.

A better apartment. More subscriptions. More frequent delivery. A newer car. Premium versions of things that were fine before.

When income increases, spending can expand just as quickly. The debt remains, only now it is surrounded by more expensive habits.

I am trying to keep my core lifestyle stable as my income grows. This does not mean refusing every improvement. It means choosing upgrades consciously instead of treating them as the automatic reward for earning more.

My rule is simple: a raise should increase the gap between what I earn and what I spend.

That gap is where financial options come from.

## I Track Progress Without Obsessing Over It

Checking the balance every day does not make it fall faster.

I update my debt tracker once a month. I record:

- The remaining principal
- The amount paid that month
- The interest charged
- My emergency savings
- My estimated payoff timeline

I also track milestones: every $1,000 paid off, each loan eliminated, and every increase in the percentage of payments going toward principal.

These smaller markers matter because the final goal is distant. If the only acceptable outcome is “debt-free,” years of meaningful progress can feel like failure.

Monthly tracking gives me enough information to stay engaged without allowing the debt to occupy every spare thought.

## I Am Keeping Flexibility in the Plan

A repayment plan should respond to reality.

If I lose income, face a major expense, or become eligible for a beneficial program, I will adjust. I am not going to preserve an arbitrary payoff date at the expense of rent, healthcare, or basic stability.

I also review the rules attached to my loans before making major changes. Refinancing can lower an interest rate in some situations, but it may also remove valuable protections. Extra payments may need specific instructions to ensure they are applied as intended. Forgiveness or income-based programs can change the calculation entirely.

The plan is allowed to evolve.

What matters is that changes are deliberate, not driven by avoidance.

## The Biggest Change Is Psychological

The most important progress has not appeared on a statement.

I no longer think of my student debt as evidence that I failed. I made decisions with the information, resources, and expectations I had at the time. Whether every decision was optimal is less important than what I do now.

Shame made me avoid the numbers. Avoidance made the debt feel more powerful.

Attention has had the opposite effect.

I know what I owe. I know which loan I am targeting. I know how much I plan to pay this month. I know what I will do with a bonus, a raise, or an unexpected expense.

I am not trying to escape the debt through one heroic act. I am building a system that keeps working during ordinary months—the months when motivation is low, nothing dramatic happens, and progress looks like one automated payment after another.

That is how I am getting out from under my student debt: not with a perfect budget, a viral side hustle, or endless self-denial, but with clear numbers, sustainable habits, higher income, and time.

The balance is still there.

But now it is moving in the right direction, and so am I.
