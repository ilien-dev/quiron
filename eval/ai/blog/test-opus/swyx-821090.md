# September 11th from Singapore

I was a teenager in Singapore when the planes hit the towers. It was evening there, twelve hours ahead of New York, and I remember the moment in fragments rather than as a single clear memory: the television on in the living room, my parents unusually quiet, the same footage looping again and again until it stopped looking real.

Every year around this date, I find myself thinking about how an event on the other side of the world managed to reshape so much of the one I was growing up in.

## Watching From Far Away

For a lot of Americans, September 11th is a day they remember with total clarity: where they were, who they called, what the sky looked like. For me, it came through a screen. There was no smoke in the air, no one I knew in the buildings. And yet it felt enormous.

Singapore is a small, tightly connected country. We learned about the world through the news, and the news that night was all one story. At school the next day, teachers tried to explain what had happened, and I don't think any of them fully knew how. We held a moment of silence. I remember it being the quietest my school had ever been.

## How Things Changed

In the months and years afterward, the effects showed up in small ways around me. Security at the airport got noticeably stricter. There was more talk of terrorism in the local news, and more attention paid to national security at home. Travel, which had always felt easy, suddenly came with more questions, more lines, and more forms.

As I got older, I started to understand that the world I was entering as a young adult was shaped heavily by that day. The wars, the politics, the way we think about privacy and surveillance: so much of it traces back to those few hours.

## What It Has to Do With Tech

It might seem strange to write about this on a developer blog, but I've come to see a connection.

Many of the systems we build today, from identity verification and data retention policies to content moderation and access control, carry the fingerprints of a post-9/11 world. The balance between security and privacy isn't an abstract debate for engineers. We make decisions about it every day, sometimes without realizing it: what we log, what we store, who can see it, and for how long.

I think about that more now. The tools we build can protect people, and they can also be used to watch them. Remembering how quickly norms shifted after that day makes me want to be more careful and more intentional about the defaults I set.

## Remembering

Nearly three thousand people died that day, and many more have died since from illnesses connected to the recovery effort. Behind every number is a family who still feels it.

I didn't lose anyone. I watched from far away. But I think it's worth remembering even from a distance, because the event belongs to all of us in some way. It changed the world my generation grew up in, wherever we happened to be.

If you have your own memory of that day, from wherever you were, I'd be grateful if you shared it in the comments. It helps to remember together.
