# If you don't hire juniors, you don't deserve seniors

Every company wants senior developers.

They want people who can untangle legacy systems, make architectural decisions, mentor teammates, debug production incidents, and explain why rewriting everything in the framework of the month might be a bad idea.

They want engineers who can work independently, communicate clearly, and recognize a terrible idea before it becomes a six-month project.

But ask those same companies whether they hire juniors, and suddenly the excuses arrive.

“We need people who can hit the ground running.”

“We don’t have the bandwidth for mentoring.”

“We’re moving too fast.”

Sound familiar?

Here’s the problem: **senior developers don’t appear fully formed in a LinkedIn search result.**

Somebody hired them when they were inexperienced. Somebody reviewed their messy pull requests. Somebody explained the difference between code that works and code that survives production.

If your hiring strategy depends entirely on other companies doing that work, you’re benefiting from an investment you refuse to make.

## Everyone wants the harvest

Imagine an industry where every farm decides planting seeds is too expensive.

They’ll buy mature crops from neighboring farms instead. Much more efficient. No waiting, no uncertainty, no watering.

That strategy works beautifully until everyone adopts it.

This is the contradiction in software hiring. Companies complain about the shortage of experienced engineers while treating the development of inexperienced engineers as someone else’s responsibility.

Individually, the decision can look rational. Training takes time. Mentoring reduces someone’s immediate output. An experienced hire might solve a pressing problem faster.

Collectively, it creates a pipeline nobody wants to maintain.

You cannot demand an endless supply of senior developers while systematically closing the door on the people who could become them.

And no, publishing a blog post about “supporting the next generation of developers” does not count as maintaining that pipeline.

At some point, someone needs a paid job.

## “We can’t afford juniors” deserves a closer look

Sometimes this is true.

A three-person startup with two months of runway may genuinely need someone who has solved its exact problem before. A team handling an urgent reliability crisis may lack the capacity to support a first-time developer.

Not every company must hire a junior immediately. Not every team is ready.

But there’s a difference between a temporary constraint and a permanent business model.

If your company has operated for ten years, employs dozens of engineers, and still “doesn’t have the bandwidth” to develop anyone, that deserves scrutiny.

Where is all the bandwidth going?

Are senior engineers constantly firefighting because nobody gets time to improve the system? Is documentation missing? Does every change require knowledge held by one exhausted person?

Those conditions make hiring juniors difficult. They also make working there difficult for everyone else.

**An organization that cannot support learning has a problem that extends beyond junior hiring.**

Experienced engineers need onboarding too. They need context, feedback, and room to ask questions. Hiring someone senior does not eliminate those needs.

It often just makes companies feel more comfortable ignoring them.

## Experience is built through access

We talk about experience as if it were a personal possession people acquire through sufficient determination.

Build projects. Contribute to open source. Keep learning. Show initiative.

These things can help. They cannot reproduce every part of professional software development.

A personal project rarely teaches you how to negotiate a migration across three teams. A tutorial cannot give you the experience of maintaining a feature for two years after its original assumptions collapse.

You learn those things by participating in real work, with real constraints and people who depend on the result.

That requires access.

Someone has to let you into the room before you already know everything that happens there.

When companies reject applicants for lacking commercial experience while refusing to provide any route to gaining it, they create a locked door with the key on the other side.

Telling candidates to “just keep building” does not resolve that contradiction.

## Juniors are not defective seniors

A junior developer is not a senior developer who needs more supervision.

They occupy a different stage of development, with different expectations.

If you hire someone into their first engineering role and expect them to independently navigate an undocumented system, clarify ambiguous requirements, ship a critical feature, and manage the deployment, you have designed an unfair job.

Then, when they struggle, you call it proof that juniors don’t work out.

A reasonable junior role includes bounded problems, accessible support, and feedback delivered while it can still help.

That might mean fixing a well-understood bug, extending an established feature, improving test coverage around a specific behavior, or pairing on a small production change.

The work should be useful. It should also be appropriately scoped.

Throwing someone into chaos and seeing whether they survive is a poor substitute for an onboarding process.

Some people will survive it. That does not make it good management.

## Mentoring is work. Plan for it.

There’s a particularly frustrating version of junior hiring where leadership celebrates creating opportunities, then quietly transfers the entire cost to senior engineers.

The senior keeps the same deadlines, the same project load, and the same on-call responsibilities.

Now they also mentor two new hires.

When delivery slows, management asks why.

This arrangement is unfair to both people. The senior becomes overloaded. The junior feels guilty for asking questions. Support becomes something squeezed between supposedly more important tasks.

If you want mentoring to happen, allocate time for it.

Reduce other commitments. Make ownership clear. Recognize teaching in performance reviews. Give mentors somewhere to escalate problems they cannot solve alone.

And don’t assume the strongest individual contributor automatically knows how to teach.

Explaining a concept, choosing an appropriate task, and giving useful feedback are skills. Companies need to support their development too.

**You cannot call mentoring valuable while treating every hour spent on it as lost productivity.**

## The benefits reach beyond the new hire

Supporting juniors can expose weaknesses a team has learned to work around.

A new developer asks why the setup instructions fail. Why a simple change requires touching seven services. Why the tests pass locally but fail in CI.

Experienced employees may already know the workarounds. They stop noticing how much knowledge lives outside the documentation.

The new person cannot rely on that history.

Their questions give the team an opportunity to make the system easier to understand and operate.

There are other benefits, too. Explaining a decision forces you to examine it. Breaking work into teachable pieces can improve planning. Sharing knowledge reduces dependence on particular individuals.

None of this happens automatically. A neglected junior will not magically improve your engineering culture.

But a team that invests in helping someone become productive often builds practices that help its next experienced hire as well.

## “They’ll leave once we train them”

They might.

So might the senior developer you paid a recruiter to find.

Employment involves uncertainty. Investing in someone does not purchase permanent ownership of their career.

If people consistently leave as soon as they become more capable, investigate what happens when they grow.

Do their salaries increase? Do their responsibilities and titles reflect their contribution? Can they see a future at the company?

Or do you keep treating them as “the junior” three years after they joined?

A company that offers development without progression should expect people to find progression elsewhere.

You cannot freeze someone’s compensation, deny them opportunities, and then describe their departure as a betrayal of your investment.

Training people creates the possibility that they’ll leave with stronger skills. Refusing to train them creates its own problems.

Choose the risk you can build a healthy company around.

## You don’t need a giant graduate program

Hiring juniors does not require a campus recruiting department or a six-month curriculum.

Start with one role you can properly support.

Choose a mentor who has agreed to the responsibility and has time allocated for it. Prepare a working development environment. Identify a few small, meaningful tasks before the person arrives.

Explain what success looks like during the first month. Schedule regular conversations. Make asking for help an explicit expectation.

Then review the process.

Where did the new hire get stuck? Which explanations were missing? Was the work appropriately scoped? Did the mentor actually have enough time?

Improve those things before expanding.

A modest program that reliably helps one person grow is worth more than a large intake left to figure everything out alone.

## Build the workforce you keep asking for

The title is deliberately provocative. Companies don’t literally earn ownership of senior developers by hiring juniors.

But they should examine what they expect from the labor market and what they contribute to it.

If every hiring plan begins with “five years of experience required,” someone else must provide those first five years.

If every team needs engineers who can mentor, someone must first give people worth mentoring a chance.

The senior engineer you’re searching for was once an inexperienced applicant hoping someone would see their potential.

Someone said yes.

If your company keeps waiting for other people to say yes first, stop acting surprised when experienced talent is hard to find.

Open a role. Make room for learning. Pay someone to grow.
