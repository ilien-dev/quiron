# How I Am Getting Out From Under My Student Debt

For a long time, my student debt lived in the same mental category as a mysterious production bug: definitely there, probably expensive, and something I hoped would become less urgent if I stopped looking at it.

I made payments. I checked balances occasionally. Mostly, I tried to build a career while carrying the uncomfortable feeling that part of my future income had already been spent.

The hardest part wasn’t always the payment itself. It was the background noise. Every career decision seemed to arrive with a second question: “But what about the loans?”

I’m still working through it. This isn’t a victory lap about paying everything off with one weird trick. It’s about the practical changes helping me turn an overwhelming problem into something I can manage.

## First, I stopped treating the balance as a character judgment

There’s a particular shame that can come with student debt. You replay decisions you made before you had much experience making financial decisions at all.

Should I have chosen a different school? Worked more? Borrowed less? Understood the paperwork better?

Some reflection is useful. Endless self-prosecution isn’t.

I can acknowledge that certain decisions were expensive without making “person who made expensive decisions” my entire identity. The money is a problem to solve, not evidence that I’m incapable of solving problems.

That shift sounds small, but it made opening my accounts easier. And opening my accounts was the prerequisite for everything else.

You can’t debug a system you refuse to inspect.

## I built an inventory before building a plan

My first useful step was embarrassingly ordinary: I put everything in one place.

For each loan, I recorded:

- The current balance.
- The interest rate.
- The minimum payment.
- The payment due date.
- The loan type and servicer.

I also wrote down which details I didn’t understand.

Previously, “my student loans” had felt like one enormous, blurry obligation. The inventory turned that blur into individual accounts with specific terms.

As developers, we know the difference between “the application is slow” and “this database query takes four seconds.” Specificity creates options.

I didn’t need an elaborate dashboard. A spreadsheet was enough. In fact, resisting the urge to build a debt-tracking application was probably my first successful cost-saving decision.

The goal was visibility, not another unfinished side project.

## I made a budget that could survive an ordinary Tuesday

My early budgets assumed I would become a completely different person next month.

That person never ordered takeout, never forgot a subscription, and apparently experienced no unexpected expenses.

Actual me kept ruining the spreadsheet.

Eventually, I started budgeting around my real behavior. I looked at recent spending, identified recurring expenses, and gave irregular costs somewhere to land.

A budget that only works when nothing goes wrong is fragile.

I also stopped treating every enjoyable purchase as a moral failure. Leaving room for small pleasures made the overall plan easier to maintain. I wanted a system I could live with for years, not a punishment I could tolerate for eleven days.

The question became: what payment can I repeat consistently without borrowing again to cover the rest of my life?

## I gave the extra payment a clear destination

Once the minimum payments and basic expenses were accounted for, I needed a rule for any extra money.

Without a rule, I kept renegotiating the same decision. Should I save it? Spend it? Split it across every loan so they all looked slightly better?

I chose a target loan and directed additional payments there.

There are different ways to choose that target. Some people prioritize the highest interest rate. Others start with the smallest balance because closing an account helps them stay motivated.

For me, the important change was making the decision deliberately and understanding the tradeoff.

I also checked how my servicer handled additional payments instead of assuming the money would go exactly where I intended.

A payment strategy is only useful if the actual account behavior matches it.

## I stopped making my plan depend on a promotion

Working in tech makes it easy to imagine that the next salary increase will fix everything.

Sometimes higher income changes the equation substantially. But “I’ll deal with this when I earn more” had become a convenient way to postpone decisions I could make today.

I started working on both sides of the problem: managing my current cash flow and improving my earning potential.

On the career side, that meant documenting accomplishments, preparing for compensation conversations, and developing skills connected to the work I wanted to do.

It didn’t mean studying every evening until I became too exhausted to perform my actual job.

My debt plan needed my career to remain sustainable. Burning out in pursuit of faster repayment would undermine the income supporting the whole thing.

## I became more selective about side projects

At one point, almost every hobby looked like a potential revenue stream.

Maybe I could freelance. Build a SaaS. Sell templates. Start a newsletter. Launch a course about something I had learned approximately fifteen minutes earlier.

The possibility of extra income was exciting. The accumulated obligations were less exciting.

Now I ask a few questions before taking on paid work outside my job.

Is the scope clear? Is the compensation worth the time? Will I still have enough energy for my main responsibilities? Am I buying tools before I’ve found a customer?

Sometimes the answer supports taking the project. Sometimes the better decision is sleeping, cooking dinner, and showing up focused tomorrow.

Extra income can help. So can avoiding an expensive distraction disguised as a business opportunity.

## I put friction in the right places

Willpower is unreliable after a long day of debugging.

I made routine payments easier to remember, set calendar reminders for account reviews, and kept the spreadsheet simple enough to update quickly.

For spending decisions, I added a little friction. I canceled subscriptions I wasn’t using and started waiting before buying nonessential things.

None of this required becoming perfectly disciplined.

It required noticing where my defaults were working against me and changing a few of them.

I also kept a buffer for unexpected expenses. Sending every available dollar toward debt looked impressive until an ordinary surprise forced me to reach for credit again.

Progress felt more durable when the plan could absorb a bad week.

## I started measuring more than the remaining balance

Watching the balance decline can be frustratingly slow.

If that number is the only measure of success, plenty of responsible months can feel like failure.

So I track other signs of progress: payments made on time, fewer avoidable fees, a growing cash buffer, and less time spent worrying because I know what happens next.

I still care about the balance. I just don’t ask it to provide all my motivation.

I review the plan periodically and adjust when circumstances change. A repayment plan built around an old salary or an unrealistic expense estimate doesn’t deserve loyalty simply because I made a spreadsheet for it.

## Getting out is becoming a process

I wish I could end this with a screenshot showing a zero balance.

I’m not there yet.

What I have is a clearer picture, a repeatable process, and fewer decisions driven by panic. That has made the debt feel less like an undefined threat hanging over my career.

I’m learning that progress doesn’t always look dramatic enough for a social media announcement. Sometimes it looks like checking a statement, making the planned payment, and getting on with an ordinary day.

For now, that is how I’m getting out from under my student debt: one deliberate decision, followed by another.
