I originally opened Figma because I wanted to design a website. A few rectangles, some text, maybe a button that didn’t look like it belonged in a forgotten admin dashboard.

Then I started using it for thumbnails. Then diagrams. Then presentation slides. At some point, it became the place I opened whenever an idea needed a visual form.

What keeps me coming back is how easily I can move between rough thinking and polished output. I can dump a sentence onto a canvas, put a box around it, and gradually turn it into something worth sharing.

Here are ten ways I use Figma as a creator, including a few that have very little to do with designing interfaces.

## 1. Turning vague ideas into something I can see

Some ideas sound great in my head and fall apart the moment I try to explain them.

Before committing to a project, I use Figma to make a rough visual version. That might be a landing page headline, three screens for an app, or a sketch of how a tutorial could flow.

I keep these deliberately messy. Default fonts, plain rectangles, placeholder copy. Choosing the perfect blue is an excellent way to avoid answering whether an idea makes sense.

The useful questions come quickly:

- What is the main thing someone should notice?
- Does this need three steps or seven?
- Can I explain the value in one screen?

A rough layout often exposes missing thinking faster than another page of notes would.

## 2. Creating reusable social media graphics

Making every social post from scratch used to mean repeating the same decisions: font size, margins, background, where to put the title.

Now I keep a small collection of reusable layouts.

I have versions for short tips, quotes, announcements, and posts that need a screenshot. Each gives me a starting point while leaving room for the content to determine the final design.

The biggest improvement came from testing templates with realistic text. A layout that looks beautiful with “Build better things” might collapse under an actual eight-word headline.

I also leave more space around the edges than seems necessary. Graphics feel very different when viewed inside a busy feed on a small phone.

Templates save time, but they also make publishing feel less like starting a new design project every day.

## 3. Designing blog covers and video thumbnails

A cover image has a specific job: help someone understand what the content is about and give them a reason to look closer.

I use Figma to explore several directions side by side. One might lead with a bold title, another with a screenshot, another with a simple visual metaphor.

Seeing the options together makes weak concepts easier to spot.

My most useful habit is zooming out until the design is tiny. If I can’t read the main phrase or identify the focal point, I simplify it.

I usually remove something before I add something: an extra label, a decorative shape, a second screenshot.

For technical content, I try to show enough detail to communicate the topic without expecting anyone to read an entire code sample from a thumbnail.

## 4. Planning carousels as a complete story

A carousel can look good slide by slide and still feel exhausting to read.

I lay out the whole sequence in Figma so I can judge its rhythm. The opening needs to establish a reason to continue. The middle needs to deliver useful information. The ending needs to feel earned.

I give each slide one main job. If a slide contains a definition, an example, three exceptions, and a takeaway, it probably needs to become two slides.

Viewing everything together also reveals repetition. Sometimes I discover that three consecutive slides make essentially the same point.

Before polishing, I read only the headings in order. If they form a coherent outline, the structure is probably working. If they don’t, typography won’t rescue it.

## 5. Explaining technical concepts with diagrams

When I write about software, I often reach a point where another paragraph makes the explanation harder to follow.

That’s usually my cue to draw something.

I use simple diagrams to show request flows, component relationships, authentication steps, or how data moves through an application.

The shapes are rarely complicated. Boxes, arrows, labels, and a little spacing usually do most of the work.

What matters is deciding what to leave out. A diagram explaining a login flow probably doesn’t need to show every service in the system.

I also try to give arrows meaningful labels. “Sends access token” tells the reader more than an unexplained line.

Drawing these explanations is useful for me, too. If I can’t connect the boxes confidently, I may need to understand the topic better before publishing.

## 6. Building a small visual identity

I don’t need a huge brand document to make my work feel consistent. I do need somewhere to record decisions I’m tired of making repeatedly.

In Figma, I keep a reference page with my usual colors, type choices, spacing examples, and a few finished graphics.

I include examples of combinations that work together, because isolated color swatches don’t tell me much about how a design should feel.

This page helps when I return to a project after several weeks. Instead of trying to remember which gray I used, I can pick up where I left off.

I keep the system flexible. An educational diagram and a playful announcement shouldn’t have to look identical.

The goal is enough consistency that my work feels related, with enough freedom to fit the subject.

## 7. Mocking up landing pages before coding

As a developer, I can convince myself that jumping directly into code will be faster.

Sometimes it is. But when the content and layout are still uncertain, I find it easier to explore them in Figma first.

I sketch the main sections, add realistic copy, and work out what the page asks a visitor to do. This catches problems that CSS cannot solve, like a confusing headline or three competing calls to action.

I also consider a narrow layout early. A desktop composition can hide how much content a page actually contains.

I don’t try to settle every implementation detail in the mockup. I want enough clarity to start building with confidence, while leaving room for what I learn in the browser.

## 8. Making screenshots easier to understand

Screenshots are central to a lot of my content, but a raw capture often includes more information than the reader needs.

I bring screenshots into Figma to crop distractions, add labels, highlight a control, or place two states beside each other.

A small annotation can save a surprising amount of explanation. “Click this setting” becomes much clearer when the setting is visibly marked.

I’m careful to preserve the meaning of what I’m showing. Helpful framing should make the original interface easier to follow.

Before exporting, I check whether the text is readable at the size it will appear in the article. A screenshot that looks sharp on my canvas can become useless when squeezed into a narrow column.

## 9. Creating slides for talks and workshops

Figma works well for the kinds of presentations I make: visual explanations, practical examples, and a few strong ideas.

I start by writing the main point of each slide in plain text. Then I decide which points need a diagram, a screenshot, or simply a larger sentence.

Seeing the entire presentation laid out helps me judge pacing. Too many dense slides in a row usually means I’m asking the audience to read while I’m also asking them to listen.

I reuse layouts for section breaks and examples, which keeps preparation manageable.

I also test the exported presentation before using it. Font sizes, image clarity, and the actual presentation setup matter more than how polished everything looks on my own screen.

## 10. Keeping a visual library of experiments

Some of my most useful Figma files contain work I never published.

I keep alternate covers, abandoned layouts, interesting typography experiments, and screenshots of previous versions of my own work.

When I save inspiration from elsewhere, I include the source and a note about what caught my attention. “Interesting hierarchy” is more useful later than a mysterious screenshot with no context.

This library gives me somewhere to begin when I feel stuck. I can revisit an unfinished direction or notice a pattern in the designs I keep responding to.

It also makes progress visible. Looking back at older work helps me recognize what I’ve learned, even when that improvement felt slow at the time.

Figma has become part of how I think through creative work. I use it to ask questions, compare possibilities, and make explanations easier to follow.

If you already use it for interface design, try bringing one other task into the same canvas: your next article cover, a confusing technical explanation, or the outline of a short presentation.

Start with something you actually need to make. That’s how most of these uses became habits for me.
