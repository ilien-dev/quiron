# Talk Notes: "Self-Care on Rails" (RailsConf 2021)

What counts as a worthwhile side project? Something that attracts users? Teaches you a framework? Makes your GitHub profile look more impressive?

Ben Greenberg’s RailsConf 2021 talk, [“Self-Care on Rails,”](https://www.rubyevents.org/talks/self-care-on-rails) offers another answer: something that makes life a little easier for you and the people you love.

Drawing on his family’s experiences during the pandemic, Ben describes using Ruby and Rails to create moments of connection and reduce everyday stress. It’s a personal talk with a useful invitation: include your own needs among the problems you consider worth solving.

Ben connects this idea to the Ruby community’s tradition of helping others. He references initiatives such as Ruby for Good and raises questions about developers’ responsibility for the effects of their work. Alongside that responsibility, he makes room for self-interest: our families, concerns, and limited energy deserve attention too. [Talk recording and transcript](https://www.rubyevents.org/talks/self-care-on-rails).

That framing makes these examples interesting. They offer a different way to judge a project’s success. A tool can be valuable because it helps a few people through a difficult day. The benefit doesn’t need to extend beyond those people to justify the effort.

One example is a birthday-party application. With ordinary celebrations disrupted, Ben built a way for his son and friends to watch a movie together and communicate remotely. The app used WebRTC, with an external video API helping manage the complexity of real-time communication. [Birthday app discussion](https://www.rubyevents.org/talks/self-care-on-rails).

The useful lesson here is about choosing where to spend your effort. When the goal is helping people spend time together, using an existing service can be a sensible engineering decision. You can focus on the experience you want to create.

It also gives the project a clear definition of success. Did the children get to celebrate together? Did the experience bring some joy? Those are concrete outcomes, even if they never appear on a product dashboard.

Ben’s second example addresses a less festive problem: repetitive school paperwork. When schools required daily health declarations, he created a Ruby gem to automate the submission process through web scraping and headless browsing, with an SMS confirmation afterward. [School declaration example](https://www.rubyevents.org/talks/self-care-on-rails).

A recurring administrative task can occupy more attention than its duration suggests. There’s the work itself, remembering to do it, and worrying that you’ve forgotten. Reducing that burden can have value beyond the minutes saved.

My takeaway is to pay closer attention to those small sources of friction. We routinely notice awkward workflows in applications we maintain. It’s worth bringing some of that curiosity to our own routines.

A few prompts I’d carry into a personal project:

- What task keeps interrupting an already busy day?
- Who would benefit if it became easier?
- What existing tools could help?
- What is the smallest useful result?
- Will maintaining the solution require more energy than it saves?

That last question matters. A personal tool should fit the life it’s meant to improve. Sometimes a short script is enough. Sometimes a shared calendar or an existing app will do the job.

I also wouldn’t turn this talk into another obligation to build things after work. The invitation is to recognize your needs and choose how to meet them. Coding can be one option.

What stays with me is the scale of the examples: a birthday celebration, a morning routine, a family getting through a hard year. These are meaningful places to apply technical skills.

A project that makes tomorrow morning calmer already has someone to serve.
