# How to Find Consulting Clients

Being good at your work and finding people willing to pay for it are different skills.

That gap catches a lot of developers by surprise. You can debug a production incident, design an API, or untangle a legacy codebase, yet still feel awkward answering a simpler question: “Where will my next client come from?”

Finding consulting clients becomes easier when you stop treating it as a search for anyone who needs a developer. Your job is to identify a specific problem, find people who care about solving it, and make hiring you feel like a sensible next step.

Here’s how to build that process.

## Start with a problem people recognize

“I offer software consulting” gives a potential client too much work to do. They have to figure out what you handle, whether their problem fits, and why they should choose you.

A clearer offer connects your skills to a situation:

- “I help SaaS teams reduce slow PostgreSQL queries.”
- “I help agencies rescue delayed React projects.”
- “I automate manual reporting for operations teams.”

You don’t need to commit to one specialty forever. You need a useful starting point for conversations.

Choose a problem you can solve confidently, for people you can realistically reach. Experience helps here: recurring frustrations from previous jobs often make better consulting offers than technologies you recently learned.

## Look for buying situations

A company using your favorite framework isn’t automatically a prospect. A company struggling with a problem you understand might be.

Look for situations that create urgency: a launch slipping behind schedule, a migration without a clear owner, rising infrastructure costs, or a critical integration that keeps breaking.

These situations give your outreach context. You can explain why you’re contacting someone and ask a relevant question.

Be careful with assumptions. A public job listing or technical discussion may suggest a need, but it doesn’t prove the company wants a consultant. Treat it as a reason to investigate, rather than an invitation to pitch aggressively.

## Begin with people who know your work

Your first useful conversations may come from former coworkers, managers, agency partners, or people you’ve helped in developer communities.

Send a short, specific message:

> Hey Sam, I’m taking on consulting work helping small SaaS teams troubleshoot slow Rails applications. If you hear of a team dealing with performance issues, I’d appreciate an introduction. Happy to send a short description you can forward.

This makes the request easy to understand and pass along.

Avoid asking your entire network whether they “know anyone who needs development.” Give people a recognizable situation to listen for. Someone might forget your availability but remember you when a colleague complains about a painfully slow dashboard.

## Build proof around the work you want

Potential clients need evidence that you can handle their problem. A polished website can help, but useful proof matters more than elaborate branding.

Create a small collection of relevant examples:

- A case study explaining a problem, your approach, and the result.
- A technical article showing how you diagnose a common issue.
- A demo that illustrates a practical improvement.

If you don’t have client projects yet, use personal work or clearly labeled demonstrations. Never imply that a sample project produced business results for a real customer.

When discussing previous employment, respect confidentiality and get permission before sharing sensitive details. You can often explain your reasoning without exposing proprietary code or identifying the company.

## Publish answers to client questions

Writing can help people discover your expertise, especially when your articles address problems they already face.

For developers, that might mean publishing on DEV, your own site, or a community relevant to your specialty.

Consider topics such as:

- “How to investigate a background job queue that keeps growing.”
- “What to check before rewriting a slow application.”
- “How to scope a third-party API integration.”

Explain the symptoms, tradeoffs, and decisions involved. Include enough technical detail to demonstrate judgment.

Finish with a clear description of what you help with and how to contact you. Readers shouldn’t have to investigate whether you’re available for consulting.

## Send thoughtful, targeted outreach

Cold outreach works best as a small research exercise.

Identify a company, find a plausible reason your expertise might matter, and contact someone connected to that problem. Keep the message brief:

> Hi Maya, I saw your team is expanding its partner integrations. I help SaaS companies build and stabilize API integrations, including retries and failure recovery. Is integration reliability something your team is working on this quarter?

The goal is to start a conversation. You don’t need to attach a proposal, request an hour-long meeting, or explain your entire career.

Avoid manufactured urgency and exaggerated claims. If you can’t explain why the message is relevant to that recipient, improve your research before sending it.

## Make the first engagement easy to understand

A prospect may trust your expertise while remaining uncertain about scope, cost, or how you work.

Offer a defined first engagement when it fits the problem. Examples include a performance assessment, an integration planning workshop, or a short investigation into recurring production failures.

Explain what you’ll examine, what access you’ll need, what you’ll deliver, and what falls outside the engagement.

The deliverable should help the client make progress even if they don’t hire you again. A diagnostic engagement has value when it produces useful findings and recommendations; it shouldn’t exist merely to unlock a larger sales pitch.

## Ask questions before proposing work

When someone shows interest, resist jumping straight into implementation.

Ask what’s happening, who it affects, what they’ve tried, and why they want to address it now. Understand how they’ll judge success and who approves the spending.

A request to “rewrite the backend” might turn out to be a reporting problem, a deployment problem, or a handful of expensive queries.

Good discovery helps you recommend appropriate work. It also gives you a chance to recognize poor fits before either side commits time and money.

## Follow up and track what happens

People get busy. A missed reply doesn’t always mean rejection.

Follow up politely, adding useful context when possible. After a reasonable number of attempts, close the loop and move on.

Keep a simple record of contacts, conversations, proposals, and outcomes. Note where each opportunity came from.

Review that information regularly. If introductions lead to useful calls while broad outreach gets little response, put more effort into relationships. If calls rarely become projects, examine your qualification process, offer, and proposals.

Finding clients takes repeated effort, but it doesn’t require constant self-promotion. Start with a clear problem, show that you can solve it, and make relevant connections consistently. Then use what you learn from those conversations to improve the next one.
