Streaming looks simple until you try to do it yourself. You open your editor, hit “Go Live,” and discover that your microphone is picking up your keyboard better than your voice.

My goal is a setup that makes live coding comfortable for me and easy to follow for viewers. I want readable code, clear audio, and as few things to manage as possible while I’m debugging in public.

Here’s how I put it together.

## The desk: two screens, separate jobs

My main monitor is where the work happens: editor, terminal, browser, and whatever bug has decided to become today’s content.

The second monitor holds chat, streaming controls, and a preview of the broadcast. Keeping those separate helps me check the stream without constantly switching away from the code.

Before going live, I increase my editor and terminal font sizes. What feels slightly oversized at my desk usually looks much better in a small video player.

I also close unrelated applications and disable notifications. Nobody needs a surprise calendar reminder appearing halfway through a function.

## Audio gets the most attention

For a coding stream, I prioritize making my voice easy to understand. Viewers spend a lot of time listening while looking at fairly static text.

My microphone sits close enough that I can speak naturally without turning the gain way up. I keep it off to the side so it doesn’t block my view or sit directly in front of my mouth.

I use a small amount of noise suppression and gentle compression. The goal is consistent volume without making my voice sound heavily processed.

My pre-stream audio check is wonderfully boring:

- Record a short sample.
- Talk at a normal volume.
- Type for a few seconds.
- Listen back through headphones.

That catches more problems than staring at an audio meter.

## OBS: three scenes are enough

I use OBS Studio with three scenes:

1. **Starting soon:** a place for people to arrive while I finish getting ready.
2. **Coding:** the main workspace, with a small webcam overlay.
3. **Be right back:** a quick switch for breaks or unexpected interruptions.

The coding scene stays simple. The code takes up most of the screen, and the webcam sits somewhere that won’t cover useful content.

I prefer capturing a specific application window when practical. Before sharing a terminal or browser, I check for credentials, private tabs, and anything else that shouldn’t be on stream.

My favorite overlay feature is empty space. Every extra widget competes with the thing I’m trying to explain.

## Lighting and camera placement

My webcam is roughly at eye level, with a soft light in front of me and slightly to one side.

I don’t spend much time chasing a perfect background. I mainly want my face to be visible and the lighting to stay consistent as the stream goes on.

Camera placement also matters for comfort. If checking chat means turning completely away from the camera, a small adjustment to the monitor layout helps.

## Making live coding watchable

The most useful part of my setup is a short note beside my keyboard:

**What am I doing, and why?**

It reminds me to explain the next step before disappearing into silent concentration.

I start each stream with a small objective: build one component, investigate one bug, or explore one unfamiliar tool. That gives viewers enough context to follow along, even when the implementation gets messy.

I also keep a scratchpad for questions and tangents. I can acknowledge an interesting suggestion without immediately abandoning the current task.

## The setup keeps evolving

After each stream, I watch a few minutes of the recording and look for one thing to improve. Maybe the terminal text was too small, or a scene transition felt awkward.

Small changes are easier to evaluate than rebuilding everything at once.

My setup is ready when I can sit down, run a quick check, and focus on coding with the people who showed up.
