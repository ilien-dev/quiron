# How to Find Mentors, Grow a Network, and Make Friends

Early in my career, I thought "networking" meant standing awkwardly at a meetup with a lukewarm drink, handing business cards to strangers, and hoping one of them would offer me a job. I hated it, so I avoided it. For years I kept my head down, wrote code, and assumed good work would speak for itself.

It mostly didn't. Good work matters, but it travels through people. The developers who grew fastest around me weren't always the most talented. They were the ones who had someone to ask when they were stuck, someone to vouch for them when a role opened up, and someone to grab a coffee with after a terrible deploy.

What I eventually learned is that mentors, networks, and friendships aren't three different projects. They're the same habit with different levels of depth: **showing up, being useful, and staying in touch.** Here's how to build that habit, even if you're introverted, remote, or just starting out.

---

## Part 1: Rethink What a Mentor Is

The biggest mistake people make with mentorship is treating it like a formal role you have to apply for. You find someone impressive, send them a message saying "Will you be my mentor?", and wait. Usually you get silence, or a polite "I'm too busy right now."

That's not because people don't want to help. It's because "be my mentor" is a vague, open-ended commitment. Nobody knows what they're signing up for.

### Ask for small, specific things

Instead of asking for mentorship, ask for advice on one concrete problem:

- "I'm deciding between learning Go and Rust for backend work. You've used both. Which would you pick if you were starting today?"
- "I saw your talk on database migrations. We're about to do our first zero-downtime migration. Is there anything you wish you'd known beforehand?"
- "Could I get 15 minutes of your feedback on my portfolio? I'm applying to frontend roles and I'm not sure what to cut."

Small asks are easy to say yes to. And if the conversation goes well, you follow up. You report back on what you did with their advice. You ask another question a few weeks later. Six months on, you realize this person *is* your mentor. Neither of you ever had to say the word.

### Have many mentors, not one

You don't need a single wise guru who knows everything. It's healthier to have a loose "board of advisors":

- Someone a year or two ahead of you who remembers what your stage feels like
- Someone senior who can see the bigger picture of your career
- Someone in a different specialty who challenges your assumptions
- A peer who's figuring it out alongside you

Each one gives you a different kind of perspective, and no single person carries the burden of your entire development.

### Look closer than you think

The best mentors are often not famous developers with 100k followers. They're the senior engineer on the team next to yours, the maintainer of a small library you use, or the person who always gives thoughtful answers in your company's Slack. They're accessible, they understand your context, and they're far more likely to have time for you.

## Part 2: Grow a Network by Giving First

"Network" sounds transactional, but a good network is just a collection of people who know what you're about and think well of you. The fastest way to build one is to be useful before you need anything.

### Contribute in public

Public work does your networking for you while you sleep. A few ideas, in increasing order of effort:

1. **Answer questions.** Spend 20 minutes a week on a forum, Discord server, or Stack Overflow tag you know something about. You'll be surprised how quickly people start recognizing your name.
2. **Write about what you learn.** Not "expert" content, just honest notes. "Here's the bug that cost me two days and how I fixed it" is genuinely helpful, and it's exactly the kind of post that gets shared. (Hi, dev.to.)
3. **Contribute to open source.** Start with docs, typo fixes, or reproducing bug reports. Maintainers remember the people who make their lives easier.
4. **Give a talk.** Local meetups are almost always looking for speakers, and a 10-minute lightning talk is a great first step. After you speak, people come to *you*, which is a huge relief if you find approaching strangers hard.

### Go where your people are

Every niche in tech has its gathering places. Find two or three and become a regular rather than spreading yourself thin across twenty.

- **Local meetups** for your language or framework
- **Conferences**, especially the hallway track, where the real conversations happen
- **Online communities**: Discord servers, Slack groups, Mastodon or Bluesky circles, subreddits
- **Study groups or bootcamp alumni networks**
- **Company guilds or internal interest groups** if you work somewhere large

Consistency matters more than volume. Showing up to the same monthly meetup six times builds more relationships than attending six different events once.

### Make it easy for people to remember you

When you meet someone, have a simple way to describe what you're into: "I'm a backend dev getting into observability" is more memorable than "I do software." Specificity gives people a hook. Later, when they see a job posting or an article about observability, they'll think of you.

### Introduce people to each other

One of the most underrated networking moves is connecting two people who should know each other. "You're both working on accessibility tooling, you should talk" costs you nothing and creates goodwill on both sides. People who connect others become hubs, and hubs hear about everything.

## Part 3: Turn Contacts Into Friends

A network gets you opportunities. Friends get you through the hard parts. The leap from one to the other is mostly about repetition and a little vulnerability.

### Follow up, then follow up again

Most potential friendships die after the first conversation because nobody follows up. Be the person who does:

- Send a message the day after: "Great chatting about your side project, here's that article I mentioned."
- Comment on their posts or share their work.
- Invite them to something: a co-working session, a game night, a virtual pairing session.

It can feel forward, but almost everyone is quietly relieved when someone else takes the initiative.

### Do things together, not just talk

Friendships form through shared experiences. Pair program on a side project. Enter a hackathon as a team. Start a small book club around a technical book. Build something silly together. Working side by side creates the kind of bond that a dozen coffee chats can't.

### Share the real stuff

The developers I'm closest to are the ones I've been honest with about impostor syndrome, a layoff, a burnout spell, or a project that went sideways. You don't have to overshare with strangers, but as trust grows, letting people see the messy parts invites them to do the same. That's where friendship actually starts.

### Show up when it matters

Congratulate people on new jobs. Check in when you hear they were laid off. Offer to review their resume or introduce them to someone hiring. These moments are remembered for years.

## A Note for Introverts and Remote Developers

If all of this sounds exhausting, you're not alone. A few adjustments help a lot:

- **Favor async and written channels.** Blog posts, thoughtful forum replies, and GitHub discussions let you connect at your own pace.
- **Prefer small over big.** A five-person Discord voice chat is easier than a 300-person conference.
- **Give yourself a role.** Volunteering at an event or organizing a meetup gives you a built-in reason to talk to people.
- **Set a tiny, repeatable goal.** "One meaningful message to someone in my field per week" adds up to 52 relationships nudged forward per year.

You don't need to become a social butterfly. You just need to be consistent.

---

## Putting It Into Practice

Here's a simple plan for the next 30 days:

1. **Week 1:** Pick two communities (one local or live, one online) and join them.
2. **Week 2:** Answer three questions or leave three genuinely helpful comments.
3. **Week 3:** Ask one person a specific, small question about something they know well.
4. **Week 4:** Follow up with someone you've interacted with, and invite them to do something together.

Then repeat. That's really all it is.

---

## Final Thoughts

Mentors, networks, and friendships aren't things you *get*. They're things you *grow*, slowly, through small acts of generosity and consistency. The good news is that the bar is low: most people never follow up, never share what they've learned, and never ask for help. If you do even a little of each, you'll stand out.

And someday soon, someone will message you with a small, specific question. When they do, answer it. That's how the cycle continues.
