Every year, a certain question shows up in developer communities: "Has anyone else felt like the only woman in the room?" Within an hour the replies fill with people saying yes. SheCoded 2021 started from that conversation, but it went further. Instead of only naming the problem, it put women builders on stage to describe what they had done, and it invited allies to describe what they could do differently.

This recap covers the themes that ran through the event, for developers who couldn't attend and for anyone who wants to be a better colleague to the women on their team.

## Stories that stuck

The strongest sessions started with a specific moment rather than a general principle. A backend engineer joined a team where architecture decisions were made in a group chat she had never been added to. Her fix was modest. In each standup she asked, "Where does this decision get made, and can I see the thread?" Within a month, decisions were moving into shared channels for everyone.

A frontend developer described being the team's "accessibility person" and "documentation person" at the same time. Unpaid, invisible maintenance work tends to land on the same people, she said, and over a few years it quietly shapes promotion timelines. Her suggestion was to put maintenance tasks on the same board as feature work so the time they take becomes visible and can be assigned fairly.

A mobile engineer returning from a career break described résumé gaps treated as signals of lower commitment, and interviewers asking questions that were technically indirect but clearly not neutral. She didn't pretend the bias was imaginary. She prepared a short, factual account of her recent projects, then worked with her manager to write a clearer re-entry process for the company.

What these stories shared was specificity. Each speaker named a situation, a decision, and a result. None of them asked the audience to feel guilty. They asked the audience to notice.

## What allies actually did

The ally track was the most useful part, because it avoided the vague advice that usually closes these talks ("be supportive!"). Panelists offered concrete behaviors instead.

**Share credit in writing.** When a woman on your team solves a hard bug, mention it in a channel leadership reads, and name the specific contribution. Verbal praise in a meeting tends to disappear. Written credit tends to show up in reviews.

**Interrupt the pattern, not the person.** When a colleague is talked over, a simple "I'd like to hear the end of what you were saying" does more than a long speech later. Several panelists said this changed how the whole room listened.

**Check who's missing.** If you can add people to a channel, project, or planning session, ask whether the people affected by a decision were part of making it.

**Sponsor, don't just mentor.** Mentorship matters, but sponsorship, meaning advocating for someone's promotion or stretch assignment when they aren't in the room, is where opportunities actually move. Allies with influence were encouraged to use it deliberately.

**Ask before you assume.** Several speakers asked allies not to make them the spokesperson for all women. "Do you want to weigh in on this, or would you rather I handle it?" respects that.

## Community, not just conference talks

I appreciated the emphasis on continuity. A single event can inspire people, but working relationships are built over months. Attendees described small recurring practices: a monthly code review group for junior developers, a shared list of job postings with salary ranges, and a buddy system for people starting a role in a new country or language.

Open source came up often. Contributors who had been discouraged from reviewing pull requests described getting started by documenting something they had just learned. Maintainers said they now write "good first issue" tickets with clear context, because a vague ticket is a barrier for everyone.

## Takeaways for developers

1. **Make invisible work visible.** Put documentation, review, onboarding, and accessibility on the board alongside features.
2. **Write down decisions.** Move important conversations out of private chats and into threads people can find.
3. **Credit specifically and in writing.** A named contribution in a place leadership reads beats generic praise.
4. **Ask what support looks like for each person.** The answers will differ, and that's the point.
5. **Keep going after the event.** A conference is a starting line. The habits and relationships are the real work.

## Closing thoughts

SheCoded 2021 didn't solve the problems it discussed, and no single event could. What it did well was make experiences concrete and next steps small enough to try on a Monday morning. If you were there, pick one thing to change. If you weren't, pick one from the list above and start this week.

The field gets better when more people who build software take responsibility for who gets heard, who gets credit, and who gets a seat at the table. Women have been doing that work for a long time. Allies who join them are how it becomes the norm.
