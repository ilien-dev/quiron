Few figures in software are as influential, or as divisive, as Richard Stallman. He launched the GNU Project, wrote the GPL, and gave the free software movement its philosophical backbone. He is also at the center of a long-running argument about who should lead the institutions that carry that movement forward. The call to remove him from the Free Software Foundation (FSF) board is the clearest example of that argument, and it is worth understanding whichever side you land on.

## How we got here

In September 2019, Stallman resigned as FSF president and from his visiting position at MIT. The trigger was a message on an MIT mailing list in which he made remarks about the allegations surrounding Marvin Minsky and Jeffrey Epstein's victims. Many readers took the remarks as minimizing sexual abuse. Stallman had a history of controversial statements, and this one became the breaking point.

In March 2021, he returned to the FSF board of directors, announcing it himself during a LibrePlanet talk. The reaction was immediate. An open letter calling for his removal, and for the removal of the entire FSF board, spread quickly and gathered thousands of signatures. A counter-letter defending Stallman and criticizing the pressure campaign gathered thousands more.

Organizations took positions too. Red Hat said it was suspending funding to the FSF and participation in its events. The GNOME Foundation, the Tor Project, and the EFF publicly distanced themselves from the decision. Other projects and individual maintainers announced they would reconsider their relationship with the FSF.

## The case for removal

Supporters of the call make three main arguments.

**Leadership sets the tone.** A board member is more than an individual with opinions. They represent the organization in public and shape who feels welcome. If the FSF wants a broad, global community of contributors, people who feel alienated by its figurehead are people it will not reach.

**The pattern matters.** The criticism is not about one email. Critics point to years of public statements they consider dismissive or hurtful, and argue that the 2019 resignation was the right outcome that the 2021 return reversed without any visible accountability.

**Free software needs institutional credibility.** The FSF stewards the GPL family of licenses and campaigns on issues like user freedom and software patents. Critics say that mission is weakened when the organization's reputation is tied to controversy that distracts from the work.

## The case against removal

The other side is not monolithic, and its arguments deserve a fair hearing.

**Founder legacy.** Defenders argue that Stallman's role in creating the movement is unique, and that the FSF's identity is inseparable from his ideas. Removing him, in this view, severs the organization from its own origins.

**Process concerns.** Some signatories of the counter-letter objected less to Stallman's statements than to the way the campaign was run. They saw an online pressure campaign aimed at forcing out an entire board as a bad precedent for how nonprofits make decisions.

**Separating ideas from the person.** Others make a distinction: the philosophy of software freedom stands on its own merits, and criticism of the person should not become criticism of the principles. Several people who disagree with Stallman personally still defend the four freedoms vigorously.

## What this means for developers

You might wonder why a governance dispute should matter if you just write code. There are practical reasons.

- **Licensing.** The FSF holds copyright on a significant amount of GNU software and authors the GPL. Its stability and credibility affect the ecosystem around those licenses.
- **Funding and sponsorship.** When companies pull support, the effects ripple into events, infrastructure, and advocacy work that many projects rely on.
- **Community norms.** How open source organizations handle conduct and leadership becomes a template other projects borrow, for better or worse.
- **Your own projects.** If you maintain or contribute to a project, the same questions arise at smaller scale: what behavior from leaders is acceptable, and what does your project do when a respected founder becomes a liability?

## My take

I think the strongest version of this debate is not "cancel or defend Stallman." It is the question of what a nonprofit owes its community. An organization built on the idea that users deserve freedom has to ask whether its governance makes that freedom accessible to everyone, or only to people comfortable with its leadership.

Regardless of where you stand, a few lessons are worth taking away:

1. Organizations should have clear, public processes for conduct and leadership decisions, so outcomes do not hinge on whoever organizes the loudest campaign.
2. Founders deserve credit for what they built, but credit is not the same as permanent authority.
3. Communities can disagree sharply and still keep the work itself going. The GNU toolchain, the GPL, and countless free software projects continue regardless of the board's composition.
