# I'm building a new home outside of DEV. Here's why (and where to find me!)

There’s a particular kind of energy that comes from publishing something on DEV.

You hit the button, close the tab, reopen it five minutes later, and wonder whether anyone else has run into the same strange bug, questioned the same tool, or learned the same lesson the hard way.

Sometimes a comment turns a rough idea into a better one. Sometimes someone says, “This helped me,” and suddenly the hours spent untangling an explanation feel worth it.

That’s something I appreciate about writing here.

But I’ve been thinking about what I want my writing to become, and where I want it to live.

So I’m building a home outside of DEV: **my own website.**

## Why build my own space?

The short answer is that I want a place that can grow alongside me.

A developer’s work rarely fits neatly into a feed of published articles. There are projects, experiments, unfinished ideas, useful snippets, decisions that seemed reasonable at the time, and lessons that only make sense months later.

I want somewhere to connect those pieces.

An article about a technical problem could sit beside the project that produced it. A project page could link to the decisions behind it. An older post could carry an update explaining what changed after I learned more.

That connected history matters to me. I want the site to show how my thinking develops, including the awkward middle stages.

## I want more control over the reading experience

Publishing platforms make a lot of decisions for you, which is part of their appeal.

You can focus on writing while someone else handles the layout, hosting, discovery, and the many details that make publishing possible.

I’m grateful for that convenience. It also means accepting a structure designed for a broad community.

On my own site, I can make smaller, more personal choices.

How should code examples look? What belongs next to an article? How can someone find related posts without digging through a chronological archive? What should happen when a tutorial becomes outdated?

I’ll probably get some of those choices wrong initially.

Still, I like the idea of treating the reading experience as something I can improve over time, with the same care I put into the words.

## I want my writing to have a permanent address

There’s also a practical reason behind this move: I want a stable place to point people.

When someone asks what I’m working on, I’d like to share one address that brings together my writing, projects, and ways to get in touch.

That address should remain useful even if my publishing habits change.

Maybe I’ll write long tutorials for a while. Maybe I’ll spend a month documenting a small project. Maybe the most useful thing I publish one week will be a brief explanation of an error message.

Having my own domain gives those different kinds of work a shared home.

It also makes me think more carefully about maintenance. Links, corrections, backups, and migrations become responsibilities I can plan for directly.

## I’m making room for smaller ideas

One thing I want to explore is publishing work that feels too small for a full article.

You know the kind:

- A command that saved an afternoon.
- A debugging clue that finally made things click.
- A design decision with a surprisingly annoying tradeoff.
- A question I haven’t answered yet.

I often learn through fragments like these. Writing them down helps me understand them, and sharing them might save someone else a little time.

A personal site gives me room to experiment with formats and organize them in a way that makes sense.

Some ideas will become longer posts later. Others will stay short because they already say everything they need to say.

I’d like to get more comfortable with both.

## Building the site is part of the learning

Of course, there’s a very developer-shaped trap hiding in this announcement.

I could spend weeks choosing a framework, comparing hosting options, adjusting spacing, and convincing myself that publishing must wait until the typography feels exactly right.

I’m trying to recognize that temptation early.

The first version needs to do a few things well: load quickly, read comfortably, work on different screens, and help people find what they came for.

Everything else can earn its place later.

That constraint is useful. It forces me to ask whether a feature helps a reader or simply gives me another interesting problem to work on.

I expect the site itself will generate things worth writing about, especially when straightforward decisions turn out to have consequences I didn’t anticipate.

## What does this mean for DEV?

I still see value in being here.

DEV offers something a personal website has to build gradually: opportunities to meet people through shared interests and conversations.

I want to keep participating in those conversations and sharing work here when it fits.

My website will become the central place for my writing and project updates. DEV can remain one of the places where those ideas meet a wider community.

I’m leaving room to figure out the publishing rhythm as I go.

I’d rather make a sustainable habit than announce an ambitious schedule that becomes another source of pressure.
