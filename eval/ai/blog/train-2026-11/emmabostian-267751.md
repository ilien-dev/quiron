# DISCUSS: Technical Interview Horror Stories

You join the video call five minutes early. Your microphone works. Your editor is ready. You’ve spent the weekend reviewing data structures you haven’t touched since your last interview.

The interviewer arrives twelve minutes late.

“Let’s start with something easy,” they say, opening a problem that appears to require a minor breakthrough in computer science.

Welcome to the technical interview.

For an industry that spends so much time improving systems, we’ve accepted some remarkably buggy hiring processes. Most developers have a story about an interview that went sideways—and sometimes the code was the least uncomfortable part.

Let’s talk about those stories.

## The “small” take-home assignment

The email sounds reasonable: “This should take two to three hours.”

The requirements include authentication, a database, tests, deployment, responsive design, and “a little polish.” There’s also an optional dashboard, which you suspect is optional in the same way wearing shoes to an interview is optional.

You spend your Saturday building it. Then part of Sunday. You write a README explaining your decisions and the shortcuts you took.

Three days later, the rejection arrives:

“We were hoping for something more complete.”

A take-home can be a useful way to show how someone works. But candidates need a realistic scope, clear evaluation criteria, and a time limit the reviewers actually respect.

What’s the biggest gap you’ve seen between an assignment’s estimated time and its actual scope?

## The interviewer who needs to win

You suggest a solution.

“That won’t scale.”

You ask about expected traffic.

“Assume millions of users.”

You discuss caching.

“Assume you can’t use a cache.”

You propose a queue.

“We don’t want that complexity.”

Eventually, you’re designing a globally distributed service with no infrastructure, no budget, and one increasingly tired developer.

Constraints can make a system design discussion interesting. The trouble starts when every answer introduces a new, previously undisclosed constraint. You spend the interview guessing what the interviewer has in mind instead of explaining your reasoning.

Has anyone ever reached the end of one of these conversations and found out what the interviewer actually wanted?

## The live-coding environment from another dimension

Your usual tools are unavailable. The browser editor has unfamiliar shortcuts. Autocomplete is disabled. Something keeps eating your indentation.

Then comes the instruction:

“Please keep talking while you code.”

Fair enough—until you discover that explaining a thought immediately invites an interruption, while thinking silently invites a reminder to explain your thoughts.

You mistype a method name you’ve used for years. The interviewer makes a note.

There’s value in working through a problem together. Still, a broken editor and a running countdown can turn a coding exercise into a test of how gracefully someone handles a deeply awkward video call.

## The accidental warning sign

Sometimes the most revealing moment happens after the technical questions.

“How does the team handle production incidents?”

“We’re like a family. Everyone jumps in, whatever the hour.”

“Why is this position open?”

“The last developer couldn’t handle the pace.”

“What would success look like in the first three months?”

“We need someone to come in and fix everything.”

You close the laptop with a rejection email already forming in your own head.

An interview gives candidates information, too. Occasionally, an unpleasant conversation saves you from a much longer unpleasant experience.

## Your turn

What’s your technical interview horror story?

Was it an impossible whiteboard problem? A take-home project with surprise requirements? An interviewer who argued with a working solution? A panel that clearly hadn’t read your résumé?

And what happened afterward—did you withdraw, get rejected, or somehow end up accepting the offer?

Share as much context as you’re comfortable with, but leave out names and identifying details. I’m especially curious about what you learned: a question you now ask, a boundary you set, or a warning sign you wish you’d noticed sooner.

Bonus points for a story about an interview that started terribly and somehow turned around.
