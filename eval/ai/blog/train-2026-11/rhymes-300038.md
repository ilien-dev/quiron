Let's get one thing out of the way: no one can list *all* known Zoom issues. Zoom is a client on four operating systems, a media network, a web platform, and a SaaS company, and each layer has its own bug tracker. What I can do is group the problems into a handful of categories, explain the mechanism behind each, and point to the lessons. That's what this post is.

Details here are from public reporting and security research as I remember them. Check the primary sources before you cite anything in a compliance document.

## 1. Security vulnerabilities in the client

Zoom's client is a native app that has to do awkward things: launch from a browser link, access your camera, install itself with little friction, and update silently. Many of its most notable bugs come from that design pressure.

**The local web server (2019).** Researcher Jonathan Leitschuh showed that the Mac client installed a small local HTTP server. The goal was convenience: clicking a meeting link in a browser could launch Zoom without a confirmation prompt. The side effect was that any website could send requests to `localhost` and make Zoom open, join a meeting, and turn on your camera. Even after you uninstalled the app, the server could remain and reinstall it.

The mechanism is a classic one. Browsers block cross-origin *reads*, but they will happily *send* requests to localhost, and a local server that treats "request arrived" as "user consented" has no real authorization. Zoom removed the server after the disclosure, and Apple pushed a silent update to remove it from Macs that still had it.

**UNC path injection (2020).** On Windows, the in-meeting chat turned text that looked like a network path (`\\attacker.example\share`) into a clickable link. Clicking it made Windows try to authenticate over SMB, sending the user's NTLM credential hash to the remote host. The hash can then be cracked offline or relayed. The root cause was input handling: the app auto-linkified something that Windows treats as an authenticated resource. Zoom fixed it by not converting such paths into links.

**Installer and updater privilege escalation.** Security researcher Patrick Wardle has presented several macOS issues, including flaws in the installer and the auto-updater. The shared pattern: a privileged helper process accepts input, and the check on that input is weak, such as a signature check that can be bypassed or a race between check and use (a TOCTOU bug). A local, unprivileged user can become root. These need local access, so they are lower in severity than remote bugs, but they matter on shared machines and in post-compromise scenarios.

**The takeaway:** anything that runs privileged and takes input from outside the trust boundary is the dangerous part. That's true for Zoom and for every auto-updater.

## 2. "Zoombombing" and meeting access control

Zoombombing was not a software exploit. It was a product-default problem.

During the early-2020 surge, meeting IDs were short numeric values (nine to eleven digits). Many people posted invite links publicly, and many meetings had no password, no waiting room, and screen sharing open to all participants. Attackers and pranksters could guess or find IDs and join. Some tools even automated scanning of the ID space.

The fixes were mostly defaults:

- Passcodes required by default
- Waiting rooms on by default
- Host-only screen sharing as the default
- Longer, less guessable IDs for some meeting types
- Easier controls to remove participants and lock meetings

This is a useful example of a **secure-by-default failure**. The features that would have prevented the abuse already existed. They were opt-in, and most users never opted in.

## 3. The encryption claims

This is the issue with the most technical nuance, so I'll slow down.

In 2020, Zoom's marketing described its meetings as "end-to-end encrypted." Researchers, including journalists at The Intercept and the Citizen Lab at the University of Toronto, pointed out that this was not accurate in the usual sense. Zoom used transport encryption between clients and its servers, and the servers could in principle access the plaintext media. In end-to-end encryption (E2EE), only the participants hold the keys and the server just relays ciphertext.

Citizen Lab also found other details worth noting:

- Meeting audio and video used **AES-128 in ECB mode**. ECB encrypts identical plaintext blocks into identical ciphertext blocks, which leaks patterns. It is generally considered a poor choice for media or structured data.
- Some encryption keys were generated by Zoom servers, and in some cases those keys were distributed through servers located in China, even for meetings with no participants there.

The US Federal Trade Commission later brought a case over the company's security claims, and Zoom agreed to a settlement requiring a security program and restrictions on misrepresentations. Zoom also acquired Keybase, built a real E2EE option for meetings, and moved toward AES-256-GCM, an authenticated mode that also detects tampering.

Real E2EE comes with trade-offs, which is why it is optional rather than universal:

- Cloud recording and server-side transcription are limited when the server can't read the content.
- Dial-in phone participants can't join an E2EE meeting.
- Features that rely on server-side processing are restricted or disabled.

**The takeaway:** "encrypted" is not a single property. Ask *between which parties*, *with whose keys*, and *in which mode*.

## 4. Privacy and data handling

Several separate stories sit under this heading:

- **Data sent to third parties.** In 2020, reporting showed the iOS app sharing analytics data with Facebook through the Facebook SDK, even for users without a Facebook account. Zoom removed the SDK integration. This is an SDK supply-chain issue: you inherit the data practices of every library you embed.
- **Credential stuffing.** Hundreds of thousands of Zoom account credentials appeared for sale in 2020. This was not a breach of Zoom's servers. The credentials came from reuse of passwords leaked elsewhere. That's an attack on users, but it highlights why SSO and MFA matter for a service that suddenly became critical.
- **Class-action litigation.** Zoom settled a privacy class action in the US, reported at $85 million, over the sharing and security claims.
- **AI and terms of service (2023).** A change to the terms of service led to a public reaction that Zoom might use customer content to train AI. Zoom clarified that it would not train its AI models on audio, video, or chat without customer consent, and revised the language. The episode shows how legal wording on data rights can be read very differently from engineering intent.

## 5. Reliability and outages

Zoom is a real-time media system, and those are hard to run at scale. Known classes of reliability problems:

**Capacity events.** When school and work moved online, demand spiked far beyond what anyone had forecast. Zoom scaled quickly, but there were disruptions, including a widely noticed one on the first day of school for many districts in 2020.

**DNS and domain issues.** In April 2025, zoom.us became unreachable for many users because of a domain-level problem involving the registry. The servers were fine, but the name resolved incorrectly or not at all. It's a good reminder that your availability depends on the registrar and registry chain, not only on your own infrastructure.

**Regional and dependency failures.** Like any large service, Zoom is exposed to cloud provider incidents, certificate expirations, and bad configuration pushes. These tend to look identical to users: "Zoom is down."

## 6. Everyday quality problems (the ones you actually feel)

Most daily frustration is not a security flaw. It's the physics and plumbing of real-time media.

- **Echo and feedback.** Two devices in the same room, each with a microphone and speaker open, create a loop. Echo cancellation can't always fix it, especially when the audio path has variable delay.
- **Bluetooth headsets.** Using the microphone forces Bluetooth into a low-bandwidth profile (the headset/hands-free profile), so audio quality drops. This is a Bluetooth limitation, not a Zoom bug.
- **Jitter and packet loss.** Real-time media uses UDP-style transport, where late packets are useless. Wi-Fi congestion shows up as robotic audio and frozen video. Zoom adapts by lowering resolution and frame rate, which is why you see blur before you see a full drop.
- **High CPU and fan noise.** Video encoding, virtual backgrounds, and noise suppression are all compute-heavy. On older laptops, turning off HD video and background effects helps more than anything else.
- **Firewall and proxy trouble.** Corporate networks that block UDP force a fallback to TCP or HTTPS tunneling, which raises latency.
- **Permissions.** On macOS and Windows, camera and microphone access is controlled at the OS level, and a surprising number of "Zoom can't see my camera" reports come from another app holding the device or a privacy setting.

## What developers can learn

1. **Defaults are your real security policy.** Most users never change them.
2. **A local listener is an attack surface.** Treat `localhost` like the internet.
3. **Be precise in security claims.** "Encrypted" without naming the endpoints invites a legal and reputational problem.
4. **Privileged helpers need strict validation.** Check, then use, atomically.
5. **Your dependencies are part of your privacy policy.** Audit SDKs.
6. **Plan for the boring failures.** DNS, certificates, and capacity take down more services than clever exploits do.

## A caveat on "known"

Zoom publishes security bulletins, and CVEs are issued regularly. The list changes with every release, so any article, this one included, is a snapshot. If you administer Zoom for an organization, subscribe to the official security bulletins, keep clients updated, enforce passcodes and waiting rooms, and decide deliberately when you need E2EE and when you can live without server-side features.
