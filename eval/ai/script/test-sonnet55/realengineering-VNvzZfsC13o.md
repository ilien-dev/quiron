# Are Electric Planes Possible?

**Runtime: ~11 minutes | Format: narration with [VISUAL] cues**

---

## COLD OPEN

[VISUAL: Slow-motion shot of a small white plane lifting off a quiet runway. No engine roar, just a soft hum.]

**NARRATOR:**
This plane is taking off, and you can barely hear it. No smoke, no exhaust, no smell of burning fuel. Just a spinning propeller and a lot of very quiet air.

It's real. It's certified. People are flying it right now.

So why, when you board a flight tomorrow, will you almost certainly be sitting inside a machine that burns kerosene?

[VISUAL: Title card: "ARE ELECTRIC PLANES POSSIBLE?"]

The short answer is yes, but only sometimes. The long answer involves a single number that decides almost everything in aviation, and it's the reason electric cars took over the road while electric airliners are still mostly a dream.

Let's get into it.

---

## PART 1: THE NUMBER THAT RULES EVERYTHING

[VISUAL: A jet fuel can next to a battery pack on a scale.]

**NARRATOR:**
Cars are forgiving. If your electric car is a few hundred kilos heavier than a gas car, the world doesn't end. The road holds it up for free.

A plane doesn't get that luxury. Every kilogram has to be held in the air by wings, constantly, burning energy the entire time. So in aviation, weight isn't a detail. It's the whole game.

And that brings us to the number: **energy density**, meaning how much energy you can store per kilogram.

[VISUAL: Animated bar chart.]

Jet fuel holds roughly 12,000 watt-hours per kilogram. A very good modern lithium-ion battery cell holds around 250 to 300. And that's the cell alone. Once you add the casing, cooling, wiring, and safety systems, the usable figure for a full battery pack is lower still.

So on paper, fuel carries about forty times more energy per kilogram than a battery.

Now, you might say: "But electric motors are far more efficient!" And you'd be right. A turbine engine converts maybe 35 to 40 percent of its fuel energy into useful thrust. An electric motor can top 90 percent.

[VISUAL: Two efficiency bars, motor vs. turbine.]

That helps. It narrows the gap. But it doesn't close it. After you account for efficiency, fuel still wins by a factor of roughly fifteen to twenty.

And there's a second, sneakier problem.

### The disappearing-fuel problem

[VISUAL: A jet shrinking in weight as fuel gauge drops, next to an electric plane whose weight stays constant.]

**NARRATOR:**
A conventional plane gets lighter as it flies. It burns fuel, so by the time it lands, it can weigh tens of tonnes less than at takeoff. That makes the second half of the flight cheaper than the first.

A battery plane doesn't do that. A depleted battery weighs exactly the same as a full one. You carry the dead weight all the way to the ground.

This is why range gets brutal as planes get bigger. The heavier the aircraft, the more battery you need to lift the battery. It's a vicious spiral that engineers call the **weight snowball**, and it gets worse the farther you want to go.

So that's the problem. Now let's look at what people have actually built.

---

## PART 2: WHAT ACTUALLY FLIES TODAY

[VISUAL: Montage of small electric aircraft.]

**NARRATOR:**
Small electric planes are not science fiction. They exist.

The **Pipistrel Velis Electro**, a two-seat trainer from Slovenia, became the first fully electric aircraft to receive type certification from European regulators, back in 2020. Flight schools use it. It's perfect for the job: short lessons, lots of takeoffs and landings, and an airfield to return to every time.

Its endurance is around 50 minutes, plus reserves. For a student pilot practicing circuits, that's plenty. For a trip to another country, it's useless.

And that tells you something important. Electric flight isn't failing. It's succeeding in a **narrow slice** of aviation, the slice where short range is fine and cheap operation matters more than anything else.

[VISUAL: Map with short hops highlighted.]

### The commuter dream

Then there's the middle ground. Companies have tried building small electric commuter aircraft for short regional hops: islands, fjords, mountain valleys, routes where a ferry or a long drive is the only alternative.

A prototype called **Alice**, built by Eviation, flew in 2022, a nine-passenger design that made a short first flight. It proved the concept, but it also showed how hard the road to commercial service is. Certification, battery supply, and funding have all been serious hurdles for this entire category, and several startups in the field have struggled or restructured.

Here's the honest picture: the engineering is possible. The economics and the regulation are slow.

### The air taxi wave

[VISUAL: eVTOL aircraft hovering over a city skyline.]

**NARRATOR:**
Then there's the flashiest corner of the industry: **eVTOLs**, electric vertical takeoff and landing aircraft. Think of a helicopter with many small rotors, quieter and mechanically simpler.

Companies like Joby and Archer are working toward air taxi service over city traffic. The trips are short, ten or twenty minutes. That fits batteries well, because you don't need range, you need power for takeoff and landing, then a quick recharge.

Whether they become an everyday service or a niche for the wealthy is still an open question. But technically, this is the category where electric flight makes the most sense right now.

So small and short works. What about the big stuff, the planes that actually cause most of aviation's emissions?

---

## PART 3: THE JUMBO PROBLEM

[VISUAL: A long-haul airliner crossing an ocean.]

**NARRATOR:**
Here's an uncomfortable fact. Most aviation emissions don't come from little planes. They come from medium and long flights, narrow-bodies and wide-bodies, hauling hundreds of people over thousands of kilometers.

Aviation accounts for a bit over 2 percent of global CO₂ emissions. When you include contrails and other high-altitude effects, its climate impact is meaningfully larger.

That's a small slice of the total, but it's one of the hardest to remove, because there's no easy substitute. And with today's batteries, a fully electric transatlantic airliner is simply out of reach. The battery alone would weigh more than the plane can lift.

So the industry is chasing other routes.

### Option one: hybrid-electric

[VISUAL: Diagram of a turbine feeding a generator feeding electric motors, plus a battery.]

**NARRATOR:**
A hybrid keeps some fuel but uses batteries to cover the most power-hungry moments, like takeoff and climb. Cruise can then use a smaller, more efficient engine.

It won't make flying emission-free, but it can cut fuel burn on regional routes, and it lets us build experience with electric propulsion while batteries improve. Several companies are developing hybrid regional aircraft in the 30-seat range.

### Option two: hydrogen

[VISUAL: Hydrogen tank, fuel cell, propeller.]

Hydrogen carries a huge amount of energy per kilogram, about three times as much as jet fuel. The catch is that it takes up enormous volume, even when liquefied, and it has to be kept extremely cold. That means big, bulky tanks and a radically redesigned airframe.

Hydrogen can power electric motors through a fuel cell, or burn directly in modified engines. Airbus has explored both, but timelines have slipped, and the company has publicly pushed back its original hopes. Hydrogen isn't dead, but it's clearly harder than the early headlines suggested.

### Option three: sustainable aviation fuel

[VISUAL: Refinery and a fuel truck at an airport.]

Then there's the least glamorous option, and possibly the most important one: **sustainable aviation fuel**, or SAF. This is a drop-in fuel made from waste oils, agricultural residues, or captured carbon and green hydrogen.

Today's planes can burn it with no redesign. The challenge is scale and cost. SAF is currently a small fraction of total fuel supply and costs much more than kerosene.

So here's the realistic outlook: for long-haul flights, the near-term answer probably isn't electric at all. It's a cleaner liquid fuel.

---

## PART 4: WHAT WOULD HAVE TO CHANGE

[VISUAL: Futuristic battery lab.]

**NARRATOR:**
So what would it take to make large electric planes real?

Most estimates say you'd need battery packs around **500 watt-hours per kilogram or higher** at the pack level, and then some. Some analyses suggest that for short regional airliners, numbers in the range of 800 or more become truly compelling.

We're not there. Lithium-ion has improved steadily, but only by a few percent a year, not by leaps.

[VISUAL: Lab shots of solid-state cells and lithium-sulfur diagrams.]

There are candidates for the next jump. **Solid-state batteries** replace the liquid electrolyte with a solid one, promising higher density and better safety. **Lithium-sulfur** chemistry offers high theoretical energy per kilogram, though lifespan has been a stubborn problem.

Promising lab results are common. Cells that survive thousands of cycles, can be mass-produced, and pass aviation safety standards are much rarer. And aviation certification is unforgiving, as it should be. A battery fire in the air is not a recall, it's a catastrophe.

### What about solar?

You may be wondering about **Solar Impulse 2**, the plane that flew around the world on sunlight alone in 2015 and 2016.

[VISUAL: Solar Impulse 2 with its enormous wingspan.]

It was a spectacular achievement. But look at it: a wingspan wider than a 747's, a single pilot, and a cruising speed slower than a car on a highway. Sunlight is simply too diluted to power a practical passenger aircraft. Solar can help on the margins, but it won't carry a family to Lisbon.

### The other things that must change

And it's not just batteries. A world of electric aviation needs airports with serious charging infrastructure, since charging a large battery fast demands a huge amount of power. It needs regulators to write new rules. It needs clean electricity, because an electric plane charged on coal power just moves the emissions somewhere else.

None of that is impossible. All of it takes time.

---

## PART 5: SO, ARE THEY POSSIBLE?

[VISUAL: Split screen, small electric plane on one side, big airliner on the other.]

**NARRATOR:**
Let's pull this together.

**Are electric planes possible?** Yes. They already exist, they already fly, and for training, short hops, and urban air taxis, they make real sense today.

**Will they replace the planes we fly on now?** Not soon, and not for long distances unless batteries get dramatically better.

What's most likely is not one winner but a patchwork:

- **Battery-electric** for small aircraft and short routes.
- **Hybrid-electric** for regional flights.
- **Hydrogen** for some mid-range aircraft, if the engineering and economics work out.
- **Sustainable fuels** for the long-haul flights that dominate emissions.

[VISUAL: A map showing different aircraft types over different distances.]

And if you're wondering when that mix will arrive, the honest answer is: gradually. Aviation moves slowly on purpose. Aircraft are designed to last thirty years or more, and a new design takes a decade to certify. Even if a miracle battery were announced tomorrow, it would take years before you'd board an airliner using it.

But slow isn't the same as impossible. A century ago, crossing the Atlantic by air was a stunt. Now it's a Tuesday. The physics of electric flight don't forbid it. They just demand patience, better chemistry, and a lot of clever engineering.
