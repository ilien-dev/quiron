## INTRO (0:25)

**[VISUAL: Host on camera in the workshop, mirrors and optical rails on the bench behind them.]**

**HOST:**
Hey everyone. Today we're going to take a technique that makes invisible air visible, and we're going to make it *colorful*.

It's called schlieren imaging. If you've ever seen a photo of a bullet with a shock wave rippling off it, or a jet fighter with its supersonic shock cones glowing in the air, that was schlieren.

Most schlieren photos are black and white. They look cool, but today I'll show you how a small change to the setup turns every ripple into a rainbow. And the best part? It's cheap enough to build at home.

## WHAT IS SCHLIEREN? (1:10)

**[VISUAL: Animated diagram. Light rays pass through a block of glass, bending slightly.]**

**HOST (V.O.):**
"Schlieren" is German for "streaks" or "striations." The idea goes back to Robert Hooke in the 1600s, and the technique was refined by August Toepler in the 1860s.

Here's the core principle. Light changes speed depending on what it's traveling through. Denser material, like cold air, slows light more. Less dense material, like hot air, slows it less. That property is called the *refractive index*.

**[VISUAL: Animation shows a ray passing through a region where air density gradually changes. The ray bends toward the denser side.]**

So when light passes through air that's hot on one side and cool on the other, the ray bends, just a tiny bit. Far too little for your eye to notice.

Schlieren imaging is a clever way of turning those microscopic bends into differences you can see.

---

## THE CLASSIC SETUP (2:15)

**[VISUAL: Top-down diagram of the Z-type schlieren layout, animated step by step.]**

**HOST (V.O.):**
Here's the classic layout, called a Z-type setup.

One: a bright light source, shining through a tiny slit or pinhole. This acts like a point of light.

Two: a concave mirror, one that curves inward, collimates that light, meaning it sends it out as a wide beam of parallel rays.

Three: the beam passes through our test area. That's where the candle, the heat, or the gas goes.

Four: a second concave mirror collects the beam and focuses it back down to a point.

And five, the magic step. At that focal point, we place a **knife edge**. Literally a razor blade.

**[VISUAL: Close-up of the blade slicing into the focused spot of light. Overlay: "Blocks about half the light."]**

The blade cuts off roughly half of the focused spot. Now, if nothing is disturbing the air, the image is evenly dim. But if a ray gets bent even a little, it either lands on the blade and gets blocked, or slips past it and adds brightness.

**[VISUAL: Split-screen. Left: ray deflecting into blade, region goes dark. Right: ray deflecting away, region goes bright.]**

So: bend one way, darker. Bend the other way, brighter. Your camera sees a picture of the *gradient* of air density. That's what a schlieren image is.

## THE PROBLEM WITH BLACK AND WHITE (3:40)

**[VISUAL: Host holds up a printed black-and-white schlieren image of a lighter's gas.]**

**HOST:**
This works great, but there's a limitation. Your image is shades of gray. A faint gradient and a mild one can look nearly identical. And the knife edge only measures bending in *one direction*, perpendicular to the blade.

So how do we get more information out of the same light?

We replace the blade with color.

---

## COLOR SCHLIEREN: THE BIG IDEA (4:10)

**[VISUAL: Animation. The knife edge dissolves and is replaced by a filter with vertical stripes: red, green, blue.]**

**HOST (V.O.):**
Instead of blocking light, we *tag* it. At the focal point, we put a small filter made of colored bands. Say red, green, and blue, side by side.

With no disturbance, the focused spot of light lands on the center band. The whole image is one flat color, say green.

**[VISUAL: Green spot centered on the filter. Camera view: uniform green.]**

Now the candle's hot air bends some rays slightly left. Those rays land on the red band. Rays bent right land on blue.

**[VISUAL: Rays split to red and blue bands. Camera view: green background, with red and blue patterns around the plume.]**

Now every pixel in your photo carries a color that encodes *which way* and *how much* the light was bent. Instead of just "darker" or "brighter," you get a whole palette of information.

Even better, you can use a filter shaped like a bullseye, with concentric colored rings, or a pie-chart of colors. Then you capture bending in *every* direction at once, not just one axis.

## BUILDING IT (5:20)

**[VISUAL: Workshop footage. Host gathers parts on the bench.]**

**HOST:**
Alright, let's build it. You'll need:

A pair of concave mirrors. I'm using 6-inch parabolic mirrors, the kind sold for small telescopes. Parabolic is ideal because it focuses parallel light cleanly.

A bright LED, with a small slit in front of it. A razor-blade gap or a bit of foil with a thin cut works fine.

A camera. Anything with manual focus and manual exposure will do.

And the star of the show: our color filter.

**[VISUAL: Host prints a color stripe pattern on transparency film using an inkjet printer.]**

I made mine by printing thin stripes of red, yellow, green, and blue onto transparency film. You can also use theatrical lighting gels, or cut strips from colored cellophane. Just keep the stripes narrow, because the focused spot is tiny.

**[VISUAL: Time-lapse of alignment. Host adjusts mirrors, shows the light spot on a card.]**

Alignment is the hardest part. Set the mirrors a bit off-axis, and shine the light so it bounces from one mirror to the other. Use a white card to find the focal spot. Then place your filter right *there*, and look through it from behind. Fine-tune until the image of your slit lands cleanly on the center band.

One tip: when you adjust, go slowly. A fraction of a millimeter makes a big difference.

## WHAT THE COLORS MEAN (8:20)

**[VISUAL: Host points at a frozen frame of the candle plume with annotations.]**

**HOST:**
Let's decode one of these images. Green is our "nothing happening" background. Where you see red, light bent one way. Where you see blue, it bent the other way. The more saturated the color, the larger the bend.

Keep in mind, though: this isn't temperature, and it isn't a direct measurement of density. It's the *rate of change* of density. A sharp edge, like the boundary of a hot plume, lights up vividly, while a smooth, gradual change might barely show.

Also, color schlieren has a trade-off: the more sensitive you make it, the narrower your stripes, and the harder alignment gets.

---

## WHERE THIS IS USED (9:00)

**[VISUAL: Archive-style footage of wind tunnels, shock waves on models, combustion experiments.]**

**HOST (V.O.):**
This isn't just a pretty trick. Aerospace engineers use schlieren in wind tunnels to see shock waves around wings and nozzles. Combustion researchers use it to study flames. HVAC engineers use it to visualize airflow. Even forensic scientists use it to study how gases and droplets move from coughs and sneezes.

And modern variants like background-oriented schlieren replace mirrors with a patterned background and software, which means you can image a whole room.
