# How "Forever Chemicals" Polluted America's Water

**Runtime: ~11-12 min | Target: ~1,710 words**

---

## COLD OPEN

**[VISUAL: Slow pan over green West Virginia hills. Archival footage of cattle in a field.]**

In the late 1990s, a farmer named Wilbur Tennant started losing his cattle. Their teeth turned black. Their organs were swollen. Calves were born deformed. Tennant's farm sat next to a landfill owned by DuPont, one of the biggest chemical companies on Earth, and he was sure the company was responsible.

Nobody believed him. But the substance killing his cows wasn't a rare industrial accident. It was a chemical that is today in the blood of nearly every person in America, including you.

---

## PART 1: THE MIRACLE MOLECULE

**[VISUAL: 1930s lab footage, Teflon pan ads, mid-century kitchen scenes.]**

The story begins in 1938. A DuPont chemist named Roy Plunkett was working with refrigerant gases when he opened a cylinder that should have been full and found it empty. Inside was a slippery white powder. It was polytetrafluoroethylene, which the world would come to know as Teflon.

The secret was the carbon-fluorine bond, one of the strongest in organic chemistry. Chemicals built on it resist heat, water, oil, and almost everything else you can throw at them.

Teflon's first big job was in the Manhattan Project, where it contained corrosive materials used to enrich uranium. After the war, it moved into kitchens. And it was only the first of a huge family of compounds.

Today we call them PFAS: per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances. Depending on how you count, there are thousands of them. Companies like 3M and DuPont found they made products stain-proof, grease-proof, and waterproof. Scotchgard on your couch. Wrappers for greasy fast food. Waterproof jackets. Nonstick pans. Carpets. Dental floss. Even some cosmetics.

And there was one more application, which would turn out to be among the most contaminating of all.

---

## PART 2: THE FIRE FOAM

**[VISUAL: Military firefighting training, foam blanketing a burning jet fuel pit.]**

In the 1960s, the U.S. Navy and 3M developed aqueous film-forming foam, or AFFF. Jet fuel fires are brutally hard to put out. This foam spread a thin film across the fuel and smothered the flames, and it worked spectacularly.

So it was adopted everywhere: military bases, commercial airports, fire training grounds, refineries. For decades, crews sprayed it in training exercises, and the runoff soaked into the ground.

That ground sits above groundwater. Hundreds of military installations across the country now have known or suspected PFAS contamination, and so do many civilian airports.

---

## PART 3: WHY "FOREVER"?

**[VISUAL: Animated molecule. A carbon chain with fluorine atoms, then a "bond strength" graphic.]**

The same property that makes these chemicals useful makes them a nightmare in the environment.

Most pollutants eventually break down. Sunlight, bacteria, and chemical reactions slowly dismantle them. But the carbon-fluorine bond is so strong that nature has almost no way to break it. Some PFAS can persist in soil and water for centuries or longer, which is where the nickname "forever chemicals" comes from.

They also move easily. Many dissolve in water, so they travel through rivers and seep down into aquifers. And they accumulate. When you drink or eat them, they don't simply pass through. Some, like PFOA and PFOS, stay in the human body for years.

That combination of persistence, mobility, and accumulation is why this problem is so hard to fix.

---

## PART 4: THE FARMER AND THE LAWYER

**[VISUAL: Courthouse exteriors, document boxes, a photo of Rob Bilott.]**

Back to Wilbur Tennant. Desperate, he eventually reached a corporate defense attorney in Cincinnati named Rob Bilott. Bilott's grandmother lived near Tennant, and Bilott had spent his career defending chemical companies. Taking the case was an odd move.

In 1999, Bilott filed suit and pushed for discovery. What came back was a flood of internal documents, and what they showed was alarming.

DuPont had been using a PFAS chemical called PFOA, also known as C8, at its Washington Works plant in Parkersburg, West Virginia. The company had dumped waste containing it into unlined pits and released it into the Ohio River. Internal studies going back decades suggested the company knew it was building up in workers' blood, and that it could be harmful. Meanwhile, 3M had its own research on PFAS toxicity.

The documents showed the companies knew far more than the public did.

Bilott then did something remarkable. He went after the bigger picture. He helped file a class action on behalf of tens of thousands of residents whose drinking water had been contaminated. As part of the 2005 settlement, an independent group of epidemiologists, the C8 Science Panel, studied roughly 69,000 people.

Their conclusion was that C8 exposure had a "probable link" to six conditions: kidney cancer, testicular cancer, thyroid disease, ulcerative colitis, high cholesterol, and pregnancy-induced hypertension. Later, DuPont and its spinoff Chemours settled hundreds of personal injury cases for about 671 million dollars.

---

## PART 5: IT WAS NEVER JUST ONE TOWN

**[VISUAL: US map filling with red dots, one location at a time.]**

If this had stayed a West Virginia story, it would be tragic but contained. It didn't.

In Hoosick Falls, New York, residents learned in 2015 that their water contained PFOA at levels many times higher than what is now considered safe, traced to a nearby plant that made products with the chemical.

In Rockford, Michigan, waste from a shoe manufacturer's tannery operations, which used Scotchgard, had been dumped decades earlier. The contamination spread to wells in the surrounding area.

In North Carolina, the Cape Fear River, which supplies drinking water to hundreds of thousands of people, was found in 2017 to carry GenX, a replacement chemical made at a Chemours plant upstream.

In Maine, farmers discovered that sludge from wastewater treatment plants, sold as fertilizer for years, had loaded their fields with PFAS. Some farms had to shut down completely.

Then came the national picture. A U.S. Geological Survey study published in 2023 estimated that at least 45 percent of the country's tap water has one or more PFAS. And the CDC's national survey has found PFAS in the blood of the vast majority of Americans tested.

---

## PART 6: THE REPLACEMENT TRAP

**[VISUAL: Animated "swap" of chemical names: PFOS → shorter chains, C8 → GenX.]**

You might expect the story to end when regulators moved in. It didn't, and one reason is what's often called regrettable substitution.

By the early 2000s, 3M began phasing out PFOS and PFOA, and in 2006 the EPA convinced major manufacturers to commit to eliminating PFOA by 2015. But the companies didn't abandon the whole category. They switched to similar chemicals, often with shorter carbon chains, like GenX.

The pitch was that these newer versions were safer. But that was based on limited data, and the replacements are still extremely persistent. Some are even harder to filter out of water. Regulators and scientists were left playing whack-a-mole with a family of thousands of chemicals.

---

## PART 7: WHAT PFAS DO TO THE BODY

**[VISUAL: Simple body diagram with organs highlighted.]**

Now, the health picture. Let's be precise about what's established and what's still being studied.

The strongest evidence links certain PFAS, especially PFOA and PFOS, to higher cholesterol, reduced vaccine response in children, changes in liver enzymes, lower birth weight, thyroid disease, and kidney and testicular cancer. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classified PFOA as carcinogenic to humans in 2023.

For thousands of other PFAS, there simply isn't enough research. That uncertainty is part of the problem. These chemicals were in use for decades before most of their health effects were studied.

And effects can occur at astonishingly low concentrations. We're talking about parts per trillion, the equivalent of a few drops in an Olympic swimming pool.

---

## PART 8: THE RULES, AT LAST

**[VISUAL: EPA headquarters, a news clip of the 2024 announcement.]**

For years, the United States had no enforceable national limit for PFAS in drinking water. The EPA issued a non-binding health advisory in 2016, then tightened it dramatically in 2022.

Finally, in April 2024, the EPA finalized the first national drinking water standards for PFAS. They set limits of 4 parts per trillion for PFOA and PFOS, essentially the lowest levels reliably measurable, with additional limits for several other PFAS.

Water utilities would need to monitor, and install treatment where levels were too high. Granular activated carbon, ion exchange resins, and reverse osmosis can all remove PFAS, but they're expensive, and the filters themselves become waste that has to be handled.

The rules haven't been a straight line since. The EPA under the new administration signaled in 2025 that it would keep the PFOA and PFOS limits but extend the compliance deadline and revisit the standards for other PFAS. Water utilities, environmental groups, and industry have all been fighting over the details, including in court.

---

## PART 9: WHO PAYS?

**[VISUAL: Money graphic, utility workers, filtration plant.]**

Cleaning up is going to cost tens of billions of dollars. The question is who pays: manufacturers or ratepayers.

In 2023, 3M agreed to pay at least 10.3 billion dollars to settle claims from public water systems. DuPont, Chemours, and Corteva agreed to a settlement of about 1.19 billion dollars. Other cases brought by states over contamination and firefighting foam are still moving through the courts.

3M said it would stop making PFAS by the end of 2025. But those settlements are widely seen as a fraction of the true cost, and the chemicals already released will be with us for generations.

---

## PART 10: WHAT CAN YOU DO?

**[VISUAL: Kitchen sink, water testing kit, filter cartridges.]**

So what should an ordinary person do?

First, find out what's in your water. Your utility is required to publish annual water quality reports, and under the new rules, PFAS monitoring results will be part of them. If you're on a private well, you'll need to test it yourself with a certified lab.

Second, if levels are a concern, filters can help. Look for ones certified to NSF/ANSI standard 53 or 58 for PFOA and PFOS reduction. Reverse osmosis systems and activated carbon filters can work, as long as you maintain them. Boiling water does not remove PFAS. It can actually concentrate them.

Third, you can reduce exposure at the margins by avoiding stain-resistant treatments and checking labels on products that advertise being waterproof or nonstick.

But none of this changes the central truth. Individual choices can't solve a problem created at industrial scale. This is a problem for regulation, and for companies to be held accountable.

---

## CLOSING

**[VISUAL: Return to the West Virginia hills. Slow fade.]**

Wilbur Tennant died in 2009 of cancer, after more than a decade of fighting. His cattle, his land, and his neighbors' water were all proof of something that began as a lab accident and a scientific triumph.

The tragedy of PFAS isn't that one company did something shocking. It's that an entire industry spent decades profiting from a family of chemicals that never goes away, while the evidence of harm sat in files nobody outside the companies was allowed to see.

It took a farmer, a lawyer, and thousands of residents to drag it into the open. And now the clock is ticking, because the chemicals made to last forever are doing exactly that.
