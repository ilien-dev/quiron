# Hovercraft, Concorde, and the Dreams of the 1970s

**[COLD OPEN]**

Picture Dover in 1975. A roaring machine the size of a building skims across the beach on a cushion of air, carrying cars and passengers to France in half an hour. Overhead, a white dart with drooping nose cuts toward New York at twice the speed of sound.

This was the future. And then it wasn't.

**[TITLE CARD]**

**[SECTION ONE: RIDING ON AIR]**

In the 1950s, British engineer Christopher Cockerell tested his big idea with two coffee tins and a hairdryer. Trap air beneath a craft, and it floats above land and water alike. By 1968, the giant SR.N4 was crossing the English Channel, carrying around thirty cars and hundreds of passengers.

It was loud, bumpy, and thrilling. Newspapers promised hovertrains, hover-buses, and hover-highways. Engineers imagined a world where roads and rivers were the same thing.

**[SECTION TWO: FASTER THAN THE SUN]**

Meanwhile, Britain and France were building something even bolder. Concorde entered passenger service in 1976. It flew London to New York in about three and a half hours. Heading west, travelers could land, by the clock, before they had taken off.

Champagne at Mach 2. Windows warm to the touch. The sky turning dark purple at sixty thousand feet.

**[SECTION THREE: THE CATCH]**

But the seventies also brought the oil crisis. Suddenly, fuel-hungry marvels looked less like progress and more like luxury. Concorde's sonic boom kept it from flying supersonic over land. Only twenty were ever built, and just fourteen ever carried paying passengers.

Hovercraft burned fuel fast too, and struggled in rough seas. Then the Channel Tunnel opened in 1994. The last cross-Channel SR.N4 flight came in 2000. Concorde followed in 2003.

**[CLOSING]**

So were they failures? Maybe not. They were proof that we could do astonishing things. The real question they left behind is harder: not *can* we build the future, but can we afford to keep it?

Thanks for watching.
