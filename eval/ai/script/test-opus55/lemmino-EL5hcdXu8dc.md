## #10 - Earworms Are Almost Universal (And Gum Might Help)

*[Visual: Animated cartoon brain with a tiny jukebox inside, playing on a loop.]*

**NARRATOR:**
You know the feeling. You hear ten seconds of a jingle in a grocery store, and three days later it's still playing in your head.

Scientists have a name for this: **involuntary musical imagery**. Everyone else calls it an earworm.

Earworms are extremely common. A study led by Finnish researcher Lassi Liikkanen surveyed thousands of people and found that roughly **nine out of ten** experience an earworm at least once a week. For a large share of people, it happens every day.

Earworms usually aren't whole songs. They're almost always a short fragment, typically a chorus or a hook, repeating over and over. Researchers think that's part of why they stick. Your brain treats the unfinished loop like an unfinished task and keeps coming back to it.

The songs most likely to get stuck tend to share a few traits. They're usually upbeat. Their melodies are simple and easy to sing. And they often contain a small surprise, like an unusual interval or an unexpected rhythm, that makes them stand out.

So how do you get rid of one? In 2015, researchers at the University of Reading found something unexpected: **chewing gum**. Participants who chewed gum reported fewer unwanted musical thoughts. The theory is that chewing occupies the same parts of the brain involved in "subvocalizing," the silent, internal singing that keeps an earworm alive.

So the next time a jingle won't leave you alone, try a stick of gum.

## #9 - "Happy Birthday" Was Under Copyright for Decades

*[Visual: A birthday cake with candles. A lawyer's briefcase slides into frame.]*

**NARRATOR:**
"Happy Birthday to You" is one of the most recognized songs in the English language. People sing it at kitchen tables and in hospital rooms all over the world.

For most of the twentieth century, a company claimed to own it.

The melody goes back to 1893, when two sisters from Kentucky, **Patty and Mildred Hill**, published a children's classroom song called "Good Morning to All." Over time, people started singing birthday lyrics to the same tune. Eventually, a copyright was registered for "Happy Birthday" in the 1930s.

In 1988, the music publisher Warner/Chappell acquired the company that held that copyright. For years afterward, it was reportedly earning around **two million dollars a year** in licensing fees. That's a big part of why so many restaurants invented their own odd, clap-along birthday songs, and why movies and TV shows often avoided the real one. Using it on screen could cost thousands of dollars.

Then a documentary filmmaker making a film about the song itself was asked to pay a licensing fee, and pushed back in court. In 2015, a federal judge ruled that Warner/Chappell had never held a valid copyright to the lyrics. In 2016, the company agreed to a **fourteen-million-dollar settlement**, and the song was declared to be in the public domain.

So today, you can sing "Happy Birthday" anywhere you like. For a long time, that wasn't clearly true.

## #8 - The "Mozart Effect" Is Mostly a Myth

*[Visual: A baby in a crib wearing oversized headphones. A stack of classical CDs.]*

**NARRATOR:**
You've probably heard that playing Mozart to babies makes them smarter. It's one of the most widespread ideas in pop psychology, and it's built on a misunderstanding.

In 1993, researchers Frances Rauscher, Gordon Shaw, and Katherine Ky published a short paper in the journal *Nature*. They had **college students**, not babies, listen to ten minutes of Mozart's Sonata for Two Pianos in D major. Afterward, the students did slightly better on a spatial reasoning task, like mentally folding and cutting paper.

There were important limits. The effect was small. It was temporary, lasting only a short time. And it measured one narrow kind of task, not general intelligence.

The public didn't hear those limits. Within a few years, "Mozart makes you smarter" was everywhere. Products marketed to parents promised to boost their infants' brains. In 1998, the governor of Georgia, Zell Miller, even proposed spending state money to give every newborn in Georgia a classical music CD.

Later research mostly let the air out of the idea. Large reviews of the studies found the effect was tiny or hard to reproduce. Psychologist Glenn Schellenberg and others showed that the short-term boost likely came from **mood and arousal**. When people listen to something they enjoy and feel more alert, they tend to perform a little better. In one study, children did just as well after listening to an upbeat pop band as after Mozart.

That doesn't mean music is useless for the brain. Actively **learning to play** an instrument has been linked to benefits in attention and auditory skills. But just listening to Mozart in the background won't do much for your IQ.

## #7 - Perfect Pitch Is Rare, Unless You Speak the Right Language

*[Visual: A tuning fork vibrating. Map highlighting East and Southeast Asia.]*

**NARRATOR:**
Most people can tell when a note goes up or down. But a small number of people can hear a single note, a car horn, a microwave beep, a random piano key, and name it immediately: "That's a B-flat."

This is called **absolute pitch**, or perfect pitch. In Western populations, it's estimated to occur in roughly **one in ten thousand** people.

It isn't spread evenly, though. Two things seem to matter a lot: **starting music training early**, usually before age six or seven, and **the language you grow up speaking**.

In tonal languages like Mandarin, Cantonese, and Vietnamese, the pitch of a syllable can change its meaning. In Mandarin, the syllable "ma" can mean "mother," "horse," or something else entirely, depending on its tone.

Psychologist **Diana Deutsch** and her colleagues compared music students at the Central Conservatory of Music in Beijing with students at the Eastman School of Music in New York. Among students who had started training at similar ages, the rate of absolute pitch was dramatically higher among the Mandarin speakers.

One leading idea is that every infant may start life with some capacity to attach meaning to specific pitches. If your language rewards that skill, you keep it. If it doesn't, it may fade.

## #6 - A Single Piece of Music Will Take 639 Years to Perform

*[Visual: Exterior of an old stone church in Germany. Inside, a small, strange-looking organ.]*

**NARRATOR:**
American composer **John Cage** liked to challenge what music could be. His most famous piece, *4'33"*, premiered in 1952. The performer sits at the piano and plays nothing for four minutes and thirty-three seconds. The "music" is whatever the audience hears: coughs, rustling programs, rain on the roof.

But Cage has an even stranger record.

In the 1980s, he wrote a piece for organ called *Organ²/ASLSP*. The letters stand for "**As SLow aS Possible**." Cage didn't say exactly how slow that should be. On an organ, a note can keep sounding as long as air keeps flowing through the pipe.

So a group of musicians and philosophers in Germany decided to take the instruction seriously. In the town of **Halberstadt**, inside the St. Burchardi church, they started a performance designed to last **639 years**.

Why 639? Halberstadt is linked to an important early church organ built in **1361**. That was 639 years before the year 2000, so the organizers chose that same length.

The performance began on **September 5, 2001**, which would have been Cage's 89th birthday. It opened with a rest, so for the first seventeen months, you'd have heard only the gentle sound of the organ's bellows. The first chord sounded in 2003.

Since then, the piece has moved forward one chord change at a time, with years between some of them. Each change draws visitors from around the world. Small weights hold down the keys, and pipes are added or removed as the score requires.

If everything goes to plan, the final note will sound in the year **2640**. Nobody alive today will hear the whole thing.

## #5 - Beethoven Couldn't Hear the Applause for His Ninth Symphony

*[Visual: Painting of Vienna in the 1820s. A crowded concert hall.]*

**NARRATOR:**
Ludwig van Beethoven started losing his hearing in his late twenties. For a composer, it was devastating. In 1802, he wrote a private letter to his brothers, now called the Heiligenstadt Testament, describing his despair and admitting he had thought about ending his life. He wrote that only his art held him back.

He kept composing. And as his hearing faded almost entirely, he produced some of his greatest work, relying on the music he could imagine in his head.

On **May 7, 1824**, in Vienna, his **Ninth Symphony** premiered. It was huge and ambitious. For its final movement, Beethoven did something few had attempted: he brought **vocal soloists and a full choir** into a symphony to sing Friedrich Schiller's "Ode to Joy."

Beethoven shared the stage with the official conductor, Michael Umlauf, who had quietly told the musicians to follow him rather than Beethoven. Beethoven stood beside him, keeping time and following along in the score.

According to accounts of the evening, when the symphony ended, Beethoven was still facing the orchestra, a few beats behind. He couldn't hear the audience. One of the soloists, the young contralto **Caroline Unger**, gently turned him around so he could see the crowd on its feet, clapping, waving hats and handkerchiefs.

Today, the "Ode to Joy" melody serves as the anthem of the European Union. It was written by a man who could hardly hear it.

## #4 - When Choirs Sing Together, Their Heartbeats Sync Up

*[Visual: A choir in a rehearsal room. Overlay of animated heartbeat lines moving into sync.]*

**NARRATOR:**
There's a reason singing with a group can feel so connecting, and some of it may show up in your heart rate.

In 2013, researchers at the **University of Gothenburg** in Sweden, led by Björn Vickhoff, studied high school choir singers. They wired them up with pulse monitors and had them perform a few exercises: humming a single tone, singing a well-known hymn, and singing a slow chant.

As the singers performed, their **heart rates began to rise and fall together**.

The reason has to do with breathing. Singing controls your breath. You inhale at set points and breathe out slowly as you sing a phrase. Slow, steady exhalation activates the vagus nerve, which helps slow the heart. When everyone in a choir breathes at the same moments, their heart rhythms start to move in the same pattern.

The effect was strongest with slow, structured songs, the kind with long, even phrases. The researchers suggested this might be part of why group singing has long been associated with calm and connection, from lullabies to religious chants.

## #3 - Musical Instruments Are Over 40,000 Years Old

*[Visual: Close-up of a slender, ancient bone flute with carefully drilled holes.]*

**NARRATOR:**
How long have humans been making music? Singing almost certainly came first, and voices don't leave fossils. But instruments sometimes survive.

In 2008, archaeologists working in **Hohle Fels Cave** in southwestern Germany found pieces of a flute carved from the wing bone of a **griffon vulture**. When the pieces were put back together, the flute was more than twenty centimeters long, with five finger holes carefully drilled along its length and a notched end for blowing.

It's at least **35,000 years old**. Flutes from the nearby **Geissenklösterle** cave, made from bird bone and mammoth ivory, have been dated to roughly **40,000 years ago or more**.

These flutes come from some of the earliest communities of modern humans in Europe. That suggests music wasn't something people added after life became easy. It appears to have arrived along with our species' earliest art.

Thousands of years later, people also started **writing music down**. The oldest known written melody is the **Hurrian Hymn No. 6**, found on clay tablets in the ancient city of Ugarit, in modern-day Syria. It dates to around **1400 BCE** and is a hymn to the goddess Nikkal. Scholars still debate how to read its notation, so modern performances of it vary.

The oldest surviving piece that's **complete**, with both melody and lyrics, is the **Seikilos Epitaph**, carved on a marble gravestone in what is now Turkey about two thousand years ago. Its lyrics are short:

> *"While you live, shine. Have no grief at all. Life exists only for a short while, and time demands its due."*

## #2 - Chuck Berry Is Traveling Through Interstellar Space

*[Visual: The Voyager spacecraft drifting through space. The gold record gleams.]*

**NARRATOR:**
In 1977, NASA launched two spacecraft, **Voyager 1** and **Voyager 2**, on a grand tour of the outer planets. Each carried an unusual extra: a twelve-inch, gold-plated copper phonograph record.

The **Golden Record** was meant as a message to anyone, or anything, that might find it in the distant future. A committee led by astronomer **Carl Sagan** chose what went on it: photographs, natural sounds like thunder and birdsong, greetings in fifty-five languages, and about **ninety minutes of music** from around the world.

The music list is a wide mix. There's **Bach** and **Beethoven**. There's Javanese gamelan, Peruvian panpipes, Navajo night chant, Indian raga, and Bulgarian folk singing. There's Blind Willie Johnson's haunting blues piece "Dark Was the Night, Cold Was the Ground." And there's **Chuck Berry's "Johnny B. Goode."**

The record's cover is engraved with instructions showing how to play it, written in scientific symbols instead of words, along with a map pinpointing Earth's location using nearby pulsars.

Voyager 1 crossed into **interstellar space** in 2012. Voyager 2 followed in 2018. They are the most distant human-made objects ever built. The records are designed to survive for a very long time. Some estimates suggest they could last for **a billion years** or more.

So long after our cities are gone, a rock-and-roll song from 1958 may still be drifting between the stars.

## #1 - Music Triggers the Same Reward Chemical as Food

*[Visual: Slow-motion shot of goosebumps rising on an arm. Then a glowing brain scan.]*

**NARRATOR:**
Have you ever felt a shiver run down your spine during a song? Maybe a key change, a big chorus, or the moment a singer hits a powerful note?

That feeling is called **frisson**, and it's one of the clearest signs of what music does to your brain.

In 2011, neuroscientists at **McGill University**, led by **Valorie Salimpoor**, published a landmark study in *Nature Neuroscience*. They asked volunteers to bring in music that reliably gave them chills. Then they scanned the volunteers' brains while they listened.

They found that music caused the brain to release **dopamine**, the same chemical tied to the pleasure of food, sex, and other basic rewards. Music isn't something we need to survive, yet the brain rewards it in a similar way.

They also found that dopamine showed up at **two different moments**.

At the **peak** of the music, when the chills hit, dopamine was released in a region called the **nucleus accumbens**, closely linked to pleasure.

A few seconds **before** the peak, dopamine was released in a separate area called the **caudate**, which is tied to anticipation. The brain was predicting the good part and rewarding the wait.

That may help explain music's pull. A song builds tension, makes you expect a payoff, then either delivers or surprises you. Your brain is guessing what comes next, and it's rewarding you along the way.
