# Why Life Seems to Speed Up as We Age

**[COLD OPEN]**

Think back to the summer you were eight years old. Remember how it stretched out? Long afternoons that felt endless, weeks that seemed to hold entire lifetimes of adventures, and a school year that felt like it would never end.

Now think about last year. Where did it go?

If you're over thirty, you've probably said something like, "I can't believe it's already November." And you're not alone. Almost everyone reports that time seems to accelerate as they get older. Birthdays come faster. Holidays pile on top of each other. Decades slip by.

But here's the strange part: a clock ticks at exactly the same rate whether you're eight or eighty. An hour is an hour. A year is a year. So why does it *feel* so different?

Today, we're going to explore the psychology, neuroscience, and even the physics behind one of the most universal human experiences, and what you can actually do to slow it down.

**[TITLE CARD: Why Life Seems to Speed Up as We Age]**

---

**[SECTION 1: THE PROPORTION THEORY]**

Let's start with the oldest explanation, one that goes back to the 1800s.

In 1877, a French philosopher named Paul Janet proposed a simple idea: we experience time relative to how much of it we've already lived.

When you're five years old, one year is twenty percent of your entire life. That's huge. When you're fifty, one year is just two percent. It's a much smaller slice of your total experience, so it feels smaller.

Under this theory, time doesn't just feel faster as you age. It feels *proportionally* faster. The years between five and ten might feel as long, subjectively, as the years between forty and eighty.

It's an elegant idea, and it has a certain intuitive appeal. But many researchers think it's incomplete. After all, we don't usually measure a single afternoon against our whole lifetime. When you're stuck in a boring meeting, it doesn't feel shorter just because you're older. In fact, it can feel painfully long.

So the proportion theory might describe the pattern, but it doesn't really explain the mechanism. For that, we need to look at memory.

---

**[SECTION 2: NOVELTY AND MEMORY]**

Here's the key insight that changed how scientists think about this: the way we *judge* time looking back is different from the way we *experience* it in the moment.

Psychologists distinguish between two kinds of time perception. **Prospective time** is how long something feels while it's happening. **Retrospective time** is how long it feels when you look back on it.

And these two can be completely opposite.

Consider a vacation. Let's say you spend a week in a new country. Every day is packed with new sights, new food, new streets, new people. While you're there, the days seem to fly by because you're busy and engaged. But when you get home and look back, that week feels enormous. It feels like you were gone for a month.

Now compare that to a typical week of work. Wake up, commute, sit at a desk, come home, watch something, sleep, repeat. While you're living it, some moments can drag. But when you look back on it, the whole week collapses into almost nothing. What did you do last Tuesday? You probably can't say.

The writer and psychologist Claudia Hammond calls this the **"holiday paradox."** And it points to something important: our brains estimate how much time has passed based on how many distinct memories we've formed.

More new memories means time feels longer in retrospect. Fewer new memories means time feels shorter.

---

**[SECTION 3: WHY CHILDHOOD IS SO "LONG"]**

Now apply that to childhood.

When you're a kid, almost everything is new. The first time you ride a bike. The first time you see the ocean. Your first day of school, your first best friend, your first heartbreak. The world is an endless stream of novel experiences, and your brain is recording all of it in rich detail.

As we get older, that changes. We've seen the ocean before. We know how to drive. We've had hundreds of Mondays. Life becomes more familiar, more predictable, and more routine.

And routine is efficient. Your brain doesn't need to carefully encode the 4,000th time you brush your teeth. It compresses repeated experiences into a kind of general template: "This is what a normal day looks like." That saves mental energy, but it also means those days leave very little trace.

The result? When you look back on a year spent mostly in routine, there just isn't much there. Your brain has fewer markers to measure it by, so it concludes that the year must have been short.

This also connects to something researchers call the **"reminiscence bump."** When older adults are asked to recall their most vivid memories, a disproportionate number come from roughly ages ten to thirty. That's the period packed with firsts: first jobs, first relationships, first homes, first independence. Those years are densely recorded, so they loom large in our minds.

---

**[SECTION 4: THE BRAIN'S INTERNAL CLOCK]**

There's also a biological piece to this puzzle.

Some neuroscientists believe our sense of time is linked to the brain's internal processing speed and to chemical messengers like dopamine, which play a role in attention and timing. Research suggests that dopamine activity tends to decline gradually with age, and some scientists have proposed that this may subtly shift how we perceive the passage of time.

And then there's a more unusual theory from physics.

In 2019, Duke University engineering professor Adrian Bejan published a paper arguing that the answer lies in how quickly our brains process images. As we age, the nerves and neural pathways that carry visual information become longer and more complex, and signals can degrade and slow down. Bejan argued that as a result, we take in fewer "mental images" per unit of clock time.

He pointed to a familiar observation: babies' eyes move more frequently than adults' eyes do. If a child's brain is capturing more frames of reality per second, then, like a slow-motion camera, each moment would seem to contain more, and time would feel longer.

It's a bold theory, and not everyone agrees with it. But it adds another layer to the idea that how much information we process shapes how long time feels.

---

**[SECTION 5: THE PRESSURE OF A FULL LIFE]**

There's one more factor worth mentioning, and it's less about neuroscience and more about how we live.

Adults are busy. Really busy. Work, bills, family, errands, notifications, deadlines. When your attention is constantly pulled toward what's next, you spend less time fully present in what's happening now.

Studies by German psychologist Marc Wittmann and colleagues found something interesting: when people of different ages were asked about the speed of time, older participants were more likely to say the past *decade* had flown by. But when asked about shorter periods, like a week or a day, the age differences were much smaller. The real speeding-up seems to show up when we look back across long stretches of life.

In other words, it's not that each day goes faster. It's that the days blur together when we look back.

And time pressure plays a role too. People who feel rushed and overloaded often report that time is moving faster. When every hour is already claimed, the calendar starts to feel like it's racing ahead of you.

---

**[SECTION 6: HOW TO SLOW TIME DOWN]**

So here's the good news. If time feels fast because of routine and a lack of new memories, then we have some control over it.

You can't stop the clock. But you *can* change how your life feels when you look back on it.

**One: Seek out novelty.** Travel somewhere new, even if it's just a neighborhood you've never explored. Try a new recipe. Take a different route to work. Every new experience gives your brain something fresh to record.

**Two: Learn something new.** Pick up an instrument, a language, a sport, a craft. Learning forces your brain out of autopilot and creates rich, distinct memories.

**Three: Break up your routine.** You don't have to overhaul your life. Small changes add up. Make weekends feel different from weekdays. Plan small adventures. Create moments that stand out.

**Four: Pay attention.** Mindfulness isn't just a buzzword. When you're truly present, noticing the details, the colors, the sounds, the conversations, you encode more of your experience.

**Five: Record your life.** Keep a journal, take photos, write down one memorable thing from each day. Reflecting helps cement memories, making each year feel fuller when you look back.

---

**[CLOSING]**

The feeling that life is speeding up isn't just in your head. Well, technically it *is* in your head, but it's real, it's shared by almost everyone, and it's rooted in how our brains build our sense of time.

Childhood felt long because everything was new. Adulthood feels short because so much of it is familiar.

But that also means the solution is in our hands. The more we fill our lives with new experiences, curiosity, and attention, the longer and richer our lives will feel.

So maybe the question isn't "Where did the time go?" Maybe it's "What will I do to make this year one worth remembering?"
