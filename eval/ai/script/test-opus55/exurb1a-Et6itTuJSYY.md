# The Ants

**[COLD OPEN: Macro shot of a single ant climbing a blade of grass]**

**NARRATOR:** Right now, somewhere beneath your feet, a civilization is going about its business. It has farmers, soldiers, nurses, and engineers. It has highways and graveyards. And it has been doing this for more than a hundred million years.

**[TITLE CARD: THE ANTS]**

**NARRATOR:** There are an estimated twenty quadrillion ants on Earth. That's roughly two and a half million ants for every human alive. Put them all on a scale, and they'd outweigh every wild bird and wild mammal combined.

But numbers alone don't explain their success. To understand ants, you have to stop thinking about *the ant* and start thinking about *the colony*.

**[VISUAL: Animated cross-section of an underground nest]**

**NARRATOR:** A single ant isn't very smart. Its brain holds about a quarter of a million neurons. Yours has eighty-six billion. Yet a colony can solve problems that challenge computer scientists. It can find the shortest path to food, balance workloads, regulate temperature, and even decide when to move house.

How? No one is in charge. The queen doesn't give orders. She lays eggs. Instead, ants follow simple rules and communicate with chemicals.

**[VISUAL: Time-lapse of ants forming a trail]**

**NARRATOR:** When a forager finds food, she lays a pheromone trail on the way home. Others follow it and reinforce it. Shorter routes get traveled faster, so they build up more scent, and soon the whole colony is using the best path. There's no map and no plan. Thousands of tiny decisions add up to something that looks like intelligence.

Scientists call it *emergence*. Engineers borrowed it. Algorithms inspired by ants now help route delivery trucks and data across the internet.

**[SEGMENT TITLE: THE SPECIALISTS]**

**NARRATOR:** There are more than fourteen thousand known species, and they've found almost every way to make a living.

Leafcutter ants don't eat the leaves they carry. They chew them into mulch to grow fungus gardens. They were farming fifty million years before we were.

Army ants have no permanent home. Each night, workers link their bodies into a living nest called a *bivouac*, with the queen safe at its center.

Weaver ants stitch leaves together using silk from their own larvae, holding them like tiny glue guns.

And honeypot ants turn some of their sisters into living storage jars, swollen with nectar for the dry season.

**[VISUAL: Fire ants forming a raft on water]**

**NARRATOR:** When floods hit, fire ants grab onto one another and form a waterproof raft that can drift for weeks. No single ant decides to build it. The colony does.

**[SEGMENT TITLE: WHY IT MATTERS]**

**NARRATOR:** In many ecosystems, ants move more soil than earthworms. They spread seeds, recycle dead matter, and keep pests in check. Remove them, and whole landscapes change.

They aren't always a force for good. Invasive species like the Argentine ant have formed supercolonies stretching thousands of kilometers, crowding out native insects. Ants show us that cooperation is powerful, and that power cuts both ways.

**[CLOSING: Return to the single ant on the blade of grass]**

**NARRATOR:** So the next time you see one ant wandering across the sidewalk, remember that you aren't looking at an individual. You're looking at one cell of something much larger. It was thriving long before us, and it will likely be here long after we're gone.

The ants.
