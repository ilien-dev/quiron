# Polly: Nirvana's Darkest Song

**[COLD OPEN]**

*[Black screen. A single, out-of-tune acoustic guitar strums softly.]*

**NARRATOR:**
It's one of the quietest songs Nirvana ever recorded. No distortion. No screaming. Just a battered acoustic guitar, a hushed voice, and a few cymbal crashes.

And it might be the most disturbing thing Kurt Cobain ever wrote.

*[Title card: POLLY: NIRVANA'S DARKEST SONG]*

---

**[PART ONE: THE SONG HIDING IN PLAIN SIGHT]**

**NARRATOR:**
In September 1991, Nirvana released *Nevermind*. Within months it had knocked Michael Jackson off the top of the Billboard chart, and a scruffy band from Aberdeen, Washington became the most talked-about group on the planet.

Most people remember the album for "Smells Like Teen Spirit," "Come As You Are," and "Lithium." But tucked into the sixth slot, right after "In Bloom" and "Lithium," is a song that stops the record in its tracks.

"Polly" is under three minutes long. It sounds almost gentle, like a lullaby. A lot of listeners in 1991 sang along without ever really hearing what it was about.

Because "Polly" isn't a love song, and it isn't about a parrot, even though the lyrics mention crackers and a bird.

It's a song about a kidnapping. And it's sung from the point of view of the kidnapper.

---

**[PART TWO: THE TRUE STORY]**

*[Archival-style newspaper graphics. Tacoma, Washington, 1987.]*

**NARRATOR:**
In June 1987, a fourteen-year-old girl was heading home from a punk concert in Tacoma, Washington. She accepted a ride from a man named Gerald Friend.

She did not make it home that night.

Friend took her to his mobile home, where he held her captive and subjected her to horrific abuse. We won't go into the details here. They're documented in court records, and they're as bad as anything you can imagine.

What matters is what happened next. Later, while Friend was driving her somewhere in his truck, he stopped for gas. In that moment, she saw an opening. She escaped and ran for help. A passing driver stopped, and Friend was arrested. He was convicted and sentenced to a long prison term.

Her survival came down to courage, quick thinking, and a single moment when her captor let his guard down.

Kurt Cobain read about the case in the newspaper. And it stayed with him.

---

**[PART THREE: WHY WOULD ANYONE WRITE THIS?]**

**NARRATOR:**
Cobain wrote the song in the late 1980s, long before Nirvana was famous. The question people have always asked is simple: why would he write it from the attacker's perspective?

The answer says a lot about who Kurt Cobain was.

Throughout his short career, Cobain was outspoken about misogyny, homophobia, and sexual violence, at a time when those topics weren't a fashionable part of rock music. He called himself a feminist. He hated macho rock culture. He talked openly about wanting the meatheads who'd bullied him in high school to stop buying his records.

"Polly" doesn't glorify the attacker. It does something more uncomfortable. It puts you inside his head and makes you listen to how ordinary he sounds. He's calm. He's almost casual. He talks about his victim the way you'd talk about a pet, something to be fed, kept, and controlled. That's where the bird imagery comes from. To him, she isn't a person.

That's the horror of the song. There's no monster voice, no dramatic villain. Just a quiet, matter-of-fact man who thinks what he's doing is normal.

And then, near the end, the song turns. The narrator realizes that the girl is more resourceful than he thought. That's the real-life escape, folded right into the lyrics. The final word belongs to her survival, not to him.

---

**[PART FOUR: THE SOUND OF IT]**

*[Footage-style b-roll: Smart Studios, Madison, Wisconsin, 1990.]*

**NARRATOR:**
Musically, "Polly" is one of the oldest recordings on *Nevermind*. The version on the album came from sessions with producer Butch Vig at Smart Studios in Madison, Wisconsin, in 1990, before Dave Grohl joined the band. The drummer on the track is Chad Channing, and those sparse cymbal crashes are his.

Kurt played it on a cheap acoustic guitar he'd reportedly bought at a pawn shop for around twenty dollars. It was a twelve-string, but it only had five strings on it, and he barely kept it in tune. He supposedly never changed the strings.

Listen closely and you can hear it. The guitar is thin and slightly sour. Kurt's vocal is close-miked and almost conversational. There's a moment where his voice comes in a little early and he just keeps going.

Polished production would have ruined it. The rawness is what makes it feel like you're overhearing something you shouldn't be.

---

**[PART FIVE: "THE KID HAS HEART"]**

**NARRATOR:**
"Polly" earned respect from some unexpected places. One of the most repeated stories is about Bob Dylan. After hearing the song, Dylan reportedly said of Cobain, "The kid has heart."

Coming from the man who wrote "Hattie Carroll" and "Hurricane," songs that took real crimes and turned them into moral reckonings, that's a meaningful compliment. Whether or not every telling of the story is word-for-word accurate, the comparison fits. "Polly" belongs to the same tradition: true-crime songwriting that forces the listener to sit with injustice.

---

**[PART SIX: WHEN THE MESSAGE GOT LOST]**

*[Tone darkens. Slow push-in on the* Incesticide *album cover.]*

**NARRATOR:**
Here's where the story gets even darker.

In 1992, Nirvana released *Incesticide*, a compilation of rarities and B-sides. It included a faster, electric version of the song called "(New Wave) Polly."

But the most powerful thing about *Incesticide* wasn't the music. It was the liner notes.

In them, Cobain wrote about a horrifying incident he'd heard about: two men had raped a woman while singing the lyrics to "Polly."

He was devastated. A song he had written to expose the mindset of a predator had been used by predators. He didn't hide behind the art. He addressed it directly, in print, on his own album. He called out those men in furious, unsparing language. Then he told any fans who hated women, gay people, or people of color to stop listening to his music and leave his band alone.

It's one of the most direct statements a rock star at the peak of his fame has ever made to his own audience. And it shows that Cobain understood the risk of writing from a villain's point of view: some people will miss the point. He refused to let that slide.

---

**[PART SEVEN: UNPLUGGED]**

*[Candles. Lilies. A dark stage in New York, November 1993.]*

**NARRATOR:**
In November 1993, Nirvana recorded *MTV Unplugged in New York*. "Polly" was a natural fit. It was already acoustic and already haunting. In that setting, five months before Cobain's death in April 1994, it took on yet another layer of sadness.

Today, it's often heard through that lens. But it's worth remembering that "Polly" was never about Kurt's own pain. It was about someone else's. It was one of the rare times he aimed his songwriting outward, at a real crime and a real survivor.

---

**[OUTRO]**

**NARRATOR:**
So is "Polly" Nirvana's darkest song?

There are other contenders. "Something in the Way." "Rape Me," which Cobain wrote as an anti-rape song. Much of *In Utero*.

But "Polly" stands apart because its darkness isn't abstract. It's not about depression, fame, or Kurt's own demons. It's about a real night in Tacoma, a real man who did something unspeakable, and a real fourteen-year-old girl who refused to be his victim forever.

Cobain gave the attacker a voice so we could hear exactly how ugly it was. And he ended the song with her getting away.
