# The Falklands - MiniWars #1

**[COLD OPEN: Black screen. Wind howling. Fade in on a grey, rocky shoreline under a heavy sky.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Eight thousand miles from London, in the cold South Atlantic, there's a scattering of islands most people couldn't have found on a map in 1982.

About eighteen hundred people lived there. There were more sheep than humans, more penguins than sheep, and one main road.

In the spring of that year, two nations went to war over them. It lasted seventy-four days. It brought down a dictatorship, rescued a prime minister, and left more than nine hundred people dead.

This is the Falklands War.

**[TITLE CARD: MINIWARS #1 — THE FALKLANDS]**

---

## PART ONE: WHOSE ISLANDS?

**[VISUAL: Animated map of the South Atlantic, zooming in on the archipelago roughly 300 miles east of Argentina.]**

**NARRATOR:**
To understand why anyone would fight over these islands, you have to go back about two hundred years before the first shot.

In the 1760s, France and Britain both set up small settlements on the islands without knowing about each other. France sold its colony to Spain. Spain and Britain nearly went to war over the place in 1770, then backed down. Eventually everyone more or less left.

**[VISUAL: Flags appear and disappear over the islands in sequence: French, Spanish, British.]**

After Argentina won independence from Spain, it claimed it had inherited the islands. In the 1820s it sent a governor and settlers. Then, in 1833, a British warship arrived and expelled the Argentine authorities. Britain has held the islands ever since.

Argentina never accepted this. To Argentines, they are *Las Malvinas*, stolen territory, and generations of schoolchildren grew up learning that. To the islanders, nearly all of British descent, they were simply home.

**[VISUAL: Split screen — an Argentine classroom map labelled "Islas Malvinas", and a Falklands farmhouse flying a Union Jack.]**

Through the 1960s and 70s, the two governments talked at the United Nations. Britain quietly considered handing the islands over, perhaps leasing them back. But the islanders refused, and Parliament wouldn't force them. The talks went nowhere.

Then Argentina's government changed the equation.

---

## PART TWO: THE JUNTA'S GAMBLE

**[VISUAL: Archival-style footage of Buenos Aires. Military uniforms. Crowds.]**

**NARRATOR:**
In 1982, Argentina was ruled by a military junta led by General Leopoldo Galtieri. The regime was in deep trouble. Inflation was out of control. The economy was collapsing. And the government was responsible for the "Dirty War," in which thousands of Argentine citizens had been kidnapped, tortured and "disappeared."

People were starting to protest in the streets.

The junta needed something to unite the country behind it. Retaking the Malvinas would do exactly that.

And the timing looked good. Britain was cutting its defence budget. It had announced it would withdraw HMS *Endurance*, the only Royal Navy ship regularly patrolling the area. To the generals in Buenos Aires, it looked like Britain had stopped caring.

**[VISUAL: Text on screen — "Would Britain really fight for 1,800 people, 8,000 miles away?"]**

They bet that it wouldn't.

---

## PART THREE: INVASION

**[VISUAL: Night. Animated landing craft approaching a dark coastline.]**

**NARRATOR:**
In March 1982, Argentine scrap-metal workers landed on South Georgia, a remote British territory further east, and raised the Argentine flag. It was a spark that both sides watched closely.

Then, in the early hours of the second of April, Argentine special forces and marines came ashore near Stanley, the islands' capital.

Defending the Falklands were sixty-eight Royal Marines and a few volunteers. They fought for several hours, but against thousands of troops, the outcome was never in doubt. Governor Rex Hunt ordered them to surrender. No British personnel were killed. One Argentine officer was.

**[VISUAL: Iconic image recreation — Royal Marines face-down on a road, Argentine soldiers standing over them.]**

A day later, Argentine forces took South Georgia, though not before a small group of Marines shot down a helicopter and damaged a warship.

In Buenos Aires, crowds packed the Plaza de Mayo, cheering. Just days earlier, the same square had been full of protesters. Galtieri's gamble seemed to have paid off.

---

## PART FOUR: THE TASK FORCE

**[VISUAL: The House of Commons. Then Portsmouth harbour, ships departing, crowds waving.]**

**NARRATOR:**
In London, the news was a humiliation. Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher was unpopular, and her government looked caught completely off guard. Her foreign secretary resigned.

But the head of the Royal Navy, Admiral Sir Henry Leach, told her something crucial: a task force could be assembled, and it could retake the islands.

Thatcher said yes.

Within days, ships began leaving port. Two aircraft carriers, HMS *Hermes* and HMS *Invincible*. Destroyers and frigates. Submarines. And because Britain didn't have enough ships, it requisitioned civilian ones: ferries, tankers, container ships, even the cruise liners *Canberra* and *Queen Elizabeth 2*, converted to carry troops.

**[VISUAL: Animated map. A long dotted line stretches from Britain down to Ascension Island, then on to the Falklands.]**

The journey was enormous. The fleet staged through Ascension Island, a speck of British territory in the middle of the Atlantic, before pushing south into the winter.

Meanwhile, the diplomats tried. American Secretary of State Alexander Haig flew back and forth between London and Buenos Aires searching for a deal. Peru offered a peace plan. None of it worked. Neither side was willing to back down.

---

## PART FIVE: FIRST BLOOD

**[VISUAL: Icy waters. A grey submarine periscope.]**

**NARRATOR:**
On the twenty-fifth of April, British forces retook South Georgia, disabling an Argentine submarine in the process. The Argentine garrison surrendered without a fight.

Then the war started in earnest.

On the first of May, a single RAF Vulcan bomber flew nearly four thousand miles from Ascension Island, refuelled in the air again and again by a relay of tankers, and dropped bombs on Stanley's runway. It was codenamed "Black Buck," and for a time it was the longest bombing raid in history.

**[VISUAL: Animated flight path with multiple tanker refuelling points.]**

The next day came the war's most controversial moment.

The British submarine HMS *Conqueror* had been shadowing the Argentine cruiser *General Belgrano*, a Second World War-era ship originally built for the US Navy. On the second of May, *Conqueror* fired three torpedoes. Two hit.

The *Belgrano* sank. Three hundred and twenty-three Argentine sailors died. That was nearly half of all Argentine deaths in the entire war.

**[VISUAL: Silence. A slow pan across dark water.]**

The ship was outside Britain's declared exclusion zone and sailing away at the time, and that has been argued about ever since. Britain maintained it was a threat regardless. After the sinking, the Argentine navy largely stayed in port for the rest of the war.

Two days later, Argentina struck back.

A Super Étendard jet launched a French-made Exocet missile at the destroyer HMS *Sheffield*. The missile hit amidships. Fires spread through the ship. Twenty men were killed, and *Sheffield* later sank.

For the first time, the British public understood what modern war at sea looked like.

---

## PART SIX: BOMB ALLEY

**[VISUAL: Dawn over San Carlos Water. Landing craft heading for the beach.]**

**NARRATOR:**
On the twenty-first of May, British troops landed at San Carlos, on the western side of East Falkland, far from the main Argentine positions around Stanley.

The landings themselves went well. But the ships protecting them were sitting in a narrow stretch of water, and the Argentine Air Force came in fast and low.

**[VISUAL: Argentine Skyhawks and Daggers skimming over hills, bombs splashing around warships.]**

Argentine pilots flew with remarkable courage, at the very edge of their aircraft's range, under heavy fire. The British called the waters "Bomb Alley."

Over the next few days, the frigates *Ardent* and *Antelope* were sunk. The destroyer *Coventry* went down. And on the twenty-fifth of May, an Exocet hit the container ship *Atlantic Conveyor*, destroying almost all the heavy-lift helicopters on board.

That one loss changed the whole land campaign. Without helicopters, most British troops would have to cross East Falkland on foot.

But there was a catch in the Argentine attacks. Many of their bombs were dropped so low that the fuses didn't have time to arm. They struck British ships and failed to explode. Had more of them gone off, the war could have turned out very differently.

---

## PART SEVEN: GOOSE GREEN AND THE LONG WALK

**[VISUAL: Map highlighting the settlement of Goose Green, south of San Carlos.]**

**NARRATOR:**
On the twenty-eighth of May, the 2nd Battalion of the Parachute Regiment attacked the Argentine garrison at Goose Green.

It was a brutal, day-long fight across open, gorse-covered ground. The battalion's commander, Lieutenant Colonel H. Jones, was killed leading a charge against an Argentine position. He was awarded the Victoria Cross after his death.

By the next day, the Paras were outnumbered but had the garrison surrounded. More than nine hundred Argentine soldiers surrendered.

**[VISUAL: Troops with enormous packs marching across bleak moorland in rain.]**

Then came the march east. Royal Marines and Paras carried loads of up to a hundred pounds across peat bog, rock and freezing rain. The Marines called it "yomping." The Paras called it "tabbing." Either way, it was miserable.

On the eighth of June, disaster struck at Fitzroy. The landing ships *Sir Galahad* and *Sir Tristram* were caught unprotected in daylight while unloading troops. Argentine jets hit them both. Forty-eight men were killed, many of them Welsh Guards. It was the single worst day of the war for Britain.

---

## PART EIGHT: THE MOUNTAINS

**[VISUAL: Night. Silhouetted ridgelines around Stanley. Tracers and flares.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Stanley was ringed by mountains, and the Argentines held them all.

The British attacked at night. On the eleventh of June, they assaulted Mount Longdon, Two Sisters and Mount Harriet. Two nights later came Tumbledown, Wireless Ridge and Mount William.

This was close, savage fighting among rocks and crags, sometimes with bayonets. Argentine units, particularly their marines on Tumbledown, fought hard. Many other defenders were young conscripts, poorly supplied and frozen after weeks in the open.

**[VISUAL: Dawn breaking over captured high ground, Stanley visible below.]**

By the morning of the fourteenth of June, the British held the high ground. Argentine troops were streaming back into Stanley.

That evening, General Mario Menéndez surrendered to Major General Jeremy Moore.

The war was over.

---

## PART NINE: THE COST

**[VISUAL: Slow fade through memorials — Argentine, British, and the islands' own.]**

**NARRATOR:**
In seventy-four days, six hundred and forty-nine Argentines were killed. Two hundred and fifty-five British service personnel died. And three Falkland Islanders, all women, were killed, by British shellfire, in the last days of the war.

The consequences ran far beyond the islands.

In Argentina, the defeat destroyed the junta. Galtieri was gone within days. In 1983, Argentina returned to democracy, and members of the military government were later put on trial.

In Britain, Margaret Thatcher's popularity surged. A year later, she won a landslide re-election.

**[VISUAL: Present-day Stanley — colourful roofs, a quiet harbour.]**

Today, the Falklands are still British, with a permanent military garrison. In 2013, the islanders held a referendum. Of more than fifteen hundred votes, only three were against remaining a British territory.

And Argentina still claims the Malvinas. It's written into the country's constitution, and every year on the second of April, Argentines remember the soldiers who fought there.

**[VISUAL: Wind across an empty hillside. A single cross.]**

**NARRATOR:**
It was a small war over a small place. But for the people who fought it, and the families who lost someone, there was nothing small about it at all.
