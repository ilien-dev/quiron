# Is the Moon in Majora's Mask a Black Hole?

**[COLD OPEN. Black screen. The sound of a clock ticking. Fade in on a slow pan up Clock Town's tower, ending on the moon's grimacing face.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Three days. That's all you get. Seventy-two hours before a moon with a face like a nightmare drops out of the sky and wipes Termina off the map.

For over twenty-five years, players have been staring up at that thing and asking questions. Why does it have a face? Why is it falling? And why, when you finally get inside it, is it a peaceful green field with a single tree and a bunch of kids wearing masks?

Today I want to ask a stranger question. One that started as a joke in a comment section and then refused to leave my brain.

What if the moon in *Majora's Mask* isn't a moon at all?

What if it's a black hole?

**[TITLE CARD: "Is the Moon in Majora's Mask a Black Hole?"]**

---

## PART ONE: What Even Is a Black Hole?

**[VISUALS: Simple animated diagram of a star collapsing.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Before we start accusing a fictional moon of being a collapsed star, let's get our definitions straight.

A black hole is a region of space where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape. You get one when a huge amount of mass is squeezed into a small enough space. The boundary where escape becomes impossible is called the **event horizon**.

And here's the key thing: *any* mass can become a black hole if you compress it enough. There's a formula for exactly how small you'd need to crush something. It's called the **Schwarzschild radius**, and it looks like this:

**[ON SCREEN: r = 2GM / c²]**

**NARRATOR:**
*G* is the gravitational constant, *M* is the mass, and *c* is the speed of light. Plug in a mass, and it tells you how tiny that mass has to be before it seals itself off from the universe.

Some examples, just to calibrate your brain:

**[VISUALS: Comparison graphics for each.]**

- To turn the Sun into a black hole, you'd need to crush it down to about **3 kilometers** in radius. Smaller than most cities.
- To turn the Earth into one, you'd need to squeeze it down to about **9 millimeters**. The size of a marble.
- And our actual Moon? About **a tenth of a millimeter**. Roughly the width of a human hair.

**NARRATOR:**
So the bar is high. Really high. If the moon in Termina is a black hole, it isn't a small, cute one. It's carrying a *lot* of weight.

## PART TWO: The Evidence For

**[VISUALS: Detective corkboard aesthetic. Screenshots pinned up with red string.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Okay. Let's play prosecutor. What would make someone look at that moon and think "black hole"?

Honestly? More than you'd expect.

### Exhibit A: It's Bigger on the Inside

**[VISUALS: Link standing in the moon's interior field, endless grass, the lone tree.]**

**NARRATOR:**
When Link finally enters the moon, he doesn't find rock. He doesn't find a hollow shell. He finds a vast, open field that stretches out in every direction, with a sky, a tree, and children running in circles.

That interior is clearly far larger than the moon looks from the outside. And that's actually one of the weirdest, most real predictions of general relativity.

Inside a black hole, space and time get so warped that the usual rules of "inside" and "outside" stop making intuitive sense. Some physicists have pointed out that the *volume* inside an event horizon can, in certain mathematical descriptions, be enormous, far larger than you'd guess from the size of the horizon. There are even solutions to Einstein's equations, the famous Einstein–Rosen bridge, where the interior of a black hole connects to an entirely separate region of space.

A pocket universe hiding behind a boundary you can't see past?

That's... kind of the moon.

### Exhibit B: Time Is Broken

**[VISUALS: The three-day clock UI spinning. Link playing the Song of Time. Screen flashes white.]**

**NARRATOR:**
*Majora's Mask* is a game about time. You relive the same three days over and over. You can slow time down with the Inverted Song of Time. You can skip ahead with the Song of Double Time. Time in Termina is elastic.

Black holes do that too.

Near a massive object, time literally runs slower. This is called **gravitational time dilation**, and it isn't sci-fi. GPS satellites have to correct for it every day, because clocks in orbit tick slightly faster than clocks on the ground. Near a black hole, the effect becomes extreme. To a distant observer, someone falling toward the event horizon appears to slow down, redshift, and freeze, never quite crossing over.

**[VISUALS: The moon hovering inches above the Clock Tower in the final hours, motionless.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Think about the final moments of the three-day cycle. The moon hangs there, right above the tower, impossibly close, as though time itself is struggling to let it land. The countdown stretches. The world holds its breath.

If you squint, that looks a lot like the frozen fall of an object near an event horizon.

### Exhibit C: Information Survives the Reset

**[VISUALS: Link's inventory screen. Masks, songs, the Bombers' Notebook.]**

**NARRATOR:**
This one's my favorite.

Every time you reset the cycle, most of the world forgets. Rupees vanish unless you've banked them. Ammo disappears. Quest progress unravels. Everyone in Clock Town goes back to square one.

But some things survive. Your masks. Your songs. The notes in the Bombers' Notebook. Link's memories.

In physics, there's a famous problem called the **black hole information paradox**. When something falls into a black hole, is the information about it destroyed forever? Quantum mechanics says information can't truly be erased. General relativity seems to suggest it can. Stephen Hawking spent years wrestling with this, and physicists are still arguing about it.

**[VISUALS: Link playing the Song of Time; the world resets, but the mask icons remain.]**

**NARRATOR:**
*Majora's Mask* has its own version of the paradox. The universe collapses, and yet certain pieces of information slip through the reset intact. The masks are like little packets of preserved memory, the stories of people you've helped, carried across a boundary that should have destroyed them.

I'm not saying the developers were thinking about Hawking radiation. I'm saying the parallel is *weirdly* good.

## PART THREE: The Evidence Against

**[VISUALS: The corkboard. A hand rips the red string down.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Alright. Time to switch sides. Because as fun as this theory is, physics has some very strong objections.

### Objection One: Termina Would Already Be Gone

**[VISUALS: A math overlay on a screenshot of the moon over Clock Town.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Let's do the math. Let's be generous and say the moon in-game is about **one kilometer across**. Probably a lowball, but it makes the numbers easier.

If the event horizon were one kilometer wide, the Schwarzschild formula tells us it would need a mass of roughly **3.4 × 10²⁹ kilograms**. That's about **17% of the mass of the Sun**, crammed into something the size of a small town.

Now, what does that do to Clock Town?

Gravitational pull drops off with distance squared. If the moon is hovering about a kilometer above the tower, the gravitational acceleration at ground level would be around **two trillion times Earth's gravity**.

**[VISUALS: The Clock Town Carnival banners, frozen. Then a cartoonish "SPLAT" graphic.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Two. Trillion. G.

At that level, you don't get a dramatic falling moon cutscene. You don't get people panicking in the streets. You don't get the Deku Butler crying. Termina gets torn apart instantly. The ground, the oceans, the mountains, the atmosphere, all of it peels away and spirals upward.

And that's before we talk about **spaghettification**: tidal forces so strong that the part of your body closer to the black hole gets pulled harder than the part farther away, stretching you into a long, thin noodle. Link would not be standing on the Clock Tower playing his ocarina. Link would be pasta.

**NARRATOR:**
Honestly, you wouldn't even need it to be close. A black hole that massive anywhere in Termina's sky would wreck the planet's orbit, its tides, and eventually its entire existence. The fact that the townsfolk are mostly worried about whether the carnival is still on suggests the gravity situation is... fine.

### Objection Two: It's Not Black

**[VISUALS: The moon's glowing eyes, the visible craters, the lit surface.]**

**NARRATOR:**
This is the obvious one, but it matters. Black holes are, you know, black. Light can't escape them, so you don't see a surface. You see a shadow.

Now, a real black hole might not look *completely* dark. Matter swirling around it, called an **accretion disk**, can heat up to millions of degrees and glow brighter than entire galaxies. The famous image of the black hole in galaxy M87 is a ring of fire around a dark center.

But that's not what the moon looks like. It has a surface. It reflects light. It has *eyes* that glow. It has a mouth full of teeth. Whatever it is, it is very much an object you can see, which is the one thing a black hole isn't.

### Objection Three: You Can Leave

**[VISUALS: Link being returned to Clock Town at the end of the game. The moon fading away.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Here's the killer.

Link goes into the moon. And Link comes back out.

The whole defining feature of an event horizon is that it's a one-way door. Once you cross it, every possible path leads further in. There is no "out." You can't backflip your way across it. You can't use the Fierce Deity Mask to punch through it. Not even light gets to leave.

If the moon were a black hole, the final dungeon wouldn't be the climax of the game. It'd be the end of Link, permanently. The fact that he walks out with the credits rolling means, by definition, he never crossed an event horizon.

## PART FOUR: So What Is It?

**[VISUALS: Slow, quiet shot of the interior field. The moon child sitting beneath the tree.]**

**NARRATOR:**
So no. The moon in *Majora's Mask* isn't a black hole. Not in any physically meaningful sense.

But I don't think that's the end of the conversation. Because I think the reason this theory is so tempting is that the moon *functions* like a black hole, emotionally.

**[VISUALS: Montage of Termina's citizens — Anju waiting, the Gorons grieving, the Zoras in mourning.]**

**NARRATOR:**
A black hole is the ultimate point of no return. It's something that pulls everything toward it, that bends time around itself, that you can't look away from and can't escape. And everyone in Termina is living in the gravity well of that moon. Every conversation, every side quest, every desperate little act of kindness happens under its shadow.

The game is constantly asking: what do you do when the end is coming, and you can't stop it?

Some people run. Some people deny it. Some people get married anyway at dawn on the final day.

And Link, the only one who can step outside the countdown, gets to carry those stories with him. Like information slipping out of a black hole, those memories survive the collapse.

**[VISUALS: The moon interior, Link approaching the child under the tree.]**

**NARRATOR:**
And then you go inside. Past the boundary. Into the heart of the thing you've been dreading the entire game.

And it's not a void. It's a field. It's quiet. It's kids playing. It's a strange, sad, beautiful space where everything slows down.

Physics tells us we'll never know what's truly inside a black hole. Nothing that crosses the event horizon can ever tell us. *Majora's Mask* takes that same terrifying unknown and dares to imagine that, on the other side, there's something gentle waiting.

So, is the moon in *Majora's Mask* a black hole?

Scientifically? Absolutely not. It's too visible, too survivable, and way too polite about its gravity.

Thematically? It might be the closest thing gaming has to one.
