# How to Make Your Writing Funnier

*Educator: Cheri Steinkellner*

---

**[NARRATOR]**

A man walks into a library and asks for a book on paranoia. The librarian leans in and whispers, "It's right behind you."

Why is that funny? And can you learn to write like that? Comedy can seem like a gift some people are born with, but humor follows patterns. Writers for sitcoms, stand-up specials, and novels use the same tools again and again. Once you see them, you can use them too.

Let's start with the foundation of almost every joke: **surprise.**

A joke sets up an expectation and then breaks it. The setup leads the audience in one direction, and the punchline pulls the rug out. In the library joke, we expect the librarian to point to a shelf. Instead, she plays into the man's fear. The gap between what we expect and what we get is where the laugh lives.

To build that gap, you have to control where the surprise lands. That brings us to our first technique: **put the funny part last.**

Compare these two sentences:
"My uncle is terrified of Tuesdays and also clowns."
"My uncle is terrified of clowns, heights, and Tuesdays."

The second one works better because the strangest word comes at the very end. Once the audience hears the surprise, the joke is over. Anything after it gives them time to stop laughing. Comedy writers often rearrange a sentence a dozen times just to make sure the punch word is the final word.

You may have noticed something else in that example: it had three items. That's our second technique, **the rule of three.**

The first item sets a pattern. The second confirms it. The third breaks it. "I packed sunscreen, a towel, and my ex-wife's lawyer." Two items aren't enough to establish a pattern, and four starts to drag. Three is just right.

Our third technique: **be specific.**

Vague writing is rarely funny. "He ate a lot of food" is a fact. "He ate eleven hot dogs and then asked if there was dessert" is a picture. Specific details let the reader see the scene, and odd, precise choices are funnier than general ones. A dog is fine. A three-legged Pomeranian named Gerald is better. Certain sounds help too. Many comedy writers swear that words with hard consonants, like "k" and "p," land harder than soft ones. "Pickle" is funnier than "cucumber." Try it out loud.

Fourth: **exaggerate, then keep going.**

Take a small, true observation and stretch it until it snaps. Saying your apartment is small is a complaint. Saying it's so small you have to step outside to change your mind is a joke. Good exaggeration starts from something real, so the audience recognizes it, then pushes it past reason. In longer pieces, this is called **heightening**: each beat raises the stakes a little higher than the last, until the situation becomes absurd.

Fifth: **let character drive the comedy.**

The funniest moments in great sitcoms often aren't jokes at all. They're characters behaving exactly like themselves in situations that don't suit them. A neat freak stuck on a camping trip. A know-it-all who's confidently wrong. When readers understand who a character is, they start anticipating how that person will react, and the laugh comes from recognition as much as surprise. Give your characters strong wants, clear flaws, and a stubborn refusal to change.

Sixth: **use callbacks.**

A callback is a reference to an earlier joke that returns at an unexpected moment. It rewards the audience for paying attention and makes them feel like insiders. If Gerald the Pomeranian shows up in the final scene wearing a tiny tuxedo, readers who remember him will laugh harder than they did the first time.

Finally, the least glamorous tool of all: **revise.**

First drafts are rarely funny. Comedy depends on rhythm, and rhythm comes from cutting. Remove every extra word between the setup and the punchline. Read your work out loud. If you stumble, your reader will too. And test it on real people. Their laughter, or their silence, is the best editor you'll ever have.

Humor isn't magic. It's surprise, specificity, timing, and character, practiced until it feels effortless. So pick a moment from your own life, something small and true, and see how far you can stretch it.

And if anyone tells you comedy can't be taught, just whisper: "It's right behind you."
