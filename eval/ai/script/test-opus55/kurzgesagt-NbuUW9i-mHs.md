# Is War Over? — A Paradox Explained

**[COLD OPEN — black screen, sound of a ticking clock]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In 2011, a Harvard psychologist published an 800-page book with a bold claim: humanity is living through the most peaceful era in its history.

**[Cut to: news footage montage, tanks, drones, refugee convoys]**

Eleven years later, the largest land war in Europe since 1945 began. Today, researchers count more active armed conflicts than at any point since the Second World War.

So which is it? Is war fading away, or roaring back?

The answer is a paradox. And understanding it changes how you read every headline.

**[TITLE CARD: "IS WAR OVER?"]**

---

**[SECTION 1 — THE CASE FOR PEACE]**

**[Visual: animated line graph sloping downward across centuries]**

**NARRATOR:**
Let's start with the optimists. The psychologist was Steven Pinker, and his book, *The Better Angels of Our Nature*, made an argument backed by mountains of data.

In tribal societies, archaeologists estimate that a staggering share of people died violently, in some studies, more than one in ten. Medieval Europe was soaked in feuds, sieges, and dynastic wars. The 20th century gave us two world wars that killed tens of millions.

But after 1945, something strange happened.

**[Visual: map of Europe, borders freezing in place]**

The great powers stopped fighting each other directly. No American and Soviet armies clashed head-on. France and Germany, enemies for centuries, became partners. Historians call this "the Long Peace."

Measured as deaths per 100,000 people, battle deaths in the decades after the Cold War fell to some of the lowest levels ever recorded.

Pinker's explanation? Several forces working together. Strong states with a monopoly on force. Global trade that makes conquest less profitable than commerce. Nuclear weapons that make great-power war potentially suicidal. And a slow cultural shift that made violence less glorious and more shameful.

On paper, the trend looked unmistakable.

---

**[SECTION 2 — THE CASE AGAINST]**

**[Visual: the same graph, but zoomed out, with jagged spikes]**

**NARRATOR:**
Then came the critics, and their objection is subtle but powerful.

In 2016, statistician Nassim Nicholas Taleb and researcher Pasquale Cirillo argued that war doesn't behave like a smooth trend. It behaves like earthquakes.

Most years bring small conflicts. But every so often, a catastrophic one arrives, and that single event can outweigh everything that came before it. Statisticians call this a "fat-tailed" distribution.

**[Visual: a calm sea, then a sudden tsunami]**

Here's the problem. If a once-a-century catastrophe drives the overall numbers, then 70 years without one tells you very little. You might simply be living in the quiet stretch before the next spike.

Imagine a town that hasn't had a major flood in 70 years. Has the river become safer? Or has the town just been lucky?

From inside the calm, you genuinely can't tell.

---

**[SECTION 3 — THE PARADOX]**

**[Visual: split screen, left side "FEWER DEATHS," right side "MORE WARS"]**

**NARRATOR:**
And now the paradox sharpens, because recent data seems to support *both* sides at once.

According to the Uppsala Conflict Data Program, one of the world's most respected trackers, the number of active state-based conflicts has climbed to record highs in recent years, around sixty at a time.

But most of those are not the massive clashes of the past. They're civil wars, insurgencies, and conflicts involving armed groups: often grinding, often local, often internationalized as outside powers back different sides.

Meanwhile, the wars between states, the kind that once redrew maps, had become rare. Until Ukraine reminded the world they never truly disappeared.

**[Visual: icons multiplying across a world map]**

So we have more wars, but most are smaller. Fewer deaths over the long run, but the potential for catastrophe hasn't gone away. Both camps are looking at real data. They're just asking different questions.

Is the *average* person less likely to die in war than their ancestors? By most measures, yes.

Has humanity *escaped* the risk of devastating war? Absolutely not.

---

**[SECTION 4 — WHY IT MATTERS]**

**[Visual: a person reading news on a phone, headlines scrolling]**

**NARRATOR:**
Here's why this paradox matters to you.

If you believe peace is inevitable, you might assume the institutions that keep it, like alliances, treaties, trade, and diplomacy, will keep running on autopilot.

But peace isn't a law of physics. It's a structure that people built. And structures can be neglected, weakened, or torn down.

**[Visual: a bridge being maintained by workers]**

Think of the Long Peace less like a sunrise and more like a bridge. It's real. It holds a lot of weight. But it only stands as long as someone keeps maintaining it.

The optimists are right that progress happened. The skeptics are right that progress can be reversed. The mistake is believing either one tells the whole story.

---

**[CLOSING]**

**[Visual: slow zoom out from Earth at night, city lights glowing]**

**NARRATOR:**
So, is war over?

No. But it has changed. It has become less frequent between great powers, more fragmented, and in many places less deadly per person than in centuries past.

And yet the capacity for catastrophe is greater than ever.

That's the paradox. We may be living in the most peaceful era in human history and, at the same time, one bad decision away from the most destructive.

Which story turns out to be true isn't written in the data.

It's written in what we choose to do next.
