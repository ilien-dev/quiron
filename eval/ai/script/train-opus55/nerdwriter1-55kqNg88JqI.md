# How Emily Dickinson Writes A Poem

**[COLD OPEN]**

*[Visual: A sheet of old paper, close up. A pencil scratches out a single dash.]*

**NARRATOR:**
In 1862, a woman in Amherst, Massachusetts, mailed four poems to a stranger. She included a short note that asked one question:

"Are you too deeply occupied to say if my Verse is alive?"

The stranger was Thomas Wentworth Higginson, a literary critic. He didn't quite know what to make of what he'd received. The lines were short and strange. The punctuation looked like a mistake. The rhymes didn't rhyme.

Emily Dickinson would go on to write nearly eighteen hundred poems. Fewer than a dozen were published in her lifetime. Today she is considered one of the greatest poets in the English language.

So how did she actually do it? What does an Emily Dickinson poem look like from the inside? Let's take one apart.

*[Title card: HOW EMILY DICKINSON WRITES A POEM]*

---

**[PART ONE: THE SKELETON]**

*[Visual: A hymnal opens. Musical notation fades in.]*

**NARRATOR:**
Start with the beat. Most of Dickinson's poems are built on a rhythm she heard every Sunday: the hymn.

Hymnals of her day, especially those by Isaac Watts, used a pattern called *common meter*. It alternates a line of eight syllables with a line of six. Da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM, then da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM.

Listen:

> *Because I could not stop for Death –*
> *He kindly stopped for me –*

Eight syllables, then six. You could sing it to "Amazing Grace." People do, and it's a little unsettling.

That's the first trick. Dickinson takes the most familiar, comforting rhythm in her world, the sound of church, and fills it with things church didn't say out loud: doubt, death, ecstasy, terror. The form makes you relax. The content won't let you.

*[Visual: The hymn notation cracks down the middle.]*

---

**[PART TWO: THE RHYME THAT DOESN'T]**

**NARRATOR:**
Now look at the rhymes. Or the near-rhymes.

In a traditional hymn, "me" would rhyme with "see" or "free." Dickinson often goes somewhere close but wrong. Here's the end of "I heard a Fly buzz – when I died –":

> *And then the Windows failed – and then*
> *I could not see to see –*

Earlier in that poem she pairs "Room" with "Storm," and "Air" with "Heaves." These are *slant rhymes*. The vowels drift apart, or only the final consonant matches.

Critics in her time thought this was sloppiness. It wasn't. A perfect rhyme closes a door with a satisfying click. A slant rhyme leaves the door a little open. Your ear expects resolution and doesn't get it, and that small disappointment does emotional work. It's the sound of something not quite fitting, which is exactly what most of her poems are about.

She even described the strategy herself:

> *Tell all the truth but tell it slant –*
> *Success in Circuit lies*

---

**[PART THREE: THE DASH]**

*[Visual: A page of her handwriting. The dashes glow one by one.]*

**NARRATOR:**
And then there's the dash.

Open almost any Dickinson poem in her own handwriting and you'll see them everywhere. Some are long, some short. Some tilt up, some tilt down. Early editors found them so odd that they replaced them with commas and periods to make her look "correct."

Modern editions restored them, because the dash is doing something no comma can.

A period ends a thought. A comma organizes it. A dash interrupts it. It's a breath, a hesitation, a mind changing direction mid-sentence.

> *Hope is the thing with feathers –*
> *That perches in the soul –*

Read that with periods and it becomes a definition. Read it with dashes and it becomes someone thinking out loud, searching for the image as it arrives. The dash gives Dickinson's poems their texture of live thought. You aren't reading a conclusion. You're watching her arrive at it.

---

**[PART FOUR: THE CAPITAL LETTERS]**

**NARRATOR:**
You've probably noticed the capitals too. "Death." "Soul." "Windows." "Air."

Dickinson capitalized nouns freely, in a way that was already old-fashioned by the 1860s. It wasn't strictly consistent, but the effect is real. A capitalized word stands up off the page. Ordinary objects become almost like characters.

In "Because I could not stop for Death," Death isn't an event. He's a gentleman driving a carriage, taking her on a slow, polite ride past schoolchildren, fields of grain, and the setting sun, until they stop at a house that turns out to be a grave.

That's a core Dickinson move. She takes something enormous and abstract and makes it physical and close. Hope is a bird. Death is a suitor. Grief is a kind of weather. The capitals help turn ideas into presences.

---

**[PART FIVE: THE COMPRESSION]**

*[Visual: A long paragraph of text, squeezed down into four short lines.]*

**NARRATOR:**
Dickinson's poems are short. Many are just eight or twelve lines. But they're dense.

She leaves things out: articles, connecting words, explanations. She trusts you to make the leap. Here's a complete poem:

> *I'm Nobody! Who are you?*
> *Are you – Nobody – too?*

That's the opening, and in two lines she's already built a whole relationship: two outsiders recognizing each other, a little conspiratorial, a little funny. She doesn't describe loneliness. She just starts talking to you as if you share it.

This compression is why her poems reward rereading. On the first pass you get the music. On the third, you notice a word that means two things at once. On the tenth, a line you thought was simple turns out to be holding the whole poem up.

---

**[PART SIX: THE WORKSHOP]**

*[Visual: A small wooden desk by a window. Needle and thread. A stack of folded pages.]*

**NARRATOR:**
So what did her process actually look like?

We know more than you'd think, because she kept her drafts. Starting in the late 1850s, Dickinson copied her poems onto sheets of good stationery, stacked them, and sewed them together with thread into small booklets. Scholars call them *fascicles*. There are about forty.

These weren't for publication. They were her private archive, her own way of collecting and ordering her work.

And here's the fascinating part. In many of the fascicles, she didn't settle on a final version. Next to a word, she'd put a small cross. At the bottom of the page, she'd list alternatives. Sometimes three, four, five possible words for one spot in a line.

She often never chose. The poem lives in the fascicle with all its options still open.

Later in life her drafts got even looser. She wrote on whatever was around: the backs of envelopes, scraps of recipes, chocolate wrappers. Some late poems survive only as pencil fragments, shaped around the edges of a torn envelope flap.

For Dickinson, a poem wasn't a finished product to deliver to the world. It was something she kept living with, turning over, adjusting.

---

**[PART SEVEN: THE TEST]**

**NARRATOR:**
So how did she know when a poem was working?

Higginson eventually visited her in Amherst, in 1870. He wrote down some of what she said, and one line has become famous:

> *"If I feel physically as if the top of my head were taken off, I know that is poetry."*

Not "if it's correct." Not "if it rhymes." The test was in the body. A real poem should hit you physically, like a shock or a sudden drop.

Everything we've looked at serves that goal. The hymn meter lulls you. The slant rhyme unsettles you. The dash interrupts you. The capitals make abstractions loom. The compression forces you to leap. Each technique is a way of catching the reader off guard just long enough for the top of the head to come off.

---

**[OUTRO]**

*[Visual: The sewn fascicle closes. Slow push in on the window of a quiet house.]*

**NARRATOR:**
When Emily Dickinson died in 1886, her sister Lavinia found the poems: hundreds of them, in a locked chest, sewn and stacked and waiting.

She'd written almost entirely for herself. She asked a stranger, once, whether her verse was alive.

More than a century later, the answer is obvious.

So here's your challenge. Write eight lines in common meter. Eight syllables, then six. Use one slant rhyme. Use at least one dash where you'd normally put a period. Pick one ordinary noun and capitalize it, and treat it like a person.

Then read it out loud, and see if anything happens to the top of your head.

*[Beat.]*

Thanks for watching.
