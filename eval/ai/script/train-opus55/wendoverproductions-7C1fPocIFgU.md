# The Broken Economics of Organ Transplants

*[Runtime target: ~15 minutes]*

---

## COLD OPEN

**[B-ROLL: A hospital hallway at night. A pager buzzes on a nightstand.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Right now, more than a hundred thousand people in the United States are waiting for an organ. Most of them, nearly nine in ten, need a kidney.

Every day, roughly seventeen of them die before one arrives.

Most of the people reading the waitlist numbers assume the problem is simple: there aren't enough organs. People don't sign up to be donors. It's sad, but it's nobody's fault.

**[BEAT. Cut to black.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
That story is mostly wrong.

The shortage of organs isn't just a shortage of generosity. It's what happens when you take a good that is priceless, make its price legally zero, and then build a multibillion-dollar industry around it where everyone gets paid except the person who gave the organ.

This is the broken economics of organ transplants.

**[TITLE CARD]**

## PART ONE: THE PRICE OF ZERO

**[B-ROLL: Archival footage, 1980s Capitol Hill. Grainy congressional hearing.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In 1983, a Virginia physician named H. Barry Jacobs announced a plan to broker human kidneys. He would buy them from people, some of them from developing countries, and sell them to American patients for a fee.

The public reaction was immediate and furious. And Congress responded with the National Organ Transplant Act of 1984, which made it a federal crime to buy or sell a human organ for transplantation.

On its face, this was an easy moral call. Nobody wants a world where poor people are pressured into selling body parts to rich people. Nobody wants organs going to the highest bidder.

But economists have a way of looking at laws like this that's worth sitting with, even if you end up disagreeing.

**[GRAPHIC: A simple supply and demand chart. A horizontal line labeled "Price = $0" cuts well below where the curves intersect.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
When you set the price of something at zero by law, you get a shortage. That's not ideology. That's just what a price ceiling does. Demand stays high, supply stays low, and the gap gets filled by something else: waiting lists, rationing, and in some parts of the world, black markets.

The World Health Organization has estimated, though the number is old and hard to verify, that around one in ten kidney transplants worldwide involves trafficked organs. There's a global underground trade, centered in places with weak enforcement and desperate poverty, where sellers often receive a few thousand dollars, poor medical care, and no follow-up.

So here's the uncomfortable first point: banning payment didn't eliminate markets for organs. It pushed the worst version of one offshore.

**[BEAT]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
But the ban on payment has a stranger feature, and this is where it gets interesting.

It only applies to one person.

---

## PART TWO: EVERYONE GETS PAID BUT THE DONOR

**[B-ROLL: Operating room. Surgeons in gowns. A cooler labeled "HUMAN ORGAN FOR TRANSPLANT" being wheeled down a corridor.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Let's follow the money on a single kidney transplant in the United States.

The transplant surgeon gets paid. The anesthesiologist gets paid. The hospital gets paid. The organ procurement organization, the nonprofit that recovers organs from deceased donors, gets paid, and it bills transplant centers tens of thousands of dollars per organ for its acquisition costs. The courier who flies the cooler across the country gets paid. The pharmaceutical companies that make the immunosuppressant drugs the recipient will take for the rest of their life get paid.

**[GRAPHIC: A flowchart. Dollar signs flow to each box. One box, labeled "DONOR," shows "$0."]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Actuarial estimates put the total billed charges for a kidney transplant, from evaluation through the first months after surgery, at several hundred thousand dollars. For a heart, it's well over a million.

And the one person without whom none of this happens, the donor, receives nothing. Worse than nothing, in the case of living donors.

**[B-ROLL: A woman in her thirties sits at a kitchen table, going through bills.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
If you decide to give a kidney to a stranger, or even to your own sibling, you'll typically face weeks of recovery. You might lose wages. You'll pay for travel to the transplant center, maybe for lodging, maybe for childcare. Some donors have reported difficulty getting life insurance afterward.

There are programs that help. The National Living Donor Assistance Center reimburses some travel and, more recently, some lost wages, and federal rule changes in recent years have expanded who qualifies. A number of states offer tax deductions or paid leave for donors. These are real improvements.

But think about what the system asks of a living donor: undergo major surgery, accept a small but real lifetime risk, give the most valuable gift a human being can give, and then, ideally, break even.

When we design a system where the act of giving costs you money, we shouldn't be surprised when fewer people give.

## PART THREE: THE DIALYSIS PARADOX

**[B-ROLL: A dialysis clinic. Rows of reclining chairs, patients connected to machines.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
To understand why this shortage is so expensive, you have to understand what happens to the people who don't get a kidney.

They go on dialysis.

Dialysis is a remarkable technology. A machine filters your blood, doing a rough approximation of what your kidneys used to do. Most patients go to a clinic three times a week, for three or four hours each session, for years.

It keeps people alive. But it's a hard way to live. Patients on dialysis have substantially worse survival than comparable patients who receive a transplant. Many can't hold full-time jobs. Many describe it as a life organized around a machine.

**[GRAPHIC: "1972" appears on screen.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Here's a piece of history most people don't know. In 1972, Congress extended Medicare coverage to nearly every American with end-stage kidney failure, regardless of age. It's one of the only diseases singled out this way.

That decision saved enormous numbers of lives. It also created one of the most expensive line items in American healthcare.

Patients with kidney failure make up around one percent of Medicare beneficiaries, but they account for roughly seven percent of traditional Medicare spending. Dialysis costs on the order of a hundred thousand dollars per patient per year.

**[GRAPHIC: Two lines on a chart. "Dialysis" climbs steadily. "Transplant" spikes early, then flattens.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
A kidney transplant costs more up front. But after a few years, it's cheaper than staying on dialysis, and the recipient tends to live longer and work more.

Which leads to the paradox. Every kidney transplant that doesn't happen is not just a human tragedy. It's also, in cold fiscal terms, a loss for taxpayers. The government is already paying, through Medicare, hundreds of thousands of dollars to keep each waitlisted patient on dialysis.

Some economists and policy researchers have run the numbers and argued that the government could pay living donors tens of thousands of dollars each and still come out ahead, before you even count the value of the lives saved.

There's another wrinkle. The dialysis industry in America is dominated by two large companies. Their business depends on patients staying on dialysis. That doesn't mean anyone is plotting to keep people sick. But it does mean one of the most powerful commercial interests in kidney care has no financial stake in solving the shortage.

## PART FOUR: THE ORGANS WE THROW AWAY

**[B-ROLL: A medical waste bin. A clipboard. A clock ticking.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
If the shortage were purely about supply, you'd expect every available organ to be used.

It isn't.

In the United States, roughly one in four kidneys recovered from deceased donors ends up discarded. Not transplanted into anyone. Thrown away.

Some of those kidneys genuinely aren't usable. But studies comparing the U.S. to countries like France have found that many kidneys American centers discard would likely have been transplanted there, often successfully.

**[BEAT]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Why would a hospital turn down a kidney when people are dying on the waitlist?

Part of the answer is incentives.

For years, transplant centers were evaluated heavily on their one-year survival outcomes. If a center transplants a riskier, older, or less-than-perfect kidney and the outcome is worse, its statistics suffer. If the statistics suffer enough, regulators can come after the program.

But if a center declines a kidney, there's no metric for that. The patient who stays on the waitlist and dies doesn't show up in the center's transplant outcome numbers.

**[GRAPHIC: Two doors. One labeled "Accept risky kidney → Your stats could suffer." One labeled "Decline → Nobody's counting."]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
You've built a system where saying no is safe and saying yes is risky, for the institution. For the patient, it's exactly the opposite.

Regulators have recognized this and have been adjusting the metrics to account for waitlist outcomes, not just post-transplant survival. But the culture of caution runs deep.

Then there are the logistics. A deceased-donor kidney can only survive outside the body for so long. Organs get offered down long lists of potential recipients, with offers often made in the middle of the night, and every hour spent waiting for a decline is an hour the organ is degrading. Investigations have documented organs being lost in transit, delayed on commercial flights, or simply left too long.

## PART FIVE: THE MIDDLEMEN

**[B-ROLL: An office building. A sign reading "Organ Procurement Organization."]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In the United States, deceased-donor organ recovery is handled by a network of fifty-some organ procurement organizations, or OPOs. Each one holds a government-granted monopoly over its geographic region.

If you die in a hospital in a given area, one specific OPO is responsible for evaluating you as a potential donor, talking with your family, and recovering your organs. No other organization can step in.

For decades, these OPOs largely reported their own performance metrics. And critics, including researchers, a bipartisan group of senators, and the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, pointed out that the self-reported numbers made it nearly impossible to tell good performers from bad ones.

When outside researchers used independent data, like death records, to estimate how many potential donors each region actually had, the gap between the best and worst OPOs was enormous. Some regions recovered organs at several times the rate of others with similar populations.

**[GRAPHIC: A map of the U.S. divided into OPO regions, color-coded from green to red.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
If every OPO performed at the level of the top performers, by some estimates, thousands more organs a year would be transplanted.

In 2020, the federal government finalized new rules that measure OPOs using objective data and, for the first time, set up a process to decertify the worst performers. The first round of consequences under that system is arriving in this recertification cycle. The national transplant network contract, long held by a single nonprofit, has also been broken up and opened to competition.

Whether these reforms work is still an open question. But the fact that it took this long tells you something. When a monopoly isn't measured, it doesn't have to improve.

## PART SIX: WHAT OTHER COUNTRIES DO

**[B-ROLL: A sunny plaza in Madrid.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
So what does a system that works look like?

Spain is the world leader in deceased organ donation, with rates around double those of many wealthy countries. People often attribute this to Spain's "opt-out" law, where citizens are presumed to be donors unless they say otherwise.

But Spanish officials themselves have said the law isn't the main reason. Spain passed its opt-out law in 1979, and donation rates didn't take off until a decade later, when the country built a national organization focused on the logistics: trained transplant coordinators in hospitals, careful identification of every potential donor, and skilled, compassionate conversations with grieving families.

In other words, Spain fixed the operations, not just the paperwork.

**[B-ROLL: Tehran street scene.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Then there's the most controversial example: Iran.

Since 1988, Iran has allowed legal, regulated payment for living kidney donors. The government provides compensation, and donors often receive additional payment arranged through a nonprofit. Iran reportedly reduced its kidney waitlist dramatically within about a decade.

It's not a model anyone should copy uncritically. Researchers have raised serious concerns about the donors: most come from poorer backgrounds, some report regret and financial hardship afterward, and follow-up care has been inconsistent. Those concerns are real, and they're exactly what opponents of payment worry about.

But Iran is also the only country that's tried this at scale, and it's the only country that is widely reported to have come close to eliminating its kidney waitlist.

## PART SEVEN: THE CLEVER WORKAROUNDS

**[B-ROLL: A whiteboard covered in arrows connecting names.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Between "pay donors" and "do nothing," there's a middle path that's quietly saved thousands of lives.

Imagine you want to give your kidney to your husband, but you're not a blood match. Somewhere else, a woman wants to give her kidney to her brother, and she's not a match either. But you match her brother, and she matches your husband.

Swap them, and two people get kidneys.

This is a kidney exchange. And economists, including Alvin Roth, who shared the 2012 Nobel Prize in Economics partly for this work, designed the matching algorithms that make large-scale exchanges possible.

**[GRAPHIC: A chain of donors and recipients lighting up one by one.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The real magic happens with altruistic donors, people who give a kidney to a stranger with no one in particular in mind. One altruistic donor can start a chain: their kidney goes to a recipient whose incompatible partner then donates to the next person, and so on. Some chains have run for dozens of transplants.

It's a beautiful example of economics without money. When you can't use prices, you can still use clever design to match supply and demand more efficiently.

But exchanges only reshuffle the donors we already have. They don't create more of them.

---

## PART EIGHT: THE CASE FOR AND AGAINST PAYING DONORS

**[B-ROLL: A debate stage, empty. Two podiums.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
So we arrive at the hardest question. Should living donors be paid?

The case for is straightforward. People are dying. The government already pays for dialysis. Everyone else in the system is compensated. A regulated program, where the government, not the recipient, pays a fixed amount to donors, would avoid the "highest bidder" problem, allocate organs by medical need just as today, and could pay for itself.

Proposals along these lines have circulated in Congress, including one that would give donors a refundable tax credit spread over several years, and they've attracted support from economists, some transplant surgeons, and many patients.

**[BEAT]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The case against is also serious.

Critics worry that payment inevitably targets the poor. Fifty thousand dollars is a lot more persuasive to someone drowning in debt than to someone who's comfortable. Is that a free choice, or is it coercion with extra steps?

Others worry about "crowding out": that putting a price on donation could make it feel transactional and reduce altruistic giving, a fear rooted in a famous argument about paid versus unpaid blood donation, though the evidence on organs specifically is mixed.

And there's a deeper worry, one that's harder to put a number on: that some things should simply not be for sale, and that the human body is one of them.

**[B-ROLL: The woman from earlier, now walking out of a hospital, hand on her side.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Where you land on this probably depends on your values more than the data. But there's one thing nearly everyone can agree on: no donor should end up worse off for giving. Covering every dollar of lost wages, travel, childcare, and long-term health care for living donors is not "buying an organ." It's simply refusing to punish generosity.

And we are not there yet.

## CLOSING

**[B-ROLL: Return to the opening hospital hallway. Daylight now.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The organ shortage is often presented as a tragedy of nature. Not enough people die in the right circumstances. Not enough people check the box on their driver's license.

But much of it is a tragedy of design.

We've built a system where institutions are punished for taking chances on organs and rewarded for playing it safe. Where regional monopolies went decades without real measurement. Where the industry treating kidney failure profits from the status quo. Where every participant gets paid except the one who makes it all possible.

None of these are laws of physics. They're choices. And choices can be changed.

**[BEAT]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
If you want to do something today, register as a donor where you live, and more importantly, tell your family. In most places, they'll be the ones asked when the moment comes.

And if you've ever wondered whether you could be a living donor, the information is out there, and so are the people who need you.

Seventeen people will die today waiting.

The economics doesn't have to stay broken.

**[FADE TO BLACK.]**
