# The Mayans Ran Out of Stone, Not Time

**[COLD OPEN]**

December 21st, 2012. Millions of people wait for the world to end. Doomsday bunkers sell out. A Hollywood blockbuster has already shown the planet cracking in half.

And then... Saturday happens.

So what went wrong? Nothing. The Maya calendar never predicted the end of anything. To see why, we have to look at a very different ending: the moment the Maya stopped carving dates into stone.

**[TITLE CARD]**

**[SECTION 1: THE ODOMETER]**

First, a quick note. Scholars usually say "Maya" rather than "Mayan" when talking about the people. We'll use "Maya" from here on.

The Maya used several interlocking calendars, but the famous one is the Long Count. Think of it like a car's odometer. It counts days in nested units: the k'in is one day, the winal is twenty days, the tun is about a year, the k'atun is about twenty years, and the b'ak'tun is roughly 394 years.

On December 21st, 2012, the count rolled over to 13.0.0.0.0, the completion of the thirteenth b'ak'tun. That was a big, round, ceremonial number. It was not a deadline.

When your odometer hits 100,000 miles, your car doesn't explode. It just keeps going.

**[SECTION 2: THE EVIDENCE]**

We know the Maya expected time to continue, because they told us so.

At Palenque, an inscription in the Temple of the Inscriptions projects forward to a date in the year 4772. The Maya also had units bigger than the b'ak'tun, which let them count across spans far longer than the entire history of the universe as we know it.

In 2012 itself, archaeologists at Xultun in Guatemala uncovered painted calculations on a wall that track astronomical cycles thousands of years into the future.

Of all the inscriptions we have, only a couple even mention the 2012 date. One is Tortuguero Monument 6, and it describes a deity's appearance. It says nothing about destruction.

**[SECTION 3: THE REAL ENDING]**

So if time didn't end, what did?

Between roughly 800 and 900 CE, the great lowland Maya cities began to fall apart. Researchers point to a tangle of causes: severe droughts, endless warfare between rival kingdoms, deforestation, and political systems that couldn't adapt.

Kings stopped commissioning monuments. Scribes stopped carving Long Count dates. The last known one, at Toniná in Mexico, corresponds to 909 CE.

The calendar didn't run out. The stone did. Or more precisely, the people and the power needed to raise it.

**[CLOSING]**

And here's the part the doomsday crowd always skipped: the Maya never disappeared. Millions of Maya people live today in Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, and Honduras. They speak Maya languages, and some communities still keep the traditional 260-day calendar.

The world didn't end in 2012. Neither did the Maya.

They just stopped writing the dates down in stone.
