# Michael Bay: What is Bayhem?

**[COLD OPEN]**

*[Black screen. A single low rumble builds.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
There's a shot you've seen a hundred times, even if you've never noticed it.

*[CLIP: Bad Boys II, the slow-motion low-angle circle around Mike and Marcus as they rise into frame.]*

Two characters stand up. The camera sits low, looking up at them, and swings around in a slow, sweeping arc. The sun hangs behind them, orange and enormous. Slow motion. Music swells.

It lasts maybe four seconds. But in those four seconds, you know exactly whose movie you're watching.

*[Title card: MICHAEL BAY — WHAT IS BAYHEM?]*

---

**[PART ONE: THE WORD]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
"Bayhem." It's a word that gets thrown around as a punchline, a compliment, or an insult, depending on who's talking. Critics use it to describe everything they hate about modern blockbusters. Fans use it to describe everything they love. Bay has embraced the term too, and it's become something close to a brand.

But if you ask people what it actually *means*, you usually get one answer: explosions.

And sure, there are explosions. Lots of them.

*[MONTAGE: fireballs from The Rock, Armageddon, Transformers, Ambulance.]*

But explosions alone don't make a style. Plenty of directors blow things up. What makes a Michael Bay film feel like a Michael Bay film isn't *what* gets destroyed. It's *how* the camera sees it.

So let's take it apart. Let's figure out what Bayhem really is, piece by piece.

---

**[PART TWO: WHERE IT CAME FROM]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
To understand Bay's films, you have to start before the films.

*[Archival-style graphics: 1990s music video aesthetic.]*

In the late '80s and early '90s, Bay was directing music videos and commercials, working with Propaganda Films, a company that was a launching pad for a whole generation of visually aggressive directors. One of Bay's best-known commercials is the 1993 "Got Milk?" spot, where a history buff can't say "Aaron Burr" because his mouth is full of peanut butter.

That's important. Commercials and music videos teach a very specific skill: how to make an image grab you instantly. You don't have two hours to build atmosphere. You have thirty seconds. Every frame has to sell something.

Bay carried that philosophy straight into features. *Bad Boys* in 1995. *The Rock* in 1996. *Armageddon* in 1998. And the instinct never changed: every shot should feel like the most expensive, most exciting shot in the movie.

The question is how you actually *do* that. And the answer is a set of techniques Bay has used, refined, and stacked on top of each other for three decades.

---

**[PART THREE: THE TOOLKIT]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Let's start with the shot from the opening.

*[On-screen text: 1. THE HERO SHOT]*

The low-angle circle. The camera starts low and wide, then tracks around its subjects while they rise, turn, or simply stand there looking determined. Often it's in slow motion. Often there's a sunset.

*[CLIPS: Armageddon crew walking toward the shuttle; Transformers soldiers; Pain & Gain.]*

Why does it work? Low angles make people look powerful. Circular movement makes the background spin behind them, so the world literally revolves around these characters. And slow motion tells the audience: *this moment matters.* It's an image built to say "hero" with no dialogue at all.

Bay uses it for astronauts, soldiers, cops, and giant robots. In *Pain & Gain*, he uses it for bodybuilders committing deeply stupid crimes, and it becomes a joke about how these guys see themselves.

*[On-screen text: 2. MOVEMENT ON MOVEMENT]*

Next: Bay almost never lets the camera sit still. But more than that, he layers motion. The camera moves one direction, the subject moves another, and something in the foreground passes between them.

*[CLIP: a chase sequence; camera dollying left while a car moves right, a pole whipping through the foreground.]*

This creates a sense of speed that has very little to do with how fast anything is actually going. Your eyes are reading motion in three layers at once. Even a dialogue scene can feel like a chase.

Foreground objects are a big part of this. Bay loves putting things between the camera and the subject: a fence, a flag, a passing body, a lens flare. They give the frame depth, and when they slide past, they give it energy.

*[On-screen text: 3. THE GOLDEN HOUR (ALL DAY LONG)]*

Then there's the light. Bay's films are drenched in what cinematographers call golden hour, that warm, low-angled light right before sunset. Except in a Bay movie, it often feels like golden hour lasts all day.

*[CLIPS: Pearl Harbor, Transformers, Bad Boys II skylines.]*

Pair that with deep teal shadows, and you get the color palette that defined a whole era of blockbusters: orange skin tones against blue-green backgrounds. They're complementary colors, so they pop against each other. It's simple color theory, pushed as hard as it'll go.

*[On-screen text: 4. THE CUT]*

And finally, the editing. Bay's films cut fast. Very fast. Action scenes can feature dozens of shots in under a minute, frequently from wildly different angles.

*[CLIP: Transformers: Revenge of the Fallen battle sequence, played with a shot counter ticking upward.]*

This is where Bay draws the most criticism, and it's worth being honest about it. In the busiest sequences, especially in the later *Transformers* films, it can be genuinely hard to tell what's happening, who's where, or which robot is which.

But here's the thing: Bay isn't really trying to show you geography. The goal isn't clarity. It's *sensation.* The cuts aren't explaining the action. They're hitting you with it.

Which brings us to the heart of it.

---

**[PART FOUR: THE PRINCIPLE]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Every one of these techniques does the same thing. They make the image feel *bigger* than it is.

The low angle makes people bigger. The layered movement makes speed bigger. The golden light makes the world bigger, warmer, more mythic. The cutting makes the energy bigger.

*[CLIP: Armageddon's slow walk; hold on it.]*

That's Bayhem. Not explosions. *Scale.* It's the idea that every frame should feel epic, every moment should feel like a climax, and the audience should never, ever be allowed to relax.

And this is also why the style divides people so sharply.

When everything is a climax, nothing is. If every shot is a hero shot, the real hero moments don't stand out. If every scene is at maximum intensity, there's no contrast, no breathing room, no build. Watching a three-hour Bay film can feel exhilarating for an hour, and exhausting by the third.

*[Beat. Quiet music.]*

But there's another way to look at it.

---

**[PART FIVE: THE CASE FOR BAY]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
It's easy to laugh at Michael Bay. It's a lot harder to do what Bay does.

Coordinating those shots requires enormous technical skill. Multiple cameras, real vehicles, real pyrotechnics, real military hardware, all timed so the camera, the actors, and the fireball hit their marks in the same instant. Bay is famous for preferring practical effects wherever possible, and for working at a relentless pace on set.

*[CLIP: Ambulance, the drone shot diving down a building and racing through traffic.]*

Look at *Ambulance* from 2022. Bay used FPV drones, the small, agile racing kind, to swoop down skyscrapers and thread between moving cars. It's a new tool, but it's used with the same old instinct: more movement, more speed, more scale. Even after thirty years, Bay keeps chasing the next way to make an image feel impossible.

And the style has been enormously influential. You can see Bay's fingerprints across action cinema, music videos, car commercials, and video game trailers. The teal-and-orange grade, the sweeping hero shot, the sunset silhouette: these became part of the visual language of the 2000s and 2010s. Even directors who'd never admit it borrowed from the toolkit.

*[On-screen text: "Bayhem" didn't just describe Bay's films. It described an era.]*

---

**[PART SIX: STYLE AS SUBSTANCE]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
So is Bayhem good or bad?

Maybe that's the wrong question. A better one might be: *what is the style for?*

*[CLIP: The Rock, a quieter moment between Goodspeed and Mason.]*

In *The Rock*, the style serves a fun, propulsive thriller with characters you actually like. In *Pain & Gain*, it becomes satire: the glossy, heroic imagery is exactly how these deluded criminals see their own lives, and the gap between that image and reality is the joke. In *13 Hours*, it's applied to a real-world tragedy, and audiences and critics argued over whether that heroic framing fit the material.

The techniques are the same in all three. What changes is whether the story *earns* them.

That's the real lesson of Bayhem, and it applies to every filmmaker. Style isn't neutral. A low-angle slow-motion circle *means* something. It says, "these people are heroes, and this moment is huge." When that's true, it's thrilling. When it isn't, the audience feels the gap.

---

**[OUTRO]**

*[Return to the opening shot: low angle, slow circle, sunset.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Love it or hate it, Michael Bay has something most directors spend their whole careers searching for: a style so distinct that it has its own name. You can recognize it in a single frame. You can recognize it with the sound off.

Bayhem isn't just chaos. It's a carefully engineered system for making images feel enormous, built from low angles, layered motion, golden light, and relentless cutting, all serving one goal: making sure you never look away.

And whatever you think of the results, it's hard to argue it doesn't work.

*[The camera completes its circle. The sun flares. Cut to black.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Thanks for watching.
