# WW1 – Oversimplified (Part 1)

**[OPENING: A peaceful map of Europe, 1914. Birds chirp. Tiny flags wave.]**

**NARRATOR:** Europe, 1914. A place of beautiful cities, proud empires, impressive mustaches, and enough political tension to make a family dinner look relaxing.

For decades, the great powers had been building armies, making alliances, and competing for influence. Everyone insisted they wanted peace, but they also kept pointing increasingly large weapons at one another.

Then, in June 1914, one assassination set off a chain reaction that dragged much of the world into war.

How did Europe get from one murdered archduke to millions of soldiers fighting in muddy trenches? Well, it’s complicated.

**[The map catches fire.]**

Let’s make it slightly less complicated.

## EUROPE’S FRIEND GROUPS

**[Europe appears as a classroom. Countries sit at desks.]**

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Europe’s major powers had divided themselves into two loose camps.

On one side was the **Triple Alliance**: Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy.

**GERMANY:** We’re a team!

**ITALY:** Absolutely. Unless circumstances change.

On the other side was the **Triple Entente**: France, Russia, and Britain. Their arrangements weren’t all the same kind of binding military alliance, but they had grown increasingly close.

**FRANCE:** We have friends now.

**GERMANY:** Why do your friends live on both sides of me?

Germany had become a powerful industrial and military state after its unification in 1871. It also wanted greater influence abroad. Britain, meanwhile, ruled a vast empire and depended on its enormous navy.

Then Germany began expanding its fleet.

**BRITAIN:** Are those battleships?

**GERMANY:** They’re for protection.

**BRITAIN:** From whom?

**GERMANY:** Whoever has the biggest navy.

**[Britain looks directly at the camera.]**

France had its own reason to distrust Germany. In 1871, after defeating France in the Franco-Prussian War, Germany took the territories of Alsace and Lorraine.

**FRANCE:** I would like those back.

**GERMANY:** I would like you to stop saying that.

Meanwhile, Austria-Hungary and Russia were both interested in the Balkans, a region in southeastern Europe where the declining Ottoman Empire had left a struggle for territory and influence.

This was especially dangerous because Austria-Hungary was an empire made up of many different peoples, some of whom wanted greater independence. Next door, Serbia hoped to unite South Slavs, including many living under Austro-Hungarian rule.

Austria-Hungary considered Serbia a threat. Russia considered itself Serbia’s protector.

**AUSTRIA-HUNGARY:** Serbia is causing trouble.

**RUSSIA:** Serbia is my friend.

**SERBIA:** I am causing some trouble.

## THE ASSASSINATION

**[A calendar flips to June 1914.]**

Archduke Franz Ferdinand was heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne. He planned a visit to Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia, which Austria-Hungary had annexed in 1908.

Several young Bosnian Serb nationalists wanted to end Austro-Hungarian rule in the region. Armed with weapons supplied through Serbian nationalist contacts, they planned to assassinate him.

On June 28, the archduke and his wife Sophie rode through Sarajevo in an open car. Along the route, one conspirator threw a bomb.

It bounced off their vehicle and exploded behind them, injuring members of the procession and bystanders. The archduke escaped.

**FRANZ FERDINAND:** Well, that was alarming.

The visit continued, but later, after a reception, the archduke decided to visit the wounded in hospital. His driver took a wrong turn. As the car slowed, a conspirator named Gavrilo Princip saw his chance.

He approached and fired two shots, killing Franz Ferdinand and Sophie.

**[The screen goes quiet.]**

The assassination horrified Europe. Austria-Hungary’s leaders also saw an opportunity to confront Serbia, which they blamed for supporting the nationalist threat. They were determined to act, but first they wanted reassurance from their ally.

Germany gave Austria-Hungary strong backing—often called the **“blank cheque.”**

**AUSTRIA-HUNGARY:** What if Russia gets involved?

**GERMANY:** We’ll support you.

**AUSTRIA-HUNGARY:** Even if this becomes a much bigger—

**GERMANY:** Yes.

**[Germany signs a very large cheque.]**

## THE JULY CRISIS

Nearly a month after the assassination, Austria-Hungary sent Serbia an ultimatum: a list of demands with a deadline of just forty-eight hours.

Serbia accepted most of them, but objected to parts that would allow Austro-Hungarian officials to take part in investigations on Serbian territory.

**SERBIA:** We agree to almost everything. Perhaps we could discuss the rest?

**AUSTRIA-HUNGARY:** No.

On July 28, Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia.

Russia began mobilizing its army. Its leaders wanted to support Serbia and prepare for a possible wider conflict.

Germany saw Russian mobilization as an immediate danger and demanded that Russia stop. When it didn’t, Germany declared war on Russia on August 1.

France, allied with Russia, began mobilizing too. Germany declared war on France on August 3.

**[Countries begin standing up from their desks.]**

**BRITAIN:** Everyone sit down.

Germany’s military planning called for a rapid attack against France before turning its full attention east against Russia. To reach France, German forces invaded neutral Belgium.

Britain had pledged to uphold Belgian neutrality and also feared German domination of Europe. On August 4, Britain declared war on Germany.

**[The classroom is now a battlefield. Italy remains seated.]**

**GERMANY:** Italy, are you coming?

**ITALY:** You see, our alliance was defensive, and this situation is somewhat—

**AUSTRIA-HUNGARY:** You’re not coming, are you?

**ITALY:** Not today.

Italy stayed neutral in 1914. It would join the war on the Allied side the following year.

## THE WESTERN FRONT

Germany hoped to defeat France quickly. Its armies swept through Belgium, committing atrocities against civilians and encountering resistance that helped delay their advance.

British troops joined the French in trying to stop them, but the Allies were pushed back. By early September, German forces were approaching Paris.

**PARIS:** This seems bad.

Then French and British forces counterattacked near the Marne River. In the **First Battle of the Marne**, they halted the German advance and forced a retreat.

**GERMANY:** Fine. We’ll go around them.

**FRANCE:** We’ll go around you.

Both sides tried to outflank the other, moving northward in a series of battles. Eventually, continuous lines of trenches stretched from the Swiss border to the North Sea.

There was no easy way around anymore.

**[Two soldiers peer over opposite trenches.]**

**SOLDIER ONE:** Should we charge?

**SOLDIER TWO:** Into the machine guns?

**SOLDIER ONE:** Good point.

Artillery, machine guns, rifles, and barbed wire made attacking extremely costly. Soldiers dug deeper and fortified their positions. Between the opposing trenches lay **no man’s land**, a stretch of exposed ground that could be torn apart by shells and swept by gunfire.

Life in the trenches was miserable: mud, rats, lice, disease, and the constant threat of bombardment. And when orders came to attack, soldiers climbed out and crossed open ground under fire.

The quick victory everyone had hoped for was disappearing into the mud.

## THE EASTERN FRONT

While this was happening in the west, Russia was fighting Germany and Austria-Hungary in the east.

Russia mobilized more quickly than German planners had expected and invaded East Prussia. But at the **Battle of Tannenberg** in August 1914, German forces surrounded and destroyed much of a Russian army.

**RUSSIA:** That could have gone better.

Russia had more success against Austria-Hungary farther south, capturing territory in Galicia. The eastern front covered enormous distances, and unlike the western front, its battle lines would continue to move considerably.

The war was also spreading beyond Europe. European empires drew soldiers and resources from colonies around the world. Japan joined the Allies and attacked German possessions in East Asia and the Pacific.

Then, in late 1914, the Ottoman Empire entered the war alongside Germany and Austria-Hungary, opening more fronts.

**[The burning map expands to include more of the world.]**

**NARRATOR:** What had begun with a crisis in the Balkans had become a global war.

By the end of 1914, hundreds of thousands were dead. Governments prepared their people for a much longer struggle, and armies searched for ways to break the stalemate.

For a brief moment that Christmas, some soldiers along the western front stopped fighting, exchanged greetings, and met between the trenches.

Then the war continued.

**[The screen fades to black.]**
