# Did Dark Energy Just Disappear? | Space Time | PBS Digital Studios

In 1998, astronomers made a discovery that seemed to break the universe.

They were measuring distant exploding stars, expecting to find that cosmic expansion was slowing down. Gravity pulls matter together, so surely it must also be putting the brakes on the expansion of the universe.

Instead, the explosions suggested the opposite. Cosmic expansion was speeding up.

Something appeared to be driving galaxies apart faster and faster. We called that something *dark energy*. And over the past quarter century, it has become the largest ingredient in our standard model of cosmology.

But now, new maps of the universe suggest that dark energy may not be behaving the way we thought. It might have changed over cosmic history. It might be weakening. And in some possible models, it could eventually fade away.

So did dark energy just disappear?

Well, no. But the possibility that it could change—or even that we’ve misunderstood it entirely—might force us to rewrite the future of the cosmos.

To understand why dark energy is causing trouble, we need to start with what astronomers actually observe.

Imagine throwing a ball straight up. As it rises, Earth’s gravity slows it down. If you throw it hard enough, it might escape entirely, but gravity will still be trying to slow it.

For much of the twentieth century, cosmologists pictured the expanding universe in a similar way. Galaxies were moving apart because space itself was expanding, a motion that began in the hot early universe. Matter’s gravity should gradually slow that expansion.

The big question was whether the universe contained enough matter to stop expanding altogether. If it did, perhaps the expansion would reverse, ending in a Big Crunch. If it didn’t, the universe would expand forever, but at an ever more sedate pace.

Then came those supernovae.

Certain exploding stars, called Type Ia supernovae, can be used as distance markers. They don’t all have exactly the same intrinsic brightness, but astronomers can measure features of their light curves and make corrections that allow them to estimate how bright they really were.

Compare that intrinsic brightness with how bright a supernova appears in our telescopes, and you can estimate its distance.

Astronomers can also measure its redshift: how much the expansion of space has stretched the light on its journey to us. Redshift tells us how much the universe has expanded since that light was emitted.

Put those measurements together for supernovae at different distances, and you can reconstruct how cosmic expansion has changed over time.

The distant supernovae turned out to be dimmer—and therefore farther away—than expected in a universe whose expansion was simply slowing down. The best explanation was that expansion had been accelerating over the more recent part of cosmic history.

That didn’t mean galaxies suddenly started flying through space because of some mysterious new force. On very large scales, the geometry of space itself was evolving in a way that made distant, unbound galaxies recede from one another at an increasing rate.

And the leading explanation for that behavior was an idea Einstein had introduced decades earlier: the *cosmological constant*.

In general relativity, the gravitational behavior of a substance depends on both its energy density and its pressure. Ordinary matter has positive energy density and relatively little pressure. Radiation has positive pressure. Both contribute to the gravitational tendency that slows cosmic expansion.

A cosmological constant is different. It has a constant energy density and a pressure that is negative enough to make the expansion accelerate.

You can think of it as an energy associated with space itself. As the universe expands and the amount of space increases, the energy density stays the same. There is more total energy associated with the larger volume, but in an expanding universe, global energy conservation is subtler than it is in a fixed, unchanging space.

That constant dark energy is represented by the Greek letter lambda, Λ. Add it to a universe containing cold dark matter, ordinary matter, and radiation, and you get the standard cosmological model: Lambda Cold Dark Matter, or ΛCDM.

It’s a remarkably successful model. It describes the broad features of the cosmic microwave background—the ancient light released when the universe was about 380,000 years old. It explains many features of how galaxies cluster. And it fits a vast range of observations of an evolving universe.

In the usual accounting, about five percent of the universe’s present-day energy budget is ordinary matter. Around twenty-five to thirty percent is dark matter. And roughly two-thirds is dark energy.

But there’s an enormous caveat hidden inside that tidy pie chart.

We don’t know what dark energy is.

The cosmological constant is mathematically simple, and so far it has worked extremely well. But when physicists try to connect it to calculations of vacuum energy in quantum field theory, the numbers are spectacularly difficult to reconcile. Depending on how you frame the calculation, the predicted contribution can be vastly larger than the value inferred from the universe.

And the cosmological constant isn’t the only possible explanation for acceleration.

What if dark energy is a field that evolves over time? What if its density changes as the universe expands? What if gravity itself behaves differently on the largest scales?

These alternatives generally make slightly different predictions for the history of cosmic expansion. So to test them, we need to measure that history as precisely as possible.

Enter the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, or DESI.

DESI is designed to map the positions of millions of galaxies and quasars across an enormous volume of the universe. Its instrument can collect spectra from thousands of objects at a time, splitting their light into constituent colors and measuring their redshifts.

That gives astronomers a three-dimensional map: two directions across the sky, plus a measure related to distance along the line of sight.

And hidden in that map is a kind of cosmic ruler.

To understand where it came from, we need to go back to the early universe, before atoms formed. Back then, ordinary matter and light were locked together in a hot plasma. Gravity pulled material toward denser regions, while radiation pressure pushed back.

The result was a set of sound waves propagating through that primordial plasma.

When the universe cooled enough for electrons and nuclei to combine into neutral atoms, light could travel freely. The pressure waves stopped propagating through the matter in the same way, leaving an imprint in the distribution of matter.

That imprint is known as baryon acoustic oscillations, or BAO.

It doesn’t mean galaxies are arranged in neat shells that you can pick out by eye. The effect is statistical. If you select a galaxy and measure the distances to lots of other galaxies, you find a slight excess of pairs separated by a characteristic distance.

Because we can infer the physical scale of that ruler from early-universe physics, we can compare it with its apparent size at different redshifts. This gives us information about both cosmic distances and the expansion rate at different times.

DESI’s maps let astronomers measure that ruler over a substantial stretch of cosmic history. And when those measurements are combined with other observations—particularly the cosmic microwave background and different collections of supernova data—some analyses have found a tantalizing preference for a model in which dark energy changes with time.

That’s the result behind the headlines claiming that dark energy might be fading.

But there are several important layers of uncertainty here.

First, DESI does not take a photograph of dark energy. No telescope can point at a patch of it and watch it drain away. The result comes from comparing measurements of the universe with mathematical models and asking which expansion histories best fit the combined data.

Second, the strength of the preference depends on exactly which other data sets and assumptions are used. Different supernova compilations, for example, can produce different levels of preference for evolving dark energy.

Third, a preference for a changing model is not the same thing as a confirmed discovery. Scientists use statistical significance to estimate how surprising a result would be if a simpler model were correct. The reported hints have been intriguing, but interpreting them requires care: measurements have uncertainties, models have assumptions, and combining several data sets can expose subtle systematic effects.

A systematic effect is an error or bias that doesn’t simply disappear when you collect more data. It could come from calibration, how objects are selected, how their properties are modeled, or some other feature of the measurement process.

That doesn’t mean the hint is an error. It means cosmologists need to determine whether the pattern survives independent checks and improved data.

So what does it actually mean for dark energy to change?

Cosmologists often describe a component of the universe using an *equation-of-state parameter*, written as **w**. This is the ratio of its pressure to its energy density, with the appropriate factors of the speed of light included.

For a cosmological constant, w is exactly minus one, and it remains minus one forever.

Other forms of dark energy can have a different value of w. Some models also allow that value to change over time.

A common way to look for that change is to use a simple mathematical expression with two adjustable parameters: one describing the value of w today, and another describing how it varies as the universe expands.

If the best-fit values land away from the cosmological-constant prediction, that can be a clue that dark energy is dynamic.

But a clue is not yet an explanation.

That two-parameter expression is a useful way to test whether the data prefer a departure from ΛCDM. It isn’t necessarily a complete physical theory of dark energy. And depending on how its parameters are interpreted, extending it too far into the past or future can lead to behavior that may not be physically realistic.

In other words, even if the fit suggests dark energy has evolved, we can’t simply draw a straight line through the result and announce the date on which dark energy will vanish.

Still, we can explore the possibilities.

One idea is that dark energy comes from a field filling space, sometimes called *quintessence*. Like a ball moving across a landscape, the field can evolve. Its behavior depends on the shape of its potential: the rules that determine how much energy it has in different states.

If the field changes slowly enough, it can produce negative pressure and drive accelerated expansion. But unlike a true cosmological constant, its energy density may vary.

In some versions, the acceleration could eventually slow or stop. The universe might continue expanding forever, just without accelerating forever.

In other versions, the field could behave very differently. Depending on the underlying physics, the far future might involve a new phase of expansion—or even conditions that lead to a collapse. Current observations do not tell us which, if any, of these scenarios is right.

There are also models in which the effective dark energy density grows rather than shrinks. If that growth continued in a particular way, it could lead to a “Big Rip,” where the expansion eventually overcomes the forces binding galaxies, stars, planets, and ultimately smaller structures.

But the words “could lead” are doing a lot of work there. A changing value of w inferred from a limited range of cosmic history does not, by itself, establish any of those futures.

And there’s an even more radical possibility: perhaps the apparent dark energy is telling us that our theory of gravity needs an adjustment.

General relativity has passed extraordinary tests, from the motion of planets to gravitational waves. But cosmology asks us to apply it across distances and timescales far larger than those of many other tests.

Some modified-gravity theories can reproduce an accelerating universe without a separate dark energy component in the usual sense. They also tend to change the way cosmic structures grow, so measurements of galaxy clustering, gravitational lensing, and the motions of galaxies can help distinguish them from dark energy models.

That distinction matters. Two theories might predict almost the same expansion history while making different predictions about how matter clumps together.

So the next step isn’t just to measure expansion more precisely. It’s to compare multiple, independent views of the universe and see whether they tell a consistent story.

That includes better galaxy maps, more carefully calibrated supernova measurements, gravitational lensing surveys, and improved observations of the cosmic microwave background. It also means checking how sensitive the conclusions are to the choices made in analyzing the data.

This is why cosmologists are excited even though nobody should be declaring dark energy dead.

If the hint disappears as measurements improve, ΛCDM survives another serious test. That would still be valuable: we would know more precisely how closely dark energy resembles a cosmological constant.

If the hint grows stronger, the implications would be enormous. We’d have evidence that the dominant ingredient in today’s cosmic energy budget is not a fixed property of space. We might be seeing a field evolve across billions of years, or a sign that our theory of gravity needs to be extended.

Either result would sharpen one of the deepest questions in physics.

Because the mystery of dark energy isn’t just why galaxies are receding from one another. It’s why the universe contains a component with just the right properties to become important so late in cosmic history.

In the early universe, matter and radiation dominated. As space expanded, matter became more dilute. If dark energy is a cosmological constant, its density stayed the same. Eventually, matter thinned out enough for dark energy to take over, and cosmic expansion began to accelerate.

Why is that transition happening in the broad neighborhood of the era when we happen to be observing it? Is that a coincidence? A clue about some evolving field? Or a question we’re framing incorrectly?

For now, we don’t know.

Dark energy has not disappeared from our observations. The universe is still best described as undergoing accelerated expansion, and a cosmological constant remains a powerful, successful explanation.

But the new measurements have opened a crack in a picture that once seemed almost immovable. Through that crack, we may be glimpsing a more complicated cosmic history—or we may be seeing the limits of our current measurements.

Either way, the universe is giving us another chance to ask what empty space is made of, how gravity works across the largest distances, and what kind of future awaits all of space and time.
