# Why Britain Uses Separate Hot and Cold Taps

**[Opening shot: A hand darts between two taps, recoiling from the hot water and then the cold.]**

**NARRATOR:**
You’re standing at a British sink, trying to wash your hands. One tap delivers water that feels straight from a glacier. The other seems determined to remove your fingerprints.

Why are there two taps? Why not just mix the water?

The answer takes us back to old houses, rooftop tanks, and a time when keeping two water supplies apart was a matter of safety.

**[Cut to an illustrated old British house.]**

Many British homes were built long before modern plumbing became standard. As indoor plumbing developed, systems were often added to existing buildings and updated bit by bit.

A common arrangement sent cold water from the public mains directly to the kitchen sink. But elsewhere in the house, water might come from a storage tank, often tucked away in the loft.

That tank could also feed a hot-water cylinder. Water was heated, stored, and then sent to the hot taps.

**[Animation: One blue pipe runs from the mains; another runs through a loft tank and hot-water cylinder.]**

The problem? Water stored in a tank could become contaminated if the tank wasn’t properly covered or maintained. So the cold water coming directly from the mains was generally the more reliable choice for drinking.

And plumbers wanted to keep it that way.

If hot and cold pipes were connected inside a tap, differences in pressure could potentially push water from one supply into the other. Old plumbing rules aimed to prevent that kind of contamination, and separate taps were a straightforward solution.

**[Cut back to the sink.]**

Of course, that still leaves the question: how were you supposed to wash your hands?

The traditional answer is to put the plug in the basin, run a little hot water, add cold water, and wash in the mixture. The sink itself becomes the mixer.

That may sound inconvenient if you’re used to washing under a stream of warm water, but it was a familiar routine for generations.

**[Cut to a modern mixer tap.]**

Today, there are other ways to protect the water supply. Some mixer taps keep hot and cold water in separate channels until they reach the spout. Others use devices such as check valves to help prevent water flowing back into the wrong pipe.

And plenty of British homes now have modern plumbing and mixer taps. Separate taps haven’t survived because they’re the only legal option.

So why do you still see them?

Partly because Britain has a lot of older housing, and a working set of taps can stay in place for decades. Replacing them can mean changing the sink or altering pipework, which costs more than simply leaving them alone. And some people like the traditional look.

**[Closing shot: Water swirls together in a filled basin.]**

Those two taps are a small piece of plumbing history hiding in plain sight: a design shaped by old water systems, concerns about contamination, and habits that proved surprisingly hard to wash away.

**NARRATOR:**
So next time you meet one freezing tap and one fiery tap, remember: the missing mixer is right beneath them. It’s the sink.
