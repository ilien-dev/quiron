# The chemistry of cold packs

**[Opening shot: A basketball player lands awkwardly and grabs their ankle. A teammate snaps a cold pack and hands it over.]**

**NARRATOR:**

You’ve probably seen an instant cold pack in a first-aid kit. Squeeze it, shake it, and within seconds it turns chilly—no freezer required.

It seems almost like magic. After all, the pack starts at room temperature. How can something get colder without being placed near ice?

The answer lies in the way chemicals exchange energy with their surroundings.

**[Animation: A cold pack is shown in cross-section, with a small pouch floating inside it.]**

Inside many instant cold packs are two separate ingredients: water in a small, breakable pouch, and a solid chemical surrounding it. That solid is often a salt, such as ammonium nitrate or urea, depending on the product.

When you squeeze the pack, the inner pouch bursts. Water spills out and mixes with the solid. As the solid dissolves, the temperature drops.

But dissolving doesn’t always make things cold. Some substances release heat when they dissolve. So what makes this mixture different?

To find out, we need to look at what happens to energy during dissolving.

**[Animation: The solid’s particles are arranged in a neat lattice. Water molecules move around them.]**

In a salt such as ammonium nitrate, positively and negatively charged particles, called ions, are held together in a solid structure. Pulling those ions apart takes energy.

Meanwhile, water molecules are attracted to the ions. As water surrounds each one, new interactions form. That process releases energy.

So there’s an energy trade-off: separating particles requires energy, while forming new interactions releases it.

For an instant cold pack, the dissolving process absorbs more energy than it releases. That extra energy comes from the surroundings—including the water, the pack, and eventually your skin.

As energy is absorbed, the mixture gets colder. Chemists call a process that absorbs heat from its surroundings **endothermic**.

**[Animation: Arrows show heat flowing from a hand into the cold pack.]**

Notice that the pack doesn’t send “cold” into your skin. Instead, heat flows from your warmer skin into the colder pack. That transfer cools the area, which can help reduce pain and swelling after a minor injury.

The pack won’t stay cold forever. Once the solid has dissolved and the mixture continues absorbing heat from the room, its temperature gradually rises again.

And what about those reusable gel packs you keep in the freezer? They work differently.

A gel pack must first lose heat in the freezer. When you put it on an injury, it absorbs heat as it warms up. An instant cold pack, by contrast, carries its cooling process inside the package and starts working when you mix its ingredients.

**[Cut to two labeled energy diagrams: one slopes upward; the other slopes downward.]**

The same energy accounting helps explain instant *heat* packs, too. Some contain chemicals that release more energy when they mix or react than they absorb. That makes the process **exothermic**: heat flows out into the surroundings.

Whether a process feels hot or cold depends on the balance between energy absorbed and energy released—not simply on whether chemicals are mixing.

**[Closing shot: The player rests with the cold pack wrapped in a cloth against their ankle.]**

So the next time you snap a cold pack, think about the busy exchange happening inside. Particles are separating, water molecules are gathering around them, and energy is moving into the mixture.

All that chemistry turns a room-temperature packet into a portable way to cool an injury.
