# Oh hello, you’re alive

**[OPEN ON: A bedroom. Morning light pushes through the curtains. An alarm goes off. A hand emerges from under a blanket, fumbles for the phone, and silences it.]**

**NARRATOR:**  
Oh, hello.

You’re alive.

Again.

Congratulations on another successful night of being unconscious without dying. I know you didn’t personally supervise most of that process, but your body handled it beautifully. Heart: still going. Lungs: in and out. Brain: already bringing up something embarrassing you said seven years ago.

Excellent work, everyone.

**[CUT TO: The person staring at the ceiling.]**

And now comes the difficult part: being a person for an entire day.

There are messages to answer, things to remember, decisions to make. There’s probably an email somewhere that begins with “Just following up,” which is a phrase capable of turning a human spine into a question mark.

Maybe you woke up excited. Maybe you woke up tired. Maybe you woke up and immediately checked your phone, saw six different disasters and a video of a raccoon washing grapes, and thought, “Yes. This seems like a normal amount of information for a mammal.”

Whatever kind of morning it is, you’re here.

That sounds obvious. But obvious things are surprisingly easy to miss when you’re busy looking for your other sock.

**[CUT TO: A kitchen. Water fills a kettle.]**

Your body has been working all night.

While you were dreaming about showing up to school without pants, your cells were repairing themselves. Your brain was sorting through memories. Your immune system was keeping watch, presumably wearing tiny sunglasses and saying, “We’ve got a situation.”

And now you’re standing in the kitchen waiting for water to boil, annoyed because it’s taking forever.

Which is fair. Water is famously unhurried.

But consider, for a moment, how strange this is. You are a living thing on a planet, preparing a warm drink. You can smell the coffee or tea. You can feel the floor under your feet. You can hear someone outside starting a car, or a bird making an unnecessarily confident announcement.

None of this requires you to be happy about it. You don’t have to turn your breakfast into a spiritual experience. Sometimes toast is just toast, and sometimes it’s burnt because you were looking at your phone.

Still, it’s here. And so are you.

**[CUT TO: A crowded street. People move past the camera.]**

Look at everyone else.

That person in the green jacket has a favorite song. The woman waiting for the bus might be worrying about her dad. The guy carrying three pizzas could be on his way to a party, or he could be having an ambitious Tuesday.

Every person you pass is somewhere in the middle of a story you can’t see.

And they’re all doing it with roughly the same equipment: a body that needs food and sleep, a mind that tells convincing stories, and a heart that occasionally decides the right response to a difficult conversation is to beat like a drum solo.

It’s easy to imagine that everyone else received instructions you somehow missed. That they know how to be confident, make friends, save money, choose a career, and fold a fitted sheet.

Some of them do know how to fold a fitted sheet. We should respect them, but we should not assume they have the rest figured out.

Most people are improvising more than they let on. You can see it when someone misses a step on the sidewalk and pretends they meant to jog.

**[CUT TO: The person sitting at a desk, looking at a long to-do list.]**

There’s a lot of pressure to make your life count.

To find your purpose. To use your time well. To wake up early, exercise, drink enough water, maintain friendships, learn a language, become financially stable, and somehow develop a skincare routine with more steps than launching a spacecraft.

And look, some of those things might be good for you. Drink water. Call your friends. If a French owl on your phone is helping you learn a language, by all means, keep him happy.

But you don’t need to solve your entire life before lunch.

You can do the next small thing.

Wash one dish. Send the message you’ve been putting off. Step outside for five minutes. Open the document and write a terrible first sentence. A terrible first sentence has one significant advantage over a perfect sentence: it exists.

And if today is a day when getting out of bed feels like a major undertaking, start even smaller. Sit up. Take a breath. Reach out to someone you trust.

Small things can make room for other small things.

**[CUT TO: A montage. A dog shakes rainwater everywhere. Someone drops a spoon. A child laughs.]**

Being alive is not always beautiful.

Sometimes it’s painful or frightening. Sometimes it’s losing someone. Sometimes it’s waiting for an answer, or carrying a worry that follows you into every room. Sometimes the best part of the day is simply that it ends.

And when things are hard, you don’t owe anyone a cheerful interpretation. You don’t have to find a lesson in everything. You’re allowed to say, “This hurts,” and let that be true.

You’re also allowed to notice something good while it hurts.

A friend remembering how you take your coffee. A dog asleep with its tongue slightly out. A patch of sunlight on the wall. A song arriving at exactly the right moment.

Those things don’t erase the hard parts. But they can sit beside them, giving you somewhere to rest your attention for a minute.

**[CUT TO: Evening. The person walks home as lights come on in windows.]**

Eventually, today will become a day you barely remember.

Maybe that sounds sad. I think it’s a little comforting.

You don’t have to make every day memorable. Some days are for doing laundry, eating leftovers, and watching an episode of a show you’ve already seen. Some days you’ll make a mistake, apologize, and try again tomorrow. Some days you’ll laugh so hard you can’t explain what was funny.

And every now and then, you might stop in the middle of an ordinary moment and realize you’re here for it.

You get to taste the orange. Hear the song. Feel the cold air when you open the door. You get to know people, and let them know you, even if it’s awkward at first. Especially if it’s awkward at first. That’s usually how people work.

**[CUT TO: The bedroom again. The person sets their phone down and turns off the light.]**

So, hello.

You’re alive.

You don’t have to know exactly what to do with that right now. You can begin wherever you are, with whatever you have, and see what the next moment asks of you.

Maybe it asks you to make a cup of tea. Maybe it asks you to rest. Maybe it asks you to finally answer that email.

I’m sorry about the email.

**[A beat. The phone lights up on the bedside table.]**

**NARRATOR:**  
Tomorrow, perhaps.

**[FADE OUT.]**
