# In Praise of Chairs

**[OPENING SHOT: An empty chair in the middle of a room.]**

**NARRATOR:**

Look at this chair.

It isn’t famous. It hasn’t won any awards. There’s a scratch on one leg, and if you lean too far to the left, it makes a noise like a small door complaining.

But it’s a chair. And that’s remarkable.

Because a chair is an object that makes a promise: *You don’t have to stand right now.*

**[CUT TO: A crowded bus. Someone gives up their seat.]**

We tend to notice chairs when there aren’t enough of them. At the airport gate. At a party where everyone is balancing a plate. In a waiting room, when the only open seat is directly beneath a television playing something terrible.

A chair can be a small act of welcome. Pulling one out says, “Stay a while.” Offering one says, “I see you’re tired.” Putting another at the table says, “There’s room for you.”

And sometimes, a chair can say the opposite.

**[CUT TO: A tiny plastic stool beside a large office chair.]**

Think of the difference between the chair behind the desk and the one in front of it. One swivels. One adjusts. One seems to have been designed for a person who makes decisions. The other was apparently designed to make sure you don’t get too comfortable while waiting to hear one.

Chairs arrange more than bodies. They arrange relationships.

A circle of chairs suggests that everyone gets a turn to speak. Rows of chairs suggest that someone at the front probably has a microphone. A chair at the head of the table can make its occupant look important before they’ve said a word.

**[CUT TO: A dining chair, a barber’s chair, a park bench.]**

They also give our lives a kind of scenery.

There’s the kitchen chair where someone reads the news each morning. The chair in the corner that holds a week’s worth of clothes. The one at your grandparents’ house that everybody knows belongs to your grandfather, even though nobody has ever formally assigned it to him.

Some chairs collect memories simply by staying in the same place.

You remember the conversation because you can picture where everyone sat. You remember being small enough to curl up sideways in an armchair. You remember a hospital chair that was too narrow to sleep in, and sleeping in it anyway.

**[PAUSE.]**

A good chair meets the body where it is. It takes some of your weight without asking you to think about it. The seat is high enough that your feet find the floor. The back catches you when you lean. You can shift, settle, and eventually forget the chair is there.

That sounds simple, but bodies are different. A chair that feels perfect to one person can leave another’s feet dangling or press painfully against the backs of their legs. Even the best chair is better for some bodies than others. The generous thing is to have choices.

**[CUT TO: A chair being assembled, then repaired.]**

Someone had to decide how high to make that seat. Someone had to think about the angle of the back, the strength of the joints, the shape of an edge where a hand might rest.

And someone, somewhere, looked at an existing chair and thought, “What if this had wheels?”

A dangerous question. But an excellent one.

The history of chairs is full of experiments: folding chairs, rocking chairs, high chairs, wheelchairs, chairs that recline, chairs that stack, chairs so beautiful you’re afraid to sit in them. Each one answers a slightly different question about what a body might need—or what a room might want to say.

Some answers are better than others. We should be honest about that. If a chair requires you to maintain the posture of a concert pianist just to survive a thirty-minute meeting, the chair may have confused you with a concert pianist.

**[CUT TO: Several chairs around a table. People begin to sit.]**

Still, most chairs don’t need to be extraordinary. They just need to hold up, fit reasonably well, and be there when somebody needs them.

A chair is a little piece of architecture built for one person. It gives you a place in a room and, for a moment, lets the floor do the work of holding you up.

So here’s to the scratched chairs and the wobblers. To the folding chairs brought out when more people arrive than expected. To the chair pulled close for a long conversation, and the empty one waiting for whoever comes next.

**[FINAL SHOT: Someone enters the room, sits in the chair, and settles in.]**

Go on. Have a seat.
