# Thoughts From Your Deathbed

**[COLD OPEN]**

*[Black screen. Soft sound of a heart monitor, slow and steady. Fade in on a window with late afternoon light, dust drifting through it.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Imagine you're lying in a bed you don't recognize. The sheets are too white. The room smells faintly of antiseptic and flowers someone brought yesterday. Your body feels heavy, like it belongs to someone who has already started leaving.

And you have a clear thought: *So this is how it ends.*

Not everyone gets that moment. Many people go quickly, or without warning. But many others get time, a few days or a few weeks, long enough to look back. And when they do, they tend to say the same things.

Today, we're going to listen to them.

*[Title card: THOUGHTS FROM YOUR DEATHBED]*

---

**[SECTION ONE: THE WORK]**

*[Visual: an empty office chair, a stack of unopened mail, a closed laptop.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The first thing that surfaces, for many people, is work. Not the big accomplishments. The hours.

Palliative care nurses have written about this for years. Dying patients rarely say, "I wish I had closed more deals." They say, "I wish I hadn't worked so much." They talk about the evenings spent answering email, the weekends given to tasks that vanished the moment they were done, the promotions that felt urgent and now feel like weather that passed over.

This isn't an argument against ambition. Work can be meaningful. Still, it's worth asking yourself a quiet question today: *If this were my last month, would I want my calendar to look like this?*

Most of us already know the answer. We just haven't let ourselves say it.

---

**[SECTION TWO: THE PEOPLE]**

*[Visual: a phone lying face-down on a kitchen table. Then a photo album, pages turning slowly.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The second thing people think about, almost always, is other people.

Not the crowd at the party. Not the acquaintances. The few faces that mattered. A sibling they stopped calling after an argument they can't even remember the cause of now. A friend they meant to visit "after things settled down." A parent whose stories they half-listened to, because they assumed there would be more time.

There is always more time, until there isn't.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: the people you're thinking about right now are probably still alive. You still have the chance to call. To write the letter. To say, "I was wrong," or "I miss you," or simply, "Tell me about your day."

Don't wait for a deathbed to do what you can do on an ordinary Tuesday.

---

**[SECTION THREE: THE FEAR OF BEING SEEN]**

*[Visual: a figure standing at the edge of a crowded room, then stepping forward into light.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Here's a regret that rarely gets spoken aloud until the very end: "I wish I had let myself be seen."

People describe holding back. Keeping the opinion to themselves. Not pursuing the thing they loved because it seemed silly, or too late, or not quite good enough. Staying in the safe version of their life, polished and careful, while the real one waited just outside the frame.

They didn't fail because they tried and fell. They fell short because they never really tried at all.

And when they looked back, the failure they felt most keenly wasn't the one that hurt. It was the one they never gave themselves permission to attempt.

---

**[SECTION FOUR: THE SMALL THINGS]**

*[Visual: slow montage. Morning coffee steaming. Hands holding a child's drawing. Rain on a window. A dog asleep in a patch of sunlight.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
And then, almost unexpectedly, the memories that come back most vividly are small.

A walk with no destination. A song that played in a car on a long drive. The smell of bread baking on a cold morning. A joke shared with someone who understood it without explanation.

Nobody on their deathbed says, "I wish I had seen more of my bank statement." They remember the ordinary moments, the ones that felt unremarkable at the time, because those were the ones that made up almost everything.

So pay attention now. Notice the light in the room you're sitting in. Taste your food. Let yourself be bored sometimes, because boredom is often just life happening quietly.

---

**[SECTION FIVE: THE QUESTION BENEATH EVERYTHING]**

*[Visual: the window again. The light has shifted to golden hour.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Near the end, many people stop asking whether they did enough, and start asking whether they were kind.

Kind to others. Kind to themselves. Kind to the version of themselves that made mistakes out of fear, or exhaustion, or simple inexperience.

Several of them said something close to this: "I was harder on myself than anyone else ever was."

If that sounds familiar, consider this a gentle permission slip. You don't have to be finished. You don't have to be impressive. You're allowed to rest, to change direction, to be a beginner at something you're supposed to be good at by now.

---

**[CLOSING]**

*[Visual: the bed, now empty. The window stays open. A breeze lifts the curtain.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
If you're watching this with a full schedule and a long list of things you're waiting to do someday, I'd like you to try a small experiment.

Imagine you're on that bed. Ask yourself three questions:

What would I wish I'd spent more time on?
Who would I wish I'd reached out to?
What would I wish I'd finally allowed myself to try?

Then pick one answer and act on it this week. Not everything. Just one thing.

You don't need a deathbed to start living more deliberately. You only need to remember that the clock is already running, and that it's running for you.

*[The curtain settles. Silence. The heart monitor fades into a single steady tone, then slowly into the sound of birds outside.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Thanks for listening. If this made you think of someone, maybe send them a message today.

*[End card.]*
