# WW1 - Oversimplified (Part 2)

**[COLD OPEN]**

**NARRATOR:** Picture a man in 1916 standing in a trench. He's been there for months. His boots are full of water, his lungs are full of something worse, and above him the sky is full of shells. Somewhere behind him, a general is looking at a map with little colored pins and thinking, *"We're winning."*

Welcome back. Last time, we watched Europe stumble into the biggest war it had ever seen. This time, the stumbling is over. Everyone is in the mud, and nobody can get out.

**[TITLE CARD]**

---

**NARRATOR:** Quick recap. By the end of 1914, the Western Front had turned into two long lines of trenches running from the North Sea to Switzerland. Both sides had tried to win quickly, and both had failed. So 1915 was supposed to be the year somebody broke the stalemate.

Somebody had a clever idea. Winston Churchill, then First Lord of the Admiralty, proposed attacking the Ottoman Empire, which had joined the Central Powers. If the Allies could force open the Dardanelles Strait and capture Constantinople, they could knock Turkey out of the war and open a supply route to Russia.

**[VISUAL: Map of the Dardanelles]**

**NARRATOR:** The plan was, to put it kindly, optimistic. The Ottomans had months to dig in, and the Allies landed at Gallipoli on April 25, 1915, on beaches that were, shall we say, not designed for a friendly welcome. The Turkish commander there was a young officer named Mustafa Kemal. Years later, he would be known as Atatürk, the founder of modern Turkey. On that April morning, he was just the guy who figured out where the invaders were going to climb and put his men there first.

The campaign dragged on for eight months. Thousands died from bullets, disease, and heat. Finally, in late 1915 and early 2016, excuse me, *1916*, the Allies quietly withdrew. The big idea had cost hundreds of thousands of casualties and accomplished almost nothing. Churchill took the blame, and his career took a serious hit.

**[BEAT]**

**NARRATOR:** Meanwhile, the Germans had their own bad idea. On April 22, 1915, near the Belgian town of Ypres, they released chlorine gas from cylinders and let the wind carry it toward French and Algerian troops. It was the first large-scale use of chemical weapons on the Western Front. Soldiers clawed at their throats and drowned in their own fluids. The line broke, and then the Germans... stopped, because they had not planned for what came next. They had no reserves ready to exploit the gap. Ypres would be fought over again and again, and the gas would only get worse.

---

**NARRATOR:** Now, 1915 also had a moment that changed public opinion, especially in America. On May 7, a German U-boat torpedoed the *Lusitania*, a British passenger liner. Nearly 1,200 people died, including 128 Americans. The Germans argued the ship was carrying weapons, which was partly true. Americans were not impressed by the logic. Germany backed off submarine attacks for a while, but the anger lingered.

That same spring, Italy switched sides. The Treaty of London promised them land from Austria-Hungary if they joined the Allies, and they declared war in May 1915. Their promise of a quick victory did not go well. Their war would be a long, bloody slog in the Alps.

---

**[SEGMENT: VERDUN]**

**NARRATOR:** In early 1916, German Chief of Staff Erich von Falkenhayn had a plan that was, honestly, terrifying in its simplicity. He would not try to break through. He would attack a fortress town called Verdun, a place France could not afford to lose. The French would throw everything they had into defending it, and Germany would grind their army down in the process. His phrase was to "bleed France white."

**[VISUAL: Verdun battlefield, shell craters]**

**NARRATOR:** The battle started on February 21, 1916, and lasted until December. It became a symbol of French national pride, and it became a meat grinder. Shelling churned the ground into a lunar landscape. Villages vanished off the map entirely. Estimates of total casualties on both sides run somewhere around 700,000 to 800,000 men. Falkenhayn did bleed France, but Germany bled too, and the French kept holding on.

Here's the twist. Falkenhayn was right that the French would fight to the death, and wrong about almost everything else. By summer, Verdun had stopped being a German strategy and had become a German trap.

---

**[SEGMENT: THE SOMME]**

**NARRATOR:** To relieve pressure on Verdun, the British and French launched a huge joint offensive on the Somme River starting July 1, 1916. The British commanders had spent a week bombarding German lines and were confident the wire would be cut and the defenders dead. They were wrong. The German dugouts were deep, and many survived to climb up with machine guns.

On the first day alone, the British suffered about 57,000 casualties, nearly 20,000 of them killed. It remains the bloodiest day in the history of the British Army.

**NARRATOR:** The Somme dragged on until November. It advanced the front by only about six miles. The total casualties, from all sides, passed a million. Remember that number. It's going to keep showing up.

But the Somme also gave us something new. On September 15, the British introduced tanks to the battlefield at Flers-Courcelette. They were slow, unreliable, and frequently broke down, but they were a glimpse of what the future looked like.

---

**[SEGMENT: THE EASTERN FRONT]**

**NARRATOR:** Meanwhile, Russia had its own moment in the sun. In June 1916, General Aleksei Brusilov launched an offensive against Austria-Hungary that was actually a success. The Russians took huge numbers of prisoners and pushed deep into Galicia. Then Romania joined the Allies in August, thinking it had picked the winning team. Within months, Germany and its allies had overrun most of the country.

So by the end of 1916, the war had been going for more than two years. Nobody had won. Everybody had lost.

---

**[SEGMENT: THE UNITED STATES ENTERS]**

**NARRATOR:** Now, things got really interesting in early 1917. In January, British intelligence intercepted a telegram from German Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann to Mexico. It proposed a military alliance: if the United States entered the war, Mexico would attack it and get back Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona.

The British handed the message to Washington. When it was published in March, Americans were furious. It did not help that Germany had already resumed unrestricted submarine warfare on February 1, sinking neutral ships without warning.

On April 6, 1917, the United States declared war on Germany.

**NARRATOR:** Now, the Americans were not ready. They had a small army and needed to build one from scratch. But they had money, factories, and fresh manpower. For the Allies, the question was not whether America would matter. It was how long they could hold out until it did.

---

**[SEGMENT: THE COLLAPSE OF RUSSIA]**

**NARRATOR:** While America was gearing up, Russia was falling apart. In March 1917, food shortages, strikes, and military defeats forced Tsar Nicholas II to abdicate. A provisional government took over but kept fighting, which made it deeply unpopular.

In November, the Bolsheviks led by Vladimir Lenin seized power in Petrograd. Lenin's first priority was getting out of the war. On March 3, 1918, Russia signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, surrendering huge amounts of land. That freed up German troops for one final gamble in the West.

---

**[SEGMENT: PASSCHENDAELE AND CAMBRAI]**

**NARRATOR:** Before that gamble, the British had one more horror in store. Starting in July 1917, they attacked around the Belgian village of Passchendaele. It rained constantly. Artillery shattered drainage systems, and the ground turned into a swamp that swallowed men, horses, and entire guns. The battle lasted until November and gained only a few miles. Soldiers talked about the mud the way other people talk about storms.

A few weeks later, at Cambrai, the British tried a new approach. They used massed tanks without a long artillery bombardment, and they broke through German lines. It was a real success, for a few days, before the Germans counterattacked. Still, the lesson was clear. The future of war would involve tanks, planes, and surprise.

**[BEAT]**

**NARRATOR:** Meanwhile, in Italy, the Austro-German army smashed the Italian line at Caporetto in October 1917. The Italians fell back almost a hundred miles. Italy survived, but only because France and Britain rushed reinforcements to the rescue.

---

**[SEGMENT: THE FINAL GAMBLE]**

**NARRATOR:** By early 1918, Germany was in trouble. It was running low on food, fuel, and men, and the Americans were arriving by the hundreds of thousands. Germany had one chance: strike hard in the West before the Americans could fully deploy.

On March 21, 1918, the Germans launched the Spring Offensive. Using new infiltration tactics, small squads of storm troopers slipped past strongpoints and hit the rear areas. The British front bent badly. Paris came close enough to be shelled by long-range guns.

But Germany had gambled everything, and the offensive ran out of steam. Supply lines stretched, troops got exhausted, and Allied reserves kept coming. The Allies counterattacked during the Hundred Days Offensive, beginning August 8, 1918, near Amiens. German commanders quietly admitted the war was lost.

---

**[SEGMENT: THE END]**

**NARRATOR:** The armistice was signed in a railway carriage in the forest of Compiègne, and it took effect on November 11, 1918, at 11 a.m. Four years of war ended in a single morning. Fighting stopped, and the survivors stepped out of the trenches into a world that had been permanently changed.

Think about what that meant. Roughly 10 million soldiers were dead. Millines of civilians had died too, from famine, disease, and deliberate violence. Four empires had collapsed: the Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian, and Ottoman. The map of Europe and the Middle East was about to be redrawn, and those new borders would cause problems for a century.

**NARRATOR:** And the people who signed the peace? They had their own ideas about how the next war should be prevented. We'll get to that next time, along with the peace treaty that many historians think set the stage for the next war.

Until then, thanks for watching. If you made it through all of that, you deserve a nap, and maybe a cup of tea. Just don't go into a trench to get it.

**[END CARD]**
