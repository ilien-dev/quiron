**The Pharaoh That Wouldn't Be Forgotten**

*[COLD OPEN]*

Imagine spending your whole life building monuments to yourself, and then, after you die, your successor tries to chisel your name off every wall you ever touched. For centuries, that's nearly what happened to Hatshepsut. Yet she was one of the most remarkable rulers ancient Egypt ever produced. Stay with me, because her story is about power, memory, and the stubborn habit of the past refusing to stay buried.

*[INTRO]*

Hatshepsut was the daughter of Thutmose I and the wife of her half-brother, Thutmose II. When he died young, the throne should have passed to his son, a small child by a lesser wife. Instead, Hatshepsut stepped in as regent. Within a few years, she had moved beyond regency entirely and taken the full titles of pharaoh.

She didn't do it quietly. She had herself shown wearing the royal false beard, the kilt, and the nemes headdress, the traditional regalia of a king. Her statues and inscriptions presented her as a male ruler. Egyptian art had its conventions, and she bent them deliberately.

*[SECTION: A Reign of Building]*

Her reign, roughly twenty-two years, was a period of stability and ambition. Her most famous achievement was not a conquest but an expedition to the land of Punt, likely on the Horn of Africa. It brought back myrrh trees, incense, gold, and exotic animals. Reliefs of the voyage on her temple walls show ships loaded with goods and trees carefully transported in baskets, with their roots intact.

That temple, Djeser-Djeseru, is carved into the cliffs at Deir el-Bahari near Luxor, and it still stands. Its terraced colonnades rise dramatically from the desert floor. At Karnak, she raised a pair of obelisks. One still stands, and it is among the tallest obelisks ever erected in Egypt.

*[SECTION: The Erasure]*

Then she was gone. Hatshepsut likely died around 1458 BCE, and her stepson, Thutmose III, took full control. What followed is one of the most deliberate acts of memory erasure in ancient history.

Across Egypt, workers chiseled out her cartouches and images. At Karnak, her obelisks were walled up and partially hidden. Her name was dropped from king lists. Scholars still debate the motives. Some suspect Thutmose III resented years of being pushed aside. Others point to political reasons, or a desire for a clean line of male succession. Whatever the reason, the message was clear: this ruler should never have existed.

But the erasure was not complete. Chisel marks were often sloppy, and many images survived in corners or behind later construction. Her temple at Deir el-Bahari was never fully destroyed. The past, it turns out, is hard to erase entirely.

*[SECTION: The Rediscovery]*

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, archaeologists began piecing her story back together. Once hieroglyphs could be read, her name and titles reappeared in inscriptions. Excavations at Deir el-Bahari revealed the scale of her building program. In 2007, researchers announced that a mummy from the Valley of the Kings was likely hers, supported by DNA evidence.

Today, she is no longer a footnote. She is one of the most studied queens of the ancient world.

*[OUTRO]*

So what does her story tell us? Power can be taken away, and names can be scratched off stone, but memory has a way of pushing back. Hatshepsut's enemies wanted her forgotten. Instead, she became a case study in how the powerful try to rewrite history, and how the evidence keeps getting in the way.
