Before we start, a quick word on what we mean by money. Economists usually describe it as anything that serves three jobs: a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value. In other words, it's something people accept to pay for things, something you can price things in, and something you can save and expect to still be worth something later.

Keep those three jobs in mind, because they explain a lot of what's coming. Let's begin with number ten.

**[FACT 10]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number ten: on the island of Yap, in Micronesia, the currency was made of giant stones.

These aren't ordinary coins. They're called *rai* stones, and they're large circular discs of limestone, some of them several feet across and weighing multiple tons. They were quarried from limestone deposits on other islands, sometimes hundreds of miles away, using canoes and a great deal of human effort.

**[VISUAL: Photograph-style shots of large stone discs set against a tropical shoreline. Animated map showing Yap and the distant quarry islands.]**

Here's where it gets interesting. Because the stones were so heavy, people often didn't move them when they changed hands. Instead, the community simply agreed on who owned which stone. A famous story describes a stone that sank during transport; it was still considered valuable, because everyone knew it existed and who it belonged to. Ownership was a shared memory, not a physical object you had to haul around.

Think about that for a second. The stone at the bottom of the ocean was still money, because the trust that backed it was intact. That idea, that money is a social agreement, comes up again and again in this video.

Rai stones are still used in ceremonial and traditional exchanges on Yap today.

**[FACT 9]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number nine: the word "salary" may have a link to salt.

The most common explanation traces "salary" to the Latin word *salarium*. The exact origin is debated, but one popular theory connects it to *sal*, the Latin word for salt. Salt was valuable in the ancient world. It preserved food, it was essential for health, and it was traded widely. Some historians have claimed Roman soldiers were paid in salt, but historians disagree on how much that story is true. Many scholars think the more likely account is that *salarium* referred to an allowance, possibly used to buy salt.

**[VISUAL: A salt shaker pours slowly onto a table. Text overlay: "SALARIUM?"]**

The honest answer is that we're not completely sure. But the story stuck around for a reason. Salt was once so important that it made sense as a standard of value. When you hear "not worth his salt," that old idiom is often linked to the same era of valuable salt and the people who worked for it.

It's a good reminder that money has always been tied to what people really need.

---

**[FACT 8]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number eight: the ridges on the edge of many coins weren't just decoration. They were a defense.

Look at a US quarter, a dime, or a euro coin, and you'll see those tiny vertical grooves around the rim. That's called reeding, and it has a practical purpose.

**[VISUAL: Macro close-up of a coin's reeded edge, rotating slowly.]**

In the days when gold and silver coins were the standard, many were worth more than the metal they were made from. Clever people would shave tiny slivers of precious metal off the edges, collect the shavings, and melt them down. Do this to enough coins and you'd get a meaningful amount of gold or silver without anyone noticing that the coins had gotten lighter.

Reeding made shaving obvious. If someone sliced off a bit of a ridged edge, the damage would show. Governments adopted it widely as a way to protect the integrity of their currency.

So the next time you run your thumb along a coin's edge, you're feeling centuries of anti-fraud engineering.

**[FACT 7]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number seven: US dollar bills aren't made from paper, at least not in the way you might think.

The paper used for American banknotes is a blend of about 75 percent cotton and 25 percent linen. It's not wood pulp, the material most everyday paper comes from. The fabric blend makes the notes more durable and better able to withstand the constant folding, crumpling, and washing that bills go through.

**[VISUAL: A dollar bill is held up to a light, revealing the security threads and watermark. Then a close-up of fibers.]**

The Bureau of Engraving and Printing also embeds tiny red and blue fibers throughout the paper. These are visible under close inspection, and they're one of many security features designed to make counterfeiting harder. Other features include the watermark, the security thread, and the color-shifting ink in higher denominations.

Other countries have gone further. Several nations, including Australia, have adopted polymer banknotes, which are plastic-based and tougher. Still, the cotton-linen blend is a surprise to a lot of people, and it's a good example of how much engineering goes into the bills we treat as disposable.

**[FACT 6]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number six: the penny costs more to make than it's worth.

This one has become a famous talking point. For the United States Mint, the cost of producing a one-cent coin has exceeded its face value for years. In recent reporting, the cost to make a penny was above three cents, depending on metal prices and production numbers.

**[VISUAL: A penny on a scale, with a cost-breakdown graphic appearing beside it.]**

Why keep making them? The main reason is inertia and public habit. Pennies are deeply embedded in the way people think about prices, change, and tradition. Removing them would affect businesses, rounding rules, and cash handling in ways that are more complicated than just stopping production.

Several countries have already made that choice. Canada stopped distributing pennies in 2013, and its businesses round totals to the nearest five cents for cash transactions. Australia and others have also dropped the smallest coins. It's a debate that keeps coming back, mostly because the penny is a case study in how money stays alive long after the economics no longer justify it.

---

**[FACT 5]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number five: one of the largest banknotes ever printed was worth 100 trillion dollars.

In 2009, Zimbabwe issued a 100 trillion dollar banknote during a period of extreme hyperinflation. Prices were rising so fast that the currency was losing value almost by the hour. At the peak of the crisis, the government was printing bills in denominations that seemed almost meaningless.

**[VISUAL: A stack of colorful, oversized banknote images. Text overlay: "100,000,000,000,000."]**

That note, the largest denomination in the country's history, could buy very little. Shops often refused to accept it, and many people used foreign currencies instead. The Zimbabwean dollar was eventually abandoned, and the country moved to using foreign currencies for daily transactions.

This story is a reminder of something economists have observed for centuries: money only works if people trust it. The paper itself was never the problem. When confidence collapsed, a huge number on a note couldn't rescue the value. Hyperinflation is one of the clearest demonstrations of the three jobs of money failing at once.

**[FACT 4]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number four: paper money has been around for more than a thousand years, and it began in China.

Chinese merchants during the Tang dynasty, around the ninth century, used a system of deposit receipts for heavy iron coins. People didn't want to carry large amounts of metal, so they left coins with a merchant and received a paper note in return. Over time, these receipts became a form of currency themselves.

**[VISUAL: Illustrated scene of a merchant's shop with paper notes being exchanged. Map highlighting China.]**

By the Song dynasty, in the 11th century, the government had begun issuing paper money more widely. It was an early experiment in a concept that seems normal today: a piece of paper whose value is backed by the authority that issues it, rather than by the material it's printed on.

This shift was remarkable. It meant money could become lighter, easier to transport, and easier to scale. It also meant governments had to be careful, because printing too much paper could damage confidence. Those early lessons still shape monetary policy today.

---

**[FACT 3]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number three: the first coins we'd recognize as money appeared in Lydia, in what is now western Turkey, around the seventh century BCE.

Before coins, people used a variety of goods and commodities for trade, including grain, livestock, and metals weighed out for each transaction. The Lydians took a different approach. They stamped lumps of electrum, a naturally occurring mix of gold and silver, with an official mark to guarantee their weight and purity.

**[VISUAL: An ancient electrum coin rotates slowly. Animated stamp impressing a mark into the metal.]**

The stamp was the key innovation. It meant a person didn't need to weigh and test every piece of metal before accepting it. The mark itself carried a promise. Historians believe this idea spread quickly across the Greek world and beyond, and it changed how trade worked.

Coins did more than make transactions faster. They allowed governments to control the quality of money and to display their authority. A stamped image or symbol said, in effect, "this is trustworthy, and we stand behind it."

---

**[FACT 2]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number two: most of the money in the modern economy isn't physical cash. It's digital, and it's created in a surprising way.

This one may change how you think about your bank account. The Bank of England has explained in its published research that the majority of money in a modern economy exists as bank deposits, which are essentially electronic records. Physical cash makes up a small share of the total.

**[VISUAL: A stack of banknotes shrinks while a large digital ledger graphic expands behind it.]**

More surprising is how those deposits are created. When a bank makes a loan, it typically credits the borrower's account with a new deposit. That deposit is new money. It didn't come from someone else's savings sitting in a vault. In many cases, lending itself creates money.

This doesn't mean banks can print money without limits. They're regulated, they must manage risk, and central banks influence the overall amount of money through interest rates and other tools. But the common image of banks simply lending out existing savings is incomplete.

If you've ever wondered why money can feel so abstract, this is a big part of the answer. Much of it is a shared digital record, and the system works because people trust the record.

---

**[FACT 1]**

**NARRATOR:**
And finally, number one: some currencies have lasted for more than a thousand years, and the story of one of them goes back to Anglo-Saxon England.

The British pound sterling is one of the oldest currencies still in use. The word "pound" comes from the Latin *libra*, meaning a unit of weight, and the system of pounds, shillings, and pence developed over many centuries. The Bank of England, founded in 1694, became one of the most important financial institutions in the world, and the pound has been a major reserve currency for much of modern history.

**[VISUAL: A timeline graphic across the screen, from early medieval coins to modern banknotes.]**

What makes this so remarkable is continuity. Through wars, plagues, revolutions, and economic upheaval, the currency kept adapting. Its design changed. Its value rose and fell. Its management moved from kings to parliaments to central banks. But the basic idea survived: people agreed to use it.

Money is one of humanity's oldest and most successful inventions. It's not just metal, paper, or digital entries. It's a set of promises that people keep because they believe everyone else will keep them too.
