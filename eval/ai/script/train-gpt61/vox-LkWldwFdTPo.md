# South Sudan may be heading towards genocide

In 2011, people in South Sudan celebrated the birth of their country. Independence was supposed to mean an end to decades of war, and a chance to build a different future.

Today, civilians face another terrifying possibility: that a struggle for political power could become an even wider campaign of violence against entire communities.

United Nations investigators have documented ethnic targeting, attacks on civilians, and the deliberate dismantling of the peace agreement meant to protect them. These are serious warning signs of mass atrocities. Whether they develop into genocide depends, in part, on decisions being made right now. [UN investigation](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2026/02/un-inquiry-finds-south-sudans-political-and-military-leaders-are-driving)

To understand this crisis, start with a distinction: South Sudan and Sudan are separate countries. The devastating war in Sudan affects its southern neighbour, but South Sudan has its own conflict, political leadership, and history.

South Sudan became independent in July 2011. Just two years later, a political confrontation between President Salva Kiir and his former deputy, Riek Machar, escalated into civil war. Fighting spread beyond the capital, turning the promise of independence into another struggle for survival. [UN background](https://unmiss.unmissions.org/en/node/23884)

Kiir is Dinka; Machar is Nuer. Those identities matter because political and military leaders have mobilised support through ethnic networks.

But describing this simply as ethnic hatred misses the point. Communities are not single political actors. A Nuer civilian does not automatically support Machar, and a Dinka civilian does not automatically support Kiir. Treating identity as proof of allegiance is precisely what puts people in danger.

The Global Centre for the Responsibility to Protect identifies leaders’ exploitation of ethnic divisions, competition for power, and persistent impunity as central drivers of the violence. [Atrocity risk assessment](https://www.globalr2p.org/countries/south-sudan/)

In 2018, a revitalised peace agreement offered a way out. It established a framework for sharing power, reforming institutions, and eventually holding elections.

The idea was to replace military competition with political negotiation. Rival leaders would govern together while the country built institutions capable of resolving disputes without another war.

But a peace agreement needs more than signatures. It needs leaders willing to accept limits on their authority, and consequences when they break their commitments. [Peace agreement background](https://www.ictj.org/where-we-work/south-sudan)

That framework has been coming apart.

Machar’s detention in March 2025 marked a major escalation. The UN peacekeeping mission warned that South Sudan’s leaders faced a choice between renewed widespread conflict and the recovery promised by the agreement.

Removing a principal rival from meaningful political participation does more than damage trust. It raises a dangerous question for his supporters: if political negotiation no longer offers protection or influence, what does? [UN warning](https://peacekeeping.un.org/en/node/129015)

In February 2026, UN investigators reported that government forces had carried out widespread and systematic attacks against civilians. Their findings included unlawful killings, aerial bombardment of civilian areas, sexual violence, and forced recruitment.

The commission also identified patterns of attacks linked to ethnicity and perceived political affiliation, particularly against Nuer civilians.

These findings concern specific forces and their leadership. They do not assign responsibility to an entire ethnic community. That distinction matters both for understanding the violence and preventing further retaliation. [UN investigation](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2026/02/un-inquiry-finds-south-sudans-political-and-military-leaders-are-driving)

So, why use the word genocide?

Genocide has a specific legal meaning. It involves certain acts, including killing or inflicting serious harm, committed with the intention of destroying a protected national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, wholly or partly.

Large numbers of civilian deaths do not, by themselves, establish genocide. Proving the intent to destroy a group is essential.

A warning about genocide risk is therefore different from a finding that genocide has occurred. But prevention requires recognising danger before investigators can reconstruct every order, every attack, and every chain of command. [UN explanation](https://www.un.org/genocide-prevention/definition)

The concern is how several threats could reinforce one another.

Ethnic mobilisation can make civilians appear to be legitimate targets. Attacks can generate demands for revenge. When perpetrators escape punishment, armed groups learn that violence carries few consequences.

As political institutions weaken, local disputes can also become connected to national power struggles. A confrontation over cattle, land, or local authority can draw in armed supporters from elsewhere.

This is a risk pathway, not a prediction that every local conflict will become genocide. But it helps explain why apparently separate incidents deserve attention.

In late October 2026, violence in Akop Payam, in Warrap State, reportedly killed at least 84 people, according to state authorities. Homes and markets were burned.

The Global Centre for the Responsibility to Protect warned that recurring raids and retaliatory attacks were unfolding amid political fragmentation and increasingly militarised competition ahead of scheduled elections.

Local violence and national politics cannot safely be treated as unrelated problems. Each can make the other more dangerous. [November atrocity alert](https://www.globalr2p.org/publications/atrocity-alert-no-501/)

Protection is another concern.

Amnesty International reported that authorities and UN peacekeepers had received warnings before the March 2026 attack on Abiemnom, where authorities recorded at least 213 deaths.

UNMISS said it had repeatedly requested additional national security forces. Amnesty questioned why UN reinforcements arrived only afterward.

The practical lesson is stark: an early warning protects nobody unless someone has the ability, authority, and willingness to act on it. [Associated Press reporting](https://apnews.com/article/836a5022f387acbe6f4de51fc9b808ca)

Elections could provide political representation. They could also intensify confrontation if the process excludes major constituencies or proceeds amid widespread insecurity.

That concern is immediate. In November, African Union and UN representatives warned about the dangers surrounding the scheduled December vote. An election cannot resolve a political crisis simply by producing a result. People must have reason to believe they can participate safely and challenge outcomes peacefully. [Election warnings](https://apnews.com/article/2f416a2ec9a0d6e11f492a19f7418154)

What should happen now?

The priorities are concrete: stop attacks on civilians, secure humanitarian access, respond quickly to credible warnings, and hold commanders responsible for crimes committed by their forces.

Regional governments and international partners should press for an inclusive political settlement and support credible investigations. Protection also means listening to people in threatened communities before violence reaches them. These measures require sustained political pressure, resources, and follow-through. [UN recommendations](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2026/02/un-inquiry-finds-south-sudans-political-and-military-leaders-are-driving)

South Sudan’s future is not predetermined.

Calling attention to genocide risk should create urgency without making catastrophe sound inevitable. Leaders can stop incitement. Commanders can prevent attacks. Outside governments can demand accountability.

The purpose of a warning is to change what happens next.

South Sudan’s civilians deserve that chance.
