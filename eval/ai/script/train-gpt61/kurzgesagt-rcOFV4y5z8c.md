# Nuclear Energy Explained: How does it work? 1/3

Imagine powering an entire city by splitting something so small you can’t see it. No flames. No burning coal. Just energy released from the heart of an atom.

That’s nuclear power.

But how can splitting tiny particles produce so much electricity? And how do we keep that process under control?

In this first part, we’ll follow the energy from a single atom all the way to your wall socket.

Let’s start small. Really small.

Everything around you is made of atoms. At the center of each atom is a nucleus, containing protons and neutrons. Surrounding it are electrons.

Most nuclei are stable. But certain heavy nuclei can split apart, releasing energy. This process is called nuclear fission.

One especially useful example is uranium-235.

When a uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron, it can become unstable and split into two smaller nuclei. This releases energy, along with additional neutrons.

Those neutrons can hit other uranium nuclei, causing more fissions. Which release more neutrons. Which cause more fissions.

That’s a chain reaction.

But where does the energy actually come from?

The products of the reaction have slightly less mass than the original ingredients. That difference appears as energy, following Einstein’s famous equation: E equals mc squared.

Because the speed of light squared is an enormous number, a tiny difference in mass corresponds to a tremendous amount of energy.

Most of that energy initially becomes the motion of the split fragments. As they collide with nearby atoms, their motion turns into heat.

And heat is exactly what a power station needs.

Despite the futuristic physics, the electricity-making part is surprisingly familiar.

A nuclear plant uses heat to produce steam. The steam spins a turbine. The turbine drives a generator, which converts mechanical motion into electrical energy.

Coal plants do something similar. The big difference is the heat source: burning fuel versus splitting atoms.

So, what does this look like inside a reactor?

The uranium is typically processed into ceramic pellets. These pellets are stacked inside sealed metal tubes called fuel rods. Groups of rods form fuel assemblies, which sit inside the reactor core.

However, natural uranium contains only a small percentage of uranium-235. For most commercial reactors, that percentage is increased through a process called enrichment.

The fuel used in these reactors is very different from weapons-grade material. A nuclear power reactor cannot explode like an atomic bomb.

But its chain reaction still needs careful control.

That’s where control rods come in.

Made from materials that absorb neutrons, control rods help regulate how many neutrons remain available to cause fission.

Insert them farther into the core, and more neutrons are absorbed. Withdraw them, and more become available to sustain the reaction.

To keep power steady, each fission must, on average, lead to one more fission.

Many reactors also use a moderator, usually ordinary water, to slow neutrons down. Slower neutrons are more likely to trigger fission in uranium-235.

That water often has another job: carrying heat away from the fuel.

In a pressurized water reactor, water flows through the core under high pressure, which keeps it from boiling.

The hot water then passes through a steam generator, transferring heat to a separate water circuit. Water in that second circuit boils, producing the steam that drives the turbine.

The two water supplies stay separate.

Other designs, called boiling water reactors, make steam directly inside the reactor vessel.

After passing through the turbine, the steam is cooled in a condenser and turns back into liquid water, ready to circulate again.

The leftover heat must go somewhere, often into cooling water from a river or the sea, or into the atmosphere through cooling towers.

Those enormous white plumes rising from cooling towers? They’re mainly tiny water droplets formed as water vapor condenses. They aren’t smoke.

Finally, the reactor sits within protective barriers designed to contain radioactive material.

And even after the chain reaction stops, radioactive fission products continue producing heat. This is called decay heat, and it’s why continued cooling is essential after shutdown.

So that’s nuclear electricity: split atoms, capture their heat, make steam, spin a turbine.

The basic idea is simple. The engineering required to do it reliably is anything but.
