# WW2 - OverSimplified (Part 1)
*An original animated history script*

[OPEN ON: A map of Europe, 1919. Smoke rises from a battlefield. A soldier cautiously pokes his head above a trench.]

**NARRATOR:** In 1918, the First World War ended. Millions were dead, empires had collapsed, and Europe had learned an important lesson.

[World leaders gather around a table.]

**LEADER:** We should never do that again.

**OTHER LEADERS:** Absolutely.

[They begin rearranging borders with scissors.]

**NARRATOR:** Unfortunately, agreeing that war was terrible turned out to be much easier than agreeing on a peace that would last.

At Versailles, the victorious powers presented Germany with a treaty. Germany lost territory, faced strict limits on its armed forces, and had to pay enormous reparations. A clause assigning responsibility for war damages became a particular source of German resentment.

**GERMAN DELEGATE:** Can we negotiate?

**ALLIED DELEGATE:** Certainly. Would you prefer to sign with this pen or that pen?

**NARRATOR:** Germany signed. Many Germans felt humiliated, and extremists soon found that blaming every national problem on Versailles was an excellent way to attract an audience.

[An extremist points at a broken toaster.]

**EXTREMIST:** Versailles.

---

[GERMANY, EARLY 1920s. A woman pushes a wheelbarrow full of banknotes.]

**NARRATOR:** Germany’s new democratic government, the Weimar Republic, had a difficult start. Political violence, attempted coups, war debts, and economic disruption all threatened its survival.

In 1923, French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr after Germany fell behind on deliveries owed as reparations. The German government encouraged passive resistance and printed money to support striking workers.

The result helped turn an already serious inflation problem into hyperinflation.

**SHOPKEEPER:** One loaf of bread costs—

[He checks his watch.]

**SHOPKEEPER:** More now.

**NARRATOR:** Savings disappeared. Ordinary people suffered. And one angry Austrian thought this would be a wonderful time to seize power.

[ADOLF HITLER enters a Munich beer hall.]

Hitler led the Nazi Party, an extremist movement built around dictatorship, aggressive nationalism, antisemitism, and racial hierarchy.

In November 1923, he attempted a coup in Munich.

**HITLER:** Follow me! The revolution begins tonight!

[The attempted coup immediately collapses.]

**NARRATOR:** It did not.

Hitler went to prison, where he wrote *Mein Kampf*, setting out his hateful ideology and expansionist ambitions. After his release, he pursued power through elections and political maneuvering, while Nazi paramilitaries continued using intimidation and violence.

For a while, however, Germany’s economy recovered.

[Hitler stands beside an empty donation jar.]

**HITLER:** This is inconvenient.

---

[WALL STREET, 1929. A stock chart plummets through the floor.]

**NARRATOR:** Then came the Great Depression.

American lending had helped sustain Germany’s recovery. When the financial system crashed, loans dried up, businesses failed, and unemployment soared.

Democratic governments struggled to respond. Increasingly desperate voters turned toward parties promising dramatic solutions.

**NAZI CAMPAIGNER:** Jobs! National greatness! Someone else to blame!

**VOTER:** What are your actual policies?

**CAMPAIGNER:** Have you seen our enormous banners?

**NARRATOR:** Nazi electoral support surged. But Hitler never won a majority in a free national election.

Instead, conservative politicians believed they could use his popularity while keeping him under control. In January 1933, President Paul von Hindenburg appointed Hitler chancellor.

**CONSERVATIVE POLITICIAN:** We’ve surrounded him with responsible adults.

[Hitler locks the adults outside.]

**NARRATOR:** Within months, the Nazis dismantled German democracy.

Following the Reichstag fire, emergency measures suspended civil liberties. Political opponents were arrested. The Enabling Act allowed Hitler’s government to legislate without parliament.

Other political parties were banned. Independent trade unions were destroyed. Concentration camps imprisoned opponents and others targeted by the regime.

When Hindenburg died in 1934, Hitler combined the presidency and chancellorship, consolidating his dictatorship.

**NARRATOR:** The people who thought they could control him had made a very serious mistake.

---

[A classroom becomes a propaganda rally.]

**NARRATOR:** Nazi rule reached into schools, newspapers, workplaces, and private life. Propaganda glorified Hitler, while the secret police and a vast machinery of repression punished dissent.

Jewish Germans faced escalating persecution. The Nuremberg Laws stripped them of citizenship rights and prohibited marriages and sexual relationships between Jews and those classified as German.

In November 1938, Nazi-organized violence devastated Jewish communities. Synagogues burned, businesses were smashed, people were murdered, and roughly thirty thousand Jewish men were sent to concentration camps.

[The music stops. A family stands outside its shattered shop.]

**NARRATOR:** These were deliberate steps toward exclusion, dispossession, and ultimately genocide.

Meanwhile, Hitler was rebuilding Germany’s armed forces.

[An inspector examines a tank concealed beneath a bedsheet.]

**INSPECTOR:** Is that a tank?

**GERMAN OFFICIAL:** Agricultural equipment.

**INSPECTOR:** It has a cannon.

**GERMAN OFFICIAL:** Very stubborn potatoes.

**NARRATOR:** Germany openly reintroduced conscription and announced an air force, violating Versailles.

Britain and France protested, but neither was eager to begin another war.

Hitler noticed.

---

[ITALY. BENITO MUSSOLINI poses on a balcony.]

**NARRATOR:** Germany wasn’t Europe’s only dictatorship.

In Italy, Benito Mussolini had established a fascist regime that promised national rebirth, military glory, and substantially more portraits of Benito Mussolini.

He wanted an empire resembling ancient Rome.

**ADVISER:** Ancient Rome took centuries to build.

**MUSSOLINI:** Can we do it by Thursday?

**NARRATOR:** In 1935, Italy invaded Ethiopia, an independent African country and member of the League of Nations.

The League was supposed to preserve peace through collective action.

[League delegates gather.]

**DELEGATE:** We strongly condemn this invasion.

**SECOND DELEGATE:** Should we do something effective?

[An uncomfortable silence.]

**NARRATOR:** Sanctions were limited and failed to stop the conquest. Italian forces used chemical weapons and committed widespread atrocities.

The crisis exposed the League’s weakness and pushed Mussolini closer to Hitler.

Elsewhere, Japan had seized Manchuria in 1931. In 1937, war with China expanded dramatically, bringing devastating violence against civilians, including the Nanjing Massacre.

The international system was failing across multiple continents.

[The League’s “WORLD PEACE” sign falls off the wall.]

---

[EUROPE, 1936.]

**NARRATOR:** Hitler’s next gamble came in the Rhineland, a German region bordering France that Versailles and subsequent agreements had demilitarized.

German troops marched in.

Their orders included withdrawing if France responded militarily. Germany was not ready for a major war.

[German officers peer nervously toward the French border.]

**OFFICER:** Are they coming?

**SECOND OFFICER:** No.

**OFFICER:** Excellent. Continue looking confident.

**NARRATOR:** France, facing political uncertainty and unwilling to act without British support, did not intervene.

For Hitler, it was a major victory.

He also intervened in the Spanish Civil War, supporting Francisco Franco’s Nationalists alongside Mussolini. The conflict gave Germany opportunities to test weapons and tactics, while civilians endured bombing and other atrocities.

Then Hitler turned toward Austria.

Many Austrians supported union with Germany, but others opposed Nazism or feared annexation. Hitler applied threats and political pressure, and German troops entered Austria in March 1938.

Austria was absorbed into the German Reich.

[Hitler studies a larger map.]

**HITLER:** This map is improving.

**NARRATOR:** His ambitions were expanding with it.

---

[CZECHOSLOVAKIA appears on the map.]

**NARRATOR:** Next came Czechoslovakia, a democratic state with a strong industrial base, a substantial army, and defensive fortifications.

Hitler demanded the Sudetenland, a border region with a large ethnic German population.

He claimed to be protecting Germans abroad. In reality, he was using the issue to dismantle a neighboring country.

Europe approached war.

Britain’s prime minister, Neville Chamberlain, believed negotiation could resolve legitimate grievances and avert another catastrophe. British and French leaders also worried about military readiness and the destruction a new war might bring.

So, in September 1938, Britain, France, Germany, and Italy met in Munich.

**CZECHOSLOVAK REPRESENTATIVE:** Where’s my chair?

**OFFICIAL:** You’re not invited.

**CZECHOSLOVAK REPRESENTATIVE:** But that’s my country on the table.

**NARRATOR:** The agreement handed the Sudetenland to Germany.

Chamberlain returned home expressing confidence that peace had been preserved.

[An exhausted European family exhales.]

**NARRATOR:** For people who remembered the trenches, that hope was powerful.

But in March 1939, Hitler occupied the Czech lands. Slovakia became a nominally independent state under German domination.

His expansion had plainly gone beyond uniting German-speaking populations.

Britain and France now guaranteed Polish independence.

[Hitler looks toward Poland.]

**NARRATOR:** Unfortunately, guarantees only deter an aggressor who believes you will enforce them.

---

[MOSCOW. A portrait of STALIN watches over a meeting.]

**NARRATOR:** There was another major power to consider: the Soviet Union.

Hitler’s ideology was fiercely anti-communist, and he wanted to conquer territory in Eastern Europe. Stalin distrusted Nazi Germany, but he also distrusted Britain and France.

Attempts to negotiate an anti-German arrangement failed amid mutual suspicion and conflicting interests.

Then came a diplomatic surprise.

In August 1939, Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union signed a nonaggression pact.

**GERMAN DIPLOMAT:** We agree not to attack you.

**SOVIET DIPLOMAT:** We agree to find that temporarily useful.

**NARRATOR:** A secret protocol divided much of Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres of influence, including plans affecting Poland.

For Hitler, the pact reduced the immediate danger of fighting the Soviet Union while attacking in the west.

For Stalin, it offered time and territorial opportunities.

For Poland, it was disastrous.

[Two giant hands move across a map toward the same country.]

**NARRATOR:** On September 1, 1939, Germany invaded Poland.

---

[German aircraft roar overhead.]

**NARRATOR:** German forces combined concentrated armored attacks, mobile infantry, artillery, and air support to break through Polish defenses and disrupt communications.

This approach became associated with the term “blitzkrieg,” or lightning war.

It wasn’t magic. It depended on planning, coordination, supplies, and opponents being unable to recover quickly enough.

[An officer draws a sweeping arrow.]

**OFFICER:** Advance rapidly!

**SUPPLY OFFICER:** With fuel?

**OFFICER:** Ideally.

**NARRATOR:** Poland fought fiercely, but faced superior German forces and a difficult strategic position.

And no, Polish cavalry did not foolishly charge German tanks with lances. Cavalry generally fought dismounted, though mounted charges against infantry did occur.

**POLISH SOLDIER:** Could everyone please stop repeating that?

**NARRATOR:** On September 3, Britain and France declared war on Germany.

Two weeks later, the Soviet Union invaded Poland from the east.

Caught between two invading powers, Poland was overwhelmed. Warsaw surrendered late in September, though resistance continued and a Polish government operated in exile.

The occupiers imposed brutal repression. Germany began mass killings and persecution, particularly targeting Polish elites and Jewish communities. Soviet authorities carried out deportations, imprisonment, and executions.

The war had begun with the destruction of a country.

It would grow far larger.

---

[WESTERN FRONT. A French soldier watches an empty field.]

**NARRATOR:** After declaring war, Britain and France did not launch a major offensive capable of relieving Poland.

For months, the Western Front remained comparatively quiet. Newspapers called it the “Phoney War.”

**SOLDIER:** This war seems unusually—

[An officer clamps a hand over his mouth.]

**OFFICER:** Don’t finish that sentence.

**NARRATOR:** At sea, there was nothing phoney about it. German submarines attacked shipping, while Britain used its naval strength to blockade Germany.

In the east, the Soviet Union attacked Finland in November 1939.

Soviet leaders expected a quick victory.

[An enormous Soviet formation approaches a snowy forest.]

**SOVIET OFFICER:** There are far more of us.

[A Finnish soldier disappears into the snow.]

**NARRATOR:** The Finns made that advantage extremely expensive.

Using terrain, mobility, and winter conditions, Finnish forces inflicted heavy losses. Nevertheless, Soviet numerical and material superiority eventually forced Finland to accept peace and surrender territory in March 1940.

The Soviet military’s poor early performance attracted attention in Berlin.

**HITLER:** Interesting.

**NARRATOR:** He would draw some dangerously confident conclusions.

Meanwhile, everyone in Western Europe waited for Germany’s next move.

It came from the north.

---

[APRIL 1940. DENMARK AND NORWAY.]

**NARRATOR:** Germany invaded Denmark and Norway to secure strategic positions and protect access to Swedish iron ore shipped through Norway.

Denmark was rapidly overwhelmed.

Norway resisted, supported by British and French forces, but Germany captured key ports and airfields. After weeks of fighting, the Allies withdrew.

The campaign also triggered a political crisis in Britain.

Neville Chamberlain lost support, and on May 10, Winston Churchill became prime minister.

[Churchill enters an office stacked with urgent folders.]

**AIDE:** Congratulations, Prime Minister.

[An explosion rattles the window.]

**AIDE:** The timing could be better.

**NARRATOR:** That same day, Germany attacked the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, and France.

The Allies expected a major advance through Belgium, similar to Germany’s approach in the First World War.

Their strongest mobile forces moved north to meet it.

But Germany’s decisive armored thrust came through the Ardennes, a heavily wooded region the French considered less suitable for a large mechanized assault.

[An Allied planner labels the forest “DIFFICULT.”]

[A German tank drives over the label.]

**NARRATOR:** Difficult turned out to be different from impossible.

German forces crossed the Meuse near Sedan and raced toward the English Channel.

---

[A German arrow reaches the coast.]

**NARRATOR:** The breakthrough cut off large Allied armies in Belgium and northern France.

Communications broke down. Headquarters struggled to understand how quickly the situation was deteriorating.

**FRENCH OFFICER:** Where are the Germans?

**MESSENGER:** Behind us.

**OFFICER:** Which Germans?

**MESSENGER:** That is becoming a complicated question.

**NARRATOR:** Allied troops retreated toward Dunkirk, where the Royal Navy organized an evacuation.

Destroyers, transports, and civilian vessels helped carry troops away. Many of the smaller boats ferried men from beaches to larger ships offshore.

German aircraft attacked the evacuation, while the Royal Air Force fought to protect it.

Over nine days, more than 338,000 British and Allied troops escaped.

[An exhausted soldier climbs aboard a ship.]

**SOLDIER:** We made it.

[He looks back toward the smoke.]

**NARRATOR:** It was an extraordinary rescue, but it came after a military disaster. Huge quantities of equipment were abandoned, and many Allied soldiers remained trapped or became prisoners.

Churchill warned that wars were not won by evacuations.

France continued fighting, but its position was collapsing.

Italy now entered the war on Germany’s side.

**MUSSOLINI:** I have arrived to help win!

**NARRATOR:** His timing reflected considerable confidence in France’s imminent defeat.

---

[PARIS, JUNE 1940.]

**NARRATOR:** German troops entered Paris on June 14.

France requested an armistice, signed on June 22 in the same railway carriage used for the German armistice of 1918.

Hitler had arranged the symbolism carefully.

Germany occupied northern and western France. A French government based at Vichy controlled the unoccupied zone and collaborated with Nazi Germany.

But some French people refused to accept defeat.

From London, Charles de Gaulle called for resistance and continued the struggle through the Free French movement.

[The map of Europe darkens.]

**NARRATOR:** In a matter of weeks, Germany had defeated a country widely regarded as one of Europe’s strongest military powers.

Britain now faced the prospect of invasion.

It still had its empire, Commonwealth partners, powerful navy, and growing support from abroad. But across the Channel stood a victorious enemy controlling much of Western Europe.

**GERMAN OFFICER:** Shall we invade?

**NAVAL OFFICER:** Across that water?

**GERMAN OFFICER:** Yes.

**NAVAL OFFICER:** With the Royal Navy in it?

**NARRATOR:** Germany needed control of the skies.

And Britain intended to contest every mile.

---

[SUMMER 1940. RADAR STATIONS FACE THE CHANNEL.]

**NARRATOR:** The Luftwaffe attacked shipping, airfields, and the infrastructure supporting Britain’s air defenses.

The Royal Air Force was outnumbered in important respects, but it had an integrated system linking radar, observers, command centers, and fighter squadrons.

[An operator moves markers across a map.]

**OPERATOR:** Enemy aircraft approaching.

**PILOT:** Where?

**OPERATOR:** We’ve actually developed a system for answering that.

**NARRATOR:** British aircraft production, repair crews, ground staff, and pilots from Britain and many other countries sustained the defense.

The fighting was exhausting. Losses mounted on both sides.

Germany increasingly directed attacks toward London and other cities. The Blitz killed tens of thousands of civilians and devastated neighborhoods, but failed to force Britain to surrender.

[Residents emerge from a shelter beside a shattered street.]

**NARRATOR:** By autumn, Germany had failed to gain the air superiority it needed. The invasion was postponed indefinitely.

Hitler had conquered much of Europe, but Britain remained in the war.

And elsewhere, the conflict was spreading: across the Mediterranean, into North Africa, and throughout Asia.

[The camera pulls back from Europe to reveal the whole globe.]

**NARRATOR:** Soon, Hitler would make his biggest gamble yet, Japan would attack a sleeping industrial giant, and a European war would become a truly global catastrophe.

[FADE OUT.]

**TO BE CONTINUED.**
