Imagine stepping into a booth in New York, pressing a button, and stepping out in Tokyo. No airports. No middle seat. Just… zap.

Teleporters are a science fiction staple. But how would one actually work?

First, we need to separate two very different ideas: teleporting matter and teleporting quantum information.

Let’s start with the sci-fi version.

A classic teleporter scans your body, sends the instructions somewhere else, and rebuilds you at the destination. Think of it as an incredibly advanced combination of a scanner, a communications network, and a 3D printer.

Sounds straightforward—until you consider the details.

Your body contains roughly seven billion billion billion atoms. A teleporter would need enough information to reconstruct their arrangement and the biological processes keeping you alive.

And if the design requires copying your exact quantum state, there’s another obstacle: quantum physics forbids perfectly copying an arbitrary, unknown quantum state. That’s called the no-cloning theorem.

Then comes the question science fiction loves to dodge: what happens to the original?

If the machine creates a duplicate while you remain behind, you haven’t traveled. You’ve gained a very confusing roommate.

If it destroys the original, is the person arriving really you—or someone who remembers being you?

Physics alone doesn’t settle that philosophical question.

But here’s the twist: scientists already perform something called quantum teleportation.

It doesn’t move people, atoms, or objects across space. Instead, it transfers a quantum state from one system to another.

The process uses entanglement: a shared quantum connection established between particles.

Suppose Alice wants to teleport a particle’s state to Bob. They first share an entangled pair. Alice performs a joint measurement involving her entangled particle and the particle carrying the state.

She then sends Bob the measurement result through an ordinary communications channel.

Using that information, Bob applies the appropriate operation to his particle, recreating the original state.

The original state isn’t retained as an independent copy, so the no-cloning rule stays intact.

And because Bob needs Alice’s ordinary message, the process cannot transmit usable information faster than light.

So, will we ever teleport ourselves to work?

We have no demonstrated method for teleporting a human, and quantum teleportation isn’t a blueprint for a people-moving machine.

For now, real teleportation helps researchers explore quantum communication and future networks.

Which is impressive—even if it still won’t save you from your morning commute.
