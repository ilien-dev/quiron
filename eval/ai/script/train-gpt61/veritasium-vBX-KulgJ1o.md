# Would You Take This Bet?

Someone places a black box on the table in front of you.

On top is a red button.

“Press it,” they say, “and you have a fifty-one percent chance of winning a million dollars.”

You look at the button.

“What happens the other forty-nine percent of the time?”

“You owe us a million dollars.”

Suddenly, your hand stops moving.

A second ago, you were imagining a different life. Now you’re wondering whether bankruptcy follows you across international borders.

But mathematically, this is a good bet.

You’re more likely to win than lose.

So why does pressing that button feel like a terrible idea?

And could someone change the offer just slightly… until refusing it became the mistake?

Today, we’re going to build a bet.

You decide when you’re in.

---

Let’s start smaller.

I flip a fair coin.

Heads, you win eleven dollars.

Tails, you lose ten.

Would you take it?

Don’t spend too long calculating. Just notice your first reaction.

Maybe you’re thinking, “Sure. It’s ten bucks.”

Maybe you want to inspect the coin.

Fair enough. It’s a normal coin. Nobody’s doing magic. The terms are guaranteed.

Here’s what the math says.

Half the time, you gain eleven dollars. Half the time, you lose ten.

Average those outcomes, and your expected profit is fifty cents per flip.

That doesn’t mean the coin somehow gives you fifty cents.

You’ll either win eleven or lose ten.

The fifty cents describes what your average profit would tend toward over a very large number of flips.

One flip, though?

Anything can happen.

Which brings us to the first problem with saying a bet is “good.”

Good over how many attempts?

---

Imagine I offer you this game once.

You might say no.

Now imagine I offer it a thousand times, with stakes small enough that you can comfortably survive a bad streak.

That sounds much more appealing.

The rules didn’t change. The edge didn’t change.

What changed was your opportunity to let that edge play out.

But there’s a catch.

You need enough money to stay in the game.

If you start with ten dollars, lose the first flip, and can’t borrow, you’re finished.

The other nine hundred and ninety-nine opportunities are irrelevant.

You can be offered a favorable game and still run out of money before you benefit from it.

Keep that in mind.

We’re going to make the offer much better.

---

You now have one thousand dollars.

I offer you a coin flip with these rules:

Heads, your money increases by fifty percent.

Tails, it decreases by forty percent.

So a win turns your thousand into fifteen hundred.

A loss turns it into six hundred.

Equal chances.

Bigger upside than downside.

Your expected balance after one flip is ten hundred and fifty dollars.

A five percent expected gain.

Would you play?

And if you would play once, would you keep playing?

Before you answer, let’s try two flips.

You win the first one. Your thousand becomes fifteen hundred.

Then you lose forty percent.

You’re left with nine hundred dollars.

Hang on.

One win. One loss.

And you’re down a hundred dollars.

Try reversing the order.

Lose first: six hundred.

Then gain fifty percent: nine hundred.

Same result.

The percentages looked friendly when we averaged them.

But your money doesn’t experience an average of two possible futures.

It follows one path, with each change multiplying whatever survived the last change.

---

Here’s where this gets strange.

After two flips, there are four equally likely sequences.

Win, win: twenty-two hundred and fifty dollars.

Win, lose: nine hundred.

Lose, win: nine hundred.

Lose, lose: three hundred and sixty.

Average those balances, and you get eleven hundred and two dollars and fifty cents.

The average is up.

Yet three of the four outcomes leave you poorer.

Both statements are true.

A relatively large winning outcome pulls the average above your starting balance, even though losing money is more likely.

And if you keep betting your entire balance under these rules, a roughly even mix of wins and losses keeps grinding it down.

Every win-loss pair multiplies your money by zero point nine.

After ten such pairs, your original thousand is worth about three hundred and forty-nine dollars.

That five percent expected gain suddenly feels less comforting.

---

Picture a room full of people playing this game.

Everyone starts with a thousand dollars.

After enough rounds, a few people have extraordinary winning streaks.

Their balances are enormous.

They raise the room’s average, and someone points at that number and says, “Look how well everyone is doing.”

Meanwhile, most players are looking at much smaller balances.

This is why you should be careful whenever someone uses an average to describe an experience.

Ask what the distribution looks like.

Ask how many people actually got the result being advertised.

And ask whether the number describes many people playing once, or one person playing repeatedly.

Those can tell very different stories.

---

But we can fix our game.

You don’t have to put your whole balance at risk.

Suppose you expose only ten percent of your money to each flip and leave the rest untouched.

Now a win increases your total balance by five percent.

A loss decreases it by four percent.

Start with a thousand.

Win once, and you have ten hundred and fifty.

Then lose four percent, and you have ten hundred and eight.

One win. One loss.

This time, you’re ahead.

Same coin.

Same underlying payoff.

Different amount exposed.

That decision changes everything.

Of course, a bad streak can still hurt. Smaller bets don’t remove uncertainty.

But they can change whether repeated play helps you or destroys you.

Your choice isn’t limited to accepting or rejecting an opportunity.

Sometimes the most important question is: how much?

---

Now let’s return to the black box.

Fifty-one percent chance of winning a million.

Forty-nine percent chance of owing a million.

The expected profit is twenty thousand dollars.

That number is correct.

It’s also missing almost everything you care about.

Could you pay the debt?

Would losing affect your housing? Your family? The next twenty years of your life?

And how much would winning actually improve things?

For someone with billions, the stakes might barely register.

For someone with a few hundred dollars, the downside could be overwhelming.

The same offer can reasonably produce different answers from different people.

Even a guaranteed payment to walk away might be more valuable to you than a gamble with a higher expected payout.

Money has consequences. Those consequences don’t scale neatly with the number printed on the check.

---

Let’s change one final detail.

You can take the million-dollar bet a hundred times.

Exciting?

Wait.

You must settle each loss immediately.

You cannot borrow.

And you cannot reduce the stakes.

If you can’t cover the first loss, having a hundred chances doesn’t help.

“Repeat it enough times” only works when you can actually repeat it.

That condition gets quietly skipped in a lot of confident explanations about probability.

The spreadsheet reaches the final round.

You might not.

Before trusting the long run, check what it takes to get there.

---

So, would you take this bet?

By now, I hope your answer is a question.

What are the odds?

What can I lose?

Can I afford that loss?

How many times can I play?

Can I change the size of the bet?

And is the person offering it showing me the whole picture?

Because “positive expected value” tells you something useful.

It doesn’t make the decision for you.

Neither does fear.

Sometimes we reject tiny, manageable risks because losing feels worse than winning feels good.

Sometimes we chase a favorable-looking number while ignoring a downside we cannot survive.

The challenge is telling those situations apart.

So imagine that box one last time.

The button is waiting.

The person across the table asks, “Well? Are you going to press it?”

You look at the box.

Then you ask:

“Can we talk about the stakes?”
