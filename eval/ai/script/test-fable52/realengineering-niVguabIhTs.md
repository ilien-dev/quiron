# Burj Khalifa – How To Build Higher

**[COLD OPEN]**

*[VISUAL: Dawn over the desert. A silver needle pierces a blanket of cloud.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
There is a building so tall that you can watch the sun set from the ground, ride an elevator to the top, and watch it set all over again.

At 828 meters, the Burj Khalifa didn't just break the record for the world's tallest building. It crushed it. The previous champion, Taipei 101, stood at 508 meters. The Burj beat it by more than 300, the biggest leap in skyscraper history.

But you can't build this high by taking a normal skyscraper and adding floors. Past a certain height, the rules change and the old solutions stop working.

So how do you build higher?

*[TITLE CARD: BURJ KHALIFA – HOW TO BUILD HIGHER]*

---

**[PART ONE: THE REAL ENEMY]**

*[VISUAL: Animation of a simple tower. Arrows push down, then sideways.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Ask most people what makes a tall building difficult, and they'll say weight. All that concrete and steel pressing down.

That's part of it. But for a supertall tower, the real enemy is the wind.

*[VISUAL: Wind streaming around a rectangular tower, swirling off alternate sides.]*

When wind flows around a tall object, it doesn't just push. It peels away from the sides in spinning pockets of air called vortices, first on the left, then the right, then the left again. Each one gives the building a tiny sideways tug.

If those tugs fall into rhythm with the building's natural sway, the motion amplifies, like pushing a child on a swing at exactly the right moment. This is called vortex shedding, and it can make a tower rock hard enough to make the people inside feel sick.

The traditional fix is brute force: more steel, more stiffness, more mass. But at 800 meters, brute force becomes impossibly heavy and impossibly expensive.

The team at Skidmore, Owings & Merrill, led by architect Adrian Smith and structural engineer Bill Baker, needed a smarter idea.

---

**[PART TWO: THE SHAPE]**

*[VISUAL: Top-down view of the tower's Y-shaped floor plan.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Their answer starts with the shape.

Seen from above, the Burj Khalifa is a Y. Three wings radiate from a central hexagonal core. The form echoes the petals of a desert flower, the Hymenocallis, but the reason for it is structural.

Baker called the system the "buttressed core." Each wing braces the other two. Push against one, and the other two push back, like a tripod. The core holds the elevators and resists twisting. The wings stop the core from bending.

*[VISUAL: The three wings highlighted in turn, force arrows flowing into the core.]*

The result is a tower that is extraordinarily stiff without being extraordinarily heavy. And because each wing is narrow, the apartments and hotel rooms inside stay close to the windows.

That solved stiffness. It did not solve the wind.

---

**[PART THREE: CONFUSING THE WIND]**

*[VISUAL: Scale model of the tower in a wind tunnel, smoke streaming past.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
For that, the engineers went to the wind tunnel, where they ran more than forty tests.

What they learned shaped everything. Vortices form in an organized way when a building has the same shape all the way up. So the designers made sure it never does.

*[VISUAL: Animation of the tower rising, each wing stepping back in a spiral.]*

As the tower climbs, the wings step back one at a time, in a spiral pattern. Each setback changes the building's width, so at every level the wind meets a different shape and sheds vortices at a different rhythm.

The vortices never get organized. They never sync up. Baker's phrase for it has become famous: they were "confusing the wind."

The testing even changed the tower's orientation. The team rotated the whole design on its site so that the wings met Dubai's prevailing winds at the most favorable angle.

The Burj still moves, as every tall building does. At the very tip, it can sway well over a meter. But it does so slowly and gently, and far less than a conventional tower of that height ever could.

---

**[PART FOUR: STANDING ON SAND]**

*[VISUAL: Excavation footage, 2004. Drilling rigs on a flat, dusty site.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Now for the other end of the building.

Manhattan's skyscrapers stand on hard bedrock. Dubai has no such luck. Beneath the site lies weak, crumbly sandstone and siltstone, soaked in groundwater saltier than the sea.

So the tower doesn't rest on rock. It grips the ground.

*[VISUAL: Cutaway animation showing piles extending deep beneath a thick concrete slab.]*

Engineers drilled 192 concrete piles, each one and a half meters wide, to a depth of roughly 50 meters. The piles don't stand on anything solid. They work by friction, with the rough surface of each pile clinging to the soil around it.

On top sits a concrete raft 3.7 meters thick, spreading the load of half a million tonnes.

And because that salty groundwater would eat away at ordinary concrete and steel, the mix was specially formulated to be dense and resistant, and the foundation is fitted with a cathodic protection system, an electrical defense against corrosion.

---

**[PART FIVE: CONCRETE IN THE SKY]**

*[VISUAL: Night-time construction footage. Floodlights, cranes, workers.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Most supertall towers before the Burj were steel. This one is mostly reinforced concrete, some 330,000 cubic meters of it. Concrete is stiff, it damps vibration, and it's ideal for residential floors.

But concrete brings two problems. The first is heat.

Dubai summers can pass 45 degrees Celsius. Pour concrete in that, and it sets too quickly and cracks. So in the hottest months the crews poured at night and added ice to the mix.

The second problem is getting it up there.

*[VISUAL: Animation of a pipeline running up the tower, a counter ticking toward 606 m.]*

Concrete was pumped from the ground straight up the building. Too stiff, and the mix would jam the pipe. Too runny, and it wouldn't be strong enough. The mix had to stay fluid for the whole journey upward, then harden into some of the strongest concrete ever used in a building.

In 2008, the pumps pushed concrete to a height of 606 meters, a world record.

At its peak, around 12,000 workers were on site every day. The formwork climbed with the tower, and a new floor appeared roughly every three days.

---

**[PART SIX: THE SPIRE]**

*[VISUAL: The upper tower, steel framework against blue sky.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Above the concrete, the structure switches to steel, which is lighter and easier to lift at extreme heights.

Then comes the final piece, the pinnacle: a steel pipe more than 200 meters long. No crane on Earth could lift it into place, so the builders didn't try.

*[VISUAL: Animation of the spire sliding upward out of the tower's top.]*

They assembled it inside the building, then pushed it up through the top with hydraulic jacks, like a telescope extending.

In January 2009, the spire reached its full height, and the tallest structure humans had ever built was complete.

---

**[PART SEVEN: LIVING AT ALTITUDE]**

*[VISUAL: Interior shots of elevators, lobbies, the observation deck.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Building it was one challenge. Making it livable was another.

A single elevator can't practically serve 160 floors, because the cables alone would become too heavy. So the Burj works like a vertical railway. Express elevators carry people to sky lobbies, where they switch to local ones. In all there are 57 elevators, and the observation deck cars travel at 10 meters per second.

*[VISUAL: Sunlight flaring off the glass façade.]*

The exterior is wrapped in around 26,000 glass panels, each cut by hand and coated to reflect the desert heat.

And the hot, humid air meeting the building's cold cooling system produces condensation, enough to fill about twenty Olympic swimming pools a year. It's collected and used to water the gardens below.

---

**[CLOSING: HIGHER STILL]**

*[VISUAL: Fireworks. Opening night, January 4th, 2010.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The Burj Khalifa opened on January 4th, 2010. Six years of construction, around one and a half billion dollars, and 22 million hours of labor.

More than fifteen years later, it still holds the record.

*[VISUAL: Slow aerial orbit around the spire at sunset.]*

But its real legacy is the method. The buttressed core showed that the path upward isn't more material. It's better geometry: work with the wind instead of fighting it, and let the shape do the work.

That thinking is already shaping the next generation. In Saudi Arabia, the Jeddah Tower, designed by the same architect, aims for a full kilometer.

So how do you build higher? Not with more strength. With more understanding.
