# How I Stopped Hating Breakfast

**[COLD OPEN: kitchen, 7:04 a.m., harsh overhead light]**

(V.O.) For most of my adult life, this was my breakfast.

**[Shot: a hand pouring coffee into a mug. Nothing else on the counter.]**

Coffee. Black. Standing up. Usually while reading an email that made me angry.

And if you'd asked me why, I would have given you the speech. "I'm just not a breakfast person. My stomach doesn't wake up until noon. Breakfast is a scam invented by cereal companies."

I said that last one at parties. I was fun.

But here's the thing I never admitted: I didn't skip breakfast because I'd made some enlightened choice. I skipped it because I actively, genuinely hated it. The food, the timing, the whole ritual.

And then, about eight months ago, something changed. Now breakfast is the part of my day I protect most fiercely.

This is the story of how that happened, and it's not what the wellness people told me it would be.

**[TITLE CARD: How I Stopped Hating Breakfast]**

---

## Part One: The Hate

**[Sitting at the table, talking to camera]**

Let me be clear about what I mean by "hate," because I don't think I'm alone here.

When I was a kid, breakfast was a negotiation I always lost. My mom, who I love, believed that a child could not leave the house without eating. So every morning at 6:40, there was a bowl of something in front of me, and I was nauseous. Not dramatic nauseous. Just that low, gray, early-morning feeling where food looks like a threat.

I'd sit there pushing soggy cereal around while the clock ticked, and the bus got closer, and everyone got more annoyed. So before I was ten, my brain had filed breakfast under: pressure, nausea, and being late.

**[B-roll: slow-motion cereal going soggy in milk. Sad music, played for laughs.]**

Then I became an adult, and nobody could make me eat. So I didn't. For over a decade.

And honestly? It felt like winning.

But when I finally sat down and thought about it, I realized I had three separate problems with breakfast, and I'd been lumping them together into one big "no."

**[On-screen text: Problem 1: The Food]**

Problem one: I don't like breakfast food. I said it. Most traditional breakfast food, at least the kind I grew up with, is dessert pretending to be a meal. Sweet cereal, pastries, pancakes, juice, sweetened yogurt. I don't have a sweet tooth, and at seven in the morning, sugar makes me feel like I've been lightly poisoned.

**[On-screen text: Problem 2: The Timing]**

Problem two: I'm not hungry when I wake up. At all. And every piece of advice says, "Eat within thirty minutes of waking!" Which, for me, meant forcing food into a body that was clearly saying, "No thank you."

**[On-screen text: Problem 3: The Rush]**

Problem three, and this was the sneaky one: breakfast only ever existed inside the most stressful window of my day. I never once ate it calmly. It was always a task standing between me and the door.

So of course I hated it. I'd hate dinner too if I had to eat it nauseous, in four minutes, and it was cake.

---

## Part Two: The Crash

**[Walking outside, handheld]**

So what changed? I wish I could say I had an epiphany. What I actually had was a 3 p.m. problem.

Last winter I noticed a pattern. Coffee until noon, feel sharp, feel great. Then lunch, a huge one, because by then I was starving. And by three o'clock I was useless. Foggy, irritable, staring at the same paragraph for twenty minutes. Then I'd snack my way through the evening and go to bed too full.

I complained about this to my friend Dani, who is a nurse and has zero patience for me. And Dani said, and I'm quoting, "You run on caffeine and spite for six hours and then you're surprised you fall apart?"

Which, rude. But fair.

Now, quick disclaimer: I'm not a doctor or a dietitian, and I'm not here to tell you breakfast is mandatory. Plenty of people skip it and feel fantastic. The research on this is genuinely mixed, and your body is not my body. This is just what I found out about mine.

So Dani made me a bet. Eat breakfast every day for thirty days. If I still hated it, I'd never hear about it again.

And I said yes, mostly because I wanted to prove that I'd been right all along.

---

## Part Three: The Experiment

**[Kitchen, daylight, cooking b-roll]**

Week one was a disaster.

I did exactly what I'd always done: tried to eat "normal" breakfast at the "normal" time. Oatmeal at 6:45. Granola. A banana in the car. I hated every bite, I felt queasy, and by day five I was ready to pay Dani and quit.

**[Shot: me, staring at oatmeal with open hostility.]**

But on day six I was running late, skipped it, and ended up eating last night's leftovers at about 9:30. Rice, a fried egg, some kimchi, a little chili oil.

And it was... great? I wasn't nauseous. I wasn't choking it down. I actually enjoyed it. And that afternoon, three o'clock came and went, and I didn't notice.

That was the moment it clicked. I didn't hate breakfast. I hated one very specific version of breakfast that someone else had designed.

So I threw out the rulebook and wrote my own. Four rules. Here they are.

**[On-screen text: Rule 1: Breakfast is a time, not a menu]**

Rule one: breakfast is a time of day, not a category of food.

There is no law that says morning food has to be sweet, or beige, or shaped like a circle. In much of the world, breakfast is just... food. Soup. Rice. Beans. Fish. Bread with tomato and olive oil. Last night's stew.

Once I gave myself permission to eat savory, everything opened up. These days my regular rotation is eggs on rice, beans on toast, leftover soup, or a plate of cheese, olives, cucumber, and bread that takes ninety seconds to assemble.

If you love pancakes, eat pancakes. But if you've spent your whole life thinking you hate breakfast, ask yourself whether you actually just hate cereal.

**[On-screen text: Rule 2: Eat when you're hungry, not when you're awake]**

Rule two: wait for the hunger.

I stopped trying to eat the second I got up. Now I wake up, drink water, have my coffee, and get on with my morning. Somewhere between ninety minutes and two hours later, my stomach actually shows up and asks for something. That's when I eat.

Is that technically breakfast? Is it brunch? Is it "first lunch"? I don't care, and nobody is grading this. The first meal counts whenever it happens.

If you leave early for work, this might mean packing it and eating at your desk, or on a break. That's what I do on office days: a container of something, eaten at 9:15 like a civilized person, instead of at 6:45 like a hostage.

**[On-screen text: Rule 3: Make it boring to prepare]**

Rule three: zero decisions in the morning.

Morning me is not a chef. Morning me can barely operate a zipper. So I stopped expecting that person to cook.

On Sunday I do about twenty-five minutes of prep. I boil six eggs. I cook a pot of rice or a batch of beans. I wash and chop whatever vegetables are around. That's it.

**[B-roll: Sunday prep montage. Eggs, rice cooker, containers stacking.]**

Then every morning is assembly, not cooking. Scoop, top, eat. And I keep it deliberately repetitive, basically the same three meals on a loop. People think variety makes habits stick, but for me it was the opposite. Every choice was a chance to say, "Eh, I'll skip it." Removing the choice removed the excuse.

**[On-screen text: Rule 4: Sit down]**

Rule four, and this is the one that actually changed how I feel about mornings: sit down. Ten minutes. No phone.

I know. I know how that sounds. I rolled my eyes too.

But remember problem three: breakfast only ever existed inside a panic. As long as I was eating over the sink while scrolling through bad news, my body was still filing this meal under "stress."

So I made one small change. I sit at the table, by the window, with my plate and my coffee, and I do nothing else for ten minutes.

**[Shot: wide, quiet. Table by the window, morning light. Hold for a few seconds. No music.]**

The first few days were weirdly uncomfortable. I kept reaching for my phone like a phantom limb. But around week three, I noticed I was looking forward to it. Not the food, exactly. The pause. It became the one part of my day that didn't belong to anyone else. No inbox, no notifications, no one needing anything. Just me, eggs, and a window.

And that's when I understood that I'd been wrong about what breakfast was. I thought it was a nutritional obligation. It turns out it can be ten minutes of quiet before the world gets in.

## Part Four: What Actually Happened

**[Back at the table, to camera]**

So. Thirty days ended. Did I win the bet?

No. I lost badly. Dani has been insufferable about it.

Here's what changed, honestly, without the miracle language.

The three o'clock crash mostly disappeared. Not every day. If I sleep badly, I still feel it. But it went from a daily event to an occasional one.

My lunches got smaller and saner, because I wasn't arriving at noon ready to eat the table.

The evening snacking dropped off without me trying.

And I got a lot less snappy in the mornings. My partner noticed that one before I did, and mentioned it with what I'd describe as visible relief.

What didn't happen: I didn't lose a dramatic amount of weight. I didn't become a morning person. I didn't start waking up at five to journal. I still think the 7 a.m. smoothie-bowl people are operating on a different plane of existence, and I wish them well.

**[B-roll: me attempting a smoothie bowl. It goes poorly.]**

And there were things that didn't work. I tried overnight oats four separate ways, and I'm now confident it's not the recipe, it's me. I tried protein shakes, and they made me feel like I was drinking a chore. I tried getting up earlier to cook something elaborate, and that lasted exactly two days.

All of that was useful, though. Every failure told me something about what I actually like, instead of what I thought I was supposed to like.

---

## Part Five: If You Hate Breakfast Too

**[Kitchen, casual]**

So if you're watching this as a fellow breakfast hater, here's what I'd suggest. Not a thirty-day challenge. Just one week, and one question.

The question is: what exactly do I hate?

Is it the food? Then change the food. Eat dinner for breakfast. Eat a sandwich. Eat cold pizza with an egg on it. I'm not your supervisor.

Is it the timing? Then move it. Nine, ten, whenever your body is actually ready.

Is it the rush? Then shrink the effort to almost nothing, and give yourself even five minutes sitting down.

And if you try all of that and you still feel better without it, genuinely, skip it. That's a real answer. The point of this video isn't "everyone must eat breakfast." The point is that I spent thirty years hating something I had never actually tried on my own terms.

I'd inherited a version of breakfast from childhood, decided it was the only version, and rejected the whole thing. And I wonder how many other things in my life work like that. Things I "just don't like" that I've only ever experienced in their worst possible form. Exercise was like that for me too, but that's another video.
