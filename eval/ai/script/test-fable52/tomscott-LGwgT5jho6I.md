# Ley Lines and Avebury Henge, the Better Version of Stonehenge

**[COLD OPEN: drone shot over the stones]**

Every year, about a million people visit Stonehenge. They park, queue, and look at the stones from behind a rope.

Meanwhile, twenty miles north, there's a stone circle so big it has a village inside it. You can walk right up to the stones. You can touch them. There's even a pub in the middle.

This is Avebury, and I think it beats Stonehenge.

**[TITLE CARD]**

**[SECTION 1: The scale]**

Let's start with size. Avebury is the largest stone circle in the world. Its outer ring is over 330 metres across and originally held around a hundred standing stones, some weighing more than 40 tons.

Around it runs the henge itself: a massive ditch and bank, dug roughly 4,500 years ago using antler picks. Inside the great circle sit two smaller circles, each of which would be a famous monument on its own.

And it doesn't stop at the bank. An avenue of paired stones leads away across the fields. Nearby stands Silbury Hill, the largest artificial prehistoric mound in Europe. Up the slope is West Kennet Long Barrow, a tomb older than the circle itself. Avebury is one piece of an entire ritual landscape.

**[SECTION 2: The missing stones]**

So why isn't it more famous? Partly because we broke it.

In the Middle Ages, villagers toppled and buried many stones, possibly because of their pagan associations. One man was apparently crushed in the process. His skeleton was found beneath a stone in 1938, along with scissors and coins, earning him the nickname "the barber-surgeon."

Later, in the 1700s, stones were smashed up for building material. Much of what you see today was re-erected in the 1930s by Alexander Keiller, heir to a marmalade fortune.

**[SECTION 3: Ley lines]**

Now, the ley lines.

In 1921, an amateur archaeologist named Alfred Watkins noticed that ancient sites seemed to fall along straight lines. He called them "leys" and suggested they were prehistoric trade routes.

By the 1960s, the idea had transformed. Ley lines became channels of mystical earth energy, and Avebury became a hotspot. The famous St Michael's Line supposedly runs from Cornwall through Glastonbury Tor and straight through Avebury.

Here's the honest answer: there's no scientific evidence for it. Britain is so crowded with ancient sites that you can draw a straight line through almost any handful of them by chance. Statisticians have tested this, and the alignments are no better than chance.

But I don't think that ruins anything. The people who built Avebury clearly did care about alignment, landscape, and connection. We just don't know exactly what it all meant.

**[OUTRO]**

And that's what makes Avebury better. Nobody rushes you, and nothing stands between you and the mystery. It's just you and the stones in a field, facing a question 4,500 years old.
