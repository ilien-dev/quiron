# F for Fake (1973) - How to Structure a Video Essay

**[COLD OPEN: Welles performing a coin trick for a child at a train station]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In 1973, Orson Welles made a film about an art forger, and it's also the best lesson I know in how to structure a video essay.

Most essays, mine included, fail the same way. They aren't wrong. They're shapeless: a point, then another point, then another, until the runtime ends.

**[TITLE CARD: F FOR FAKE]**

**[CLIP: Elmyr de Hory sketching a Matisse in seconds]**

Here's the material Welles started with. Another filmmaker, François Reichenbach, had shot documentary footage of Elmyr de Hory, a forger living on Ibiza whose fakes hung in real museums. Also in the footage is Elmyr's biographer, Clifford Irving.

Then reality handed Welles a gift. Irving was exposed for writing a fake autobiography of Howard Hughes. The man who wrote the book on a faker was a faker himself.

**[CLIP: Welles at the editing table, turning to camera]**

So Welles had borrowed footage, a news scandal, and his own history with hoaxes. Listed out, it's a mess. Edited, it flows. How?

**[GRAPHIC: "AND THEN" in plain text]**

**Lesson one: kill "and then."**

The creators of South Park have a rule for their scripts. If the words "and then" fit between your scenes, you have a list, not a story. Each beat should connect with "therefore" or "but."

**[GRAPHIC: "AND THEN" crossed out. "THEREFORE" and "BUT" appear]**

Watch how Welles does it. Elmyr is a brilliant painter, *but* nobody buys his original work. *Therefore* he forges. His forgeries fool the experts, *therefore* the experts look ridiculous. *But* if experts can't tell the difference, what is their expertise worth? *Therefore*, the real subject of the film is no longer forgery. It's authority.

Every scene is a consequence or a complication, and that gives you momentum.

**[CLIP: Rapid intercutting between Elmyr and Irving, each seeming to react to the other]**

**Lesson two: tell more than one story.**

Welles never stays on one thread. He has Elmyr, Irving, Hughes, and himself, and he cuts between them constantly.

And look at *when* he cuts. He doesn't leave a story when it's finished. He leaves when it's at its most interesting. A question gets raised, and before we get the answer, we're somewhere else.

**[GRAPHIC: Two parallel lines, cutting back and forth at their peaks]**

Think of it as "meanwhile, back at the ranch." When story A hits its height, jump to story B. When B peaks, jump back. The audience is always leaning forward, because there's always an unanswered question.

It does something else too. Put two stories side by side and they start commenting on each other. Welles cuts Elmyr and Irving together so they seem to argue, even though they were filmed separately. The meaning isn't in either shot. It's in the cut.

**[CLIP: Welles in cape and hat, addressing the camera]**

**Lesson three: make a promise.**

Early on, Welles looks at us and promises that for the next hour, everything we hear will be true.

That's a contract. It tells us what kind of film we're watching and gives us a reason to pay attention. A good essay does this in its first minute: here's the question, and here's why you should stay for the answer.

**[CLIP: Oja Kodar walking past Picasso's window]**

Of course, Welles is a magician. The film runs longer than an hour. After the promise expires, he tells an elaborate story about Oja Kodar and Picasso, and every word of it is invented. Then he confesses.

It doesn't feel like a cheat, because he kept his word to the letter. The setup paid off, just not the way we expected. Your ending should already be hiding in your opening.

**[CLIP: Chartres Cathedral at dusk]**

**Lesson four: stop.**

In the middle of all this fast cutting, Welles slows down. He stands before Chartres Cathedral, a masterpiece with no signature, and quietly asks whether a name matters at all.

It's the heart of the film, and it lands because of the contrast. After all that speed, stillness feels enormous. Give your audience room to think.

**[MONTAGE: Welles at the editing table, film running through his hands]**

So that's the structure. Connect ideas with "therefore" and "but." Run parallel stories and cut at the peaks. Make a promise and pay it off sideways. Leave a moment of quiet.

None of this was in the footage. Welles found it in the editing room, which is the most encouraging lesson of all. Structure isn't something your material has. It's something you build.

**[CLIP: Welles bows. Cut to black.]**
