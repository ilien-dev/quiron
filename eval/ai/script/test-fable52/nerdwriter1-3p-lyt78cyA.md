# The Problem With DC Action Scenes

**[COLD OPEN — black screen, sound of a punch landing]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Superman punches Zod through a building. Then another building. Then a satellite. Then another building.

And somewhere around the fourth building, something strange happens.

You stop caring.

**[TITLE CARD: The Problem With DC Action Scenes]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
I want to be clear up front: this isn't a "DC bad, other studio good" video. DC films contain some of the most striking images in the entire superhero genre. That's actually what makes this frustrating. The problem isn't a lack of talent, money, or ambition.

The problem is that so many of these action scenes are built to be *looked at* instead of *felt*.

Let's break that down.

## Part One: Scale Without Stakes

**[B-ROLL: wide shots of city destruction, collapsing skyscrapers]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Here's a rule of action filmmaking that sounds backwards: the bigger the destruction, the smaller the impact.

When one car flips in a grounded thriller, you flinch. When forty buildings collapse in the third act of a superhero movie, your brain files it under "weather."

The Metropolis battle in *Man of Steel* is the classic example. Technically, it's astonishing. But the fight has no geography. We don't know where the characters are in relation to anything. We don't know what Superman is trying to protect, or what Zod needs to reach. There's no objective beyond "hit the other guy harder."

And when two invulnerable people hit each other, nothing changes. Nobody gets tired. Nobody gets hurt. Nobody adapts. The fight at minute eight looks exactly like the fight at minute two.

A great action scene is a story with a beginning, a middle, and an end. Someone has a plan. The plan fails. They improvise. A lot of DC climaxes skip all that and go straight to *volume*.

## Part Two: The Gray CGI Void

**[B-ROLL: third-act finales — dark skies, red lightning, swirling debris]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Now picture the finale of *Batman v Superman*. Then *Justice League*. Then *Wonder Woman*. Then *Black Adam*. Then *The Flash*.

Notice anything?

They all happen in the same place. A dark, smoky, vaguely apocalyptic nowhere. An abandoned port. An abandoned town. An abandoned airfield. Always at night, always evacuated, always lit by fire and lightning.

There's a practical reason for this. Darkness and smoke hide unfinished visual effects. An empty location means no civilians to animate, and no awkward questions about collateral damage.

But it comes at an enormous cost. When the environment is a void, the characters can't *use* it. Think about your favorite fight scenes ever. They almost certainly involve the space: a staircase, a kitchen, a moving train. The location creates the problems and the solutions.

In the void, there are no problems. There's only punching, and a digital monster to punch.

Which brings us to...

## Part Three: The Weightless Body

**[B-ROLL: fully digital characters colliding mid-air]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
*Wonder Woman* is, for most of its runtime, a wonderful film. And the No Man's Land sequence is one of the best action scenes in the genre.

Why does it work? Because it's built on *character*. Diana is told no. She's told it's impossible. She climbs the ladder anyway. Every bullet she deflects is an argument she's winning. The action *is* the story.

Then the third act arrives, and she fights a CGI man in a CGI field while CGI debris swirls around them.

The moment an actor gets replaced with a digital double, something subtle breaks. Real bodies have weight. They have momentum. They take a half-second to recover. Digital doubles float. They accelerate too fast, stop too cleanly, and bounce off the ground like action figures thrown by a kid.

Your eyes may not consciously catch it. But your gut does. And once your gut decides nothing on screen is real, the tension is gone.

## Part Four: Style as a Substitute

**[B-ROLL: slow-motion hero shots, speed ramping]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Let's talk about slow motion.

Used sparingly, slow motion is punctuation. It says, "This moment matters."

But if *every* moment is slowed down, then no moment matters. You're not watching a fight anymore. You're watching a series of posters.

And that's the heart of it. A lot of DC action is designed like comic book splash pages — individual, gorgeous frames. But a splash page works in a comic because you control the pacing. *You* decide how long to stare at it.

Film doesn't work that way. Film is rhythm. Cause and effect. This hit leads to that fall, which leads to this desperate grab. When the choreography exists only to connect one cool pose to the next, the sequence has no pulse.

## Part Five: When DC Gets It Right

**[B-ROLL: grounded fights, practical stunts, rain-soaked car chase]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Here's the thing. DC *knows* how to do this.

The warehouse fight in *Batman v Superman* is brutal, clear, and inventive. Batman uses the room. He uses the enemies' weapons. He gets hit, and it costs him.

The car chase in *The Batman* is terrifying because the camera is bolted to the vehicles. You feel every skid. And the goal is simple: catch the Penguin.

The opening heist in *The Dark Knight*. The corridor escape in *The Suicide Squad*. Peacemaker's scrappy, ugly, hilarious brawls.

What do they share? Clear objectives. Physical consequences. Readable space. And characters who reveal who they are through *how* they fight.

None of those things require a smaller budget. They just require the action to be written, not merely rendered.

## Conclusion

**[B-ROLL: slow push-in on a lone hero silhouette against a skyline]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
So what's the problem with DC action scenes?

It's not that they're too dark, or too serious, or too big. It's that too often, spectacle gets treated as the destination instead of the vehicle.

These are the most iconic characters in fiction. Gods, detectives, warriors, outcasts. Their fights should tell us who they are, what they want, and what they're afraid to lose.

Because we don't remember the fourth building Superman got punched through.

We remember Diana climbing that ladder.
