## Number 9: Laser Internet From Deep Space

**[VISUAL: Psyche spacecraft, infrared laser beam animation]**

For more than sixty years, spacecraft have talked to Earth using radio waves. Radio is reliable, but it's slow. Think dial-up, stretched across the solar system.

So NASA bolted an experiment called Deep Space Optical Communications onto the Psyche spacecraft. Instead of radio, it uses an invisible infrared laser.

In December 2023, it beamed an ultra-high-definition video back to Earth from 19 million miles away, about 80 times farther than the Moon. The video starred a cat named Taters chasing a laser pointer. It arrived at 267 megabits per second, faster than many home broadband connections.

Later, the system sent data from as far as 290 million miles away.

Why does this matter to you? Laser links can carry 10 to 100 times more data than radio. That means live HD video from astronauts on Mars one day. The same optical technology is also spreading through the satellite networks that deliver internet here on Earth.

## Number 8: The Quiet Supersonic Jet

**[VISUAL: X-59 rollout, long needle nose, first flight footage]**

Since 1973, commercial supersonic flight over land has been banned in the United States. The reason is the sonic boom, which rattles windows and makes people on the ground very unhappy.

NASA's answer is the X-59.

Built with Lockheed Martin's Skunk Works, this aircraft is nearly 100 feet long, and about a third of that is nose. That stretched shape keeps shock waves from merging into one thunderous boom. What reaches the ground instead is a soft thump, roughly as loud as a car door closing down the street.

The X-59 made its first flight in October 2025. It's designed to cruise at Mach 1.4, about 925 miles per hour, at 55,000 feet.

The plan is to fly it over communities, measure how people react, and hand that data to regulators. If the rules change, airlines could one day fly you from New York to Los Angeles in about half the time.

## Number 7: Nuclear Power for the Moon

**[VISUAL: lunar surface at night, reactor concept art]**

A night on the Moon lasts about two weeks. Solar panels are useless that entire time, and temperatures plunge hundreds of degrees below zero.

If humans are going to live there, they need power that never switches off.

That's the idea behind Fission Surface Power, a small nuclear reactor designed to run on the Moon. NASA proved the basic concept in 2018 with an experiment called KRUSTY, a compact reactor that ran successfully in the Nevada desert.

The original goal was a 40-kilowatt system, enough to power about 30 homes continuously for ten years. In 2025, NASA raised the ambition and began pushing for a 100-kilowatt reactor ready to launch by around 2030.

Here's the Earth connection. A reactor that runs itself in the harshest environment imaginable shares a lot with the microreactors being developed for remote towns, disaster zones, and data centers. A reactor built for the Moon has to be safe, compact, and nearly maintenance-free, and those qualities are just as useful on the ground.

## Number 6: Making Oxygen on Mars

**[VISUAL: Perseverance rover, MOXIE gold box cutaway]**

Tucked inside the Perseverance rover is a gold box about the size of a car battery. It's called MOXIE, and it did something no machine had ever done.

It made breathable oxygen out of the air of another planet.

Mars' atmosphere is about 95 percent carbon dioxide. MOXIE pulled that gas in, heated it to roughly 1,400 degrees Fahrenheit, and split the oxygen atoms off.

Over sixteen runs, it produced 122 grams of oxygen. That's about what a small dog breathes in ten hours. At its peak it made 12 grams per hour, twice its original goal, at 98 percent purity or better.

That sounds tiny, but it proves the principle. A scaled-up version could produce tons of oxygen, both for astronauts to breathe and for rocket propellant to bring them home.

That's called living off the land. And the chemistry of pulling apart carbon dioxide is the kind of research that feeds into carbon conversion technology on Earth.

## Number 5: The Clock That Could Replace GPS in Space

**[VISUAL: Deep Space Atomic Clock, toaster-size comparison]**

Your phone knows where you are because of clocks. Every GPS satellite carries atomic clocks, and your position is calculated from tiny differences in signal timing.

In deep space there's no GPS. Spacecraft navigate by sending a signal to Earth, waiting for the response, and letting engineers on the ground do the math. Near Mars, that round trip can take up to 40 minutes.

NASA's Deep Space Atomic Clock was built to fix that.

Launched in 2019, this toaster-sized instrument uses mercury ions to keep time. Over its two-year mission, it proved to be up to 50 times more stable than the clocks on GPS satellites. It would lose about one second every 10 million years.

With a clock that good on board, a spacecraft can calculate its own position in real time, like a self-driving car for the solar system.

Ultra-stable clocks could also make future navigation satellites more precise, and that would improve the location services you use every day.

## Number 4: Sailing on Sunlight

**[VISUAL: solar sail unfurling in orbit, reflective shimmer]**

Imagine a spacecraft that never runs out of fuel because it never needed any.

Light has no mass, but it does carry momentum. When photons bounce off a reflective surface, they give it a tiny push. It's a small force, but in the vacuum of space it never stops, and it adds up.

In 2024, NASA launched the Advanced Composite Solar Sail System. It started as a satellite about the size of a microwave oven. Once in orbit, it unrolled four booms and stretched out a sail of roughly 860 square feet, about the size of a small apartment.

The booms were the real test. They're made from ultra-light carbon fiber composites that roll up like a tape measure, and the design could support sails many times larger.

Here's the payoff for you. Solar sails could park spacecraft in unusual positions to watch the Sun. That would give us earlier warning of solar storms, the kind that can knock out power grids, satellites, and communications across entire continents.

## Number 3: The 3D-Printed Superalloy

**[VISUAL: glowing metal 3D printer, rocket engine test fire]**

Rocket engines and jet turbines operate at temperatures that turn ordinary metal into taffy. The hotter they can run, the more efficient they become. The limit has always been the material.

Then NASA created GRX-810.

It's a 3D-printable metal alloy laced with nanoscale oxide particles, a design NASA arrived at with help from computer modeling. It can survive temperatures above 2,000 degrees Fahrenheit. Compared with the best printable alloys before it, GRX-810 is twice as strong and can last more than a thousand times longer under stress.

Because it's 3D-printed, engineers can build complex parts in one piece. Those parts once required hundreds of welded components.

NASA has already licensed the alloy to commercial companies.

For you, the benefit shows up in jet engines that burn less fuel, and that means cheaper and cleaner flights. Power plant turbines could waste less energy. And rockets get more affordable, which lowers the cost of everything we send to orbit, including the satellites behind your weather forecast.

## Number 2: Recycling 98 Percent of Water

**[VISUAL: ISS interior, astronaut drinking from a pouch]**

Every astronaut needs about a gallon of water per day for drinking, food, and hygiene. Launching all of it from Earth is extremely expensive. For a multi-year trip to Mars, it's simply impossible.

So the International Space Station recycles nearly everything.

That includes moisture from the cabin air, which means sweat and the humidity from every breath. And yes, it includes urine.

In 2023, NASA announced that the station's life support system had reached a 98 percent water recovery rate. A new brine processor made the difference by squeezing the last usable water out of leftover wastewater.

The result is cleaner than most of the water people drink on Earth. As astronauts like to say, yesterday's coffee becomes tomorrow's coffee.

This one already touches lives on the ground. Filtration technology developed for spaceflight has been adapted for disaster relief and for communities without access to clean water. As droughts intensify, closed-loop recycling may become something cities depend on too.

## Number 1: The Satellite That Watches Earth Move

**[VISUAL: NISAR in orbit, giant mesh antenna, colorful radar maps]**

Our number one technology isn't aimed at the Moon or Mars. It's aimed at you.

In July 2025, NASA and the Indian Space Research Organisation launched NISAR, one of the most advanced Earth-observing radar satellites ever built.

It carries two different radar systems and a mesh antenna nearly 40 feet across. Radar sees through clouds and works in darkness, so NISAR can scan nearly all of Earth's land and ice surfaces every 12 days, whatever the weather.

It can detect ground movement down to fractions of an inch.

That means it can spot the ground slowly deforming along earthquake faults, or a volcano swelling before an eruption. It can track land sinking as groundwater is pumped away. It also follows melting ice sheets, shifting forests, and changing farmland.

After floods, landslides, or earthquakes, it can help responders see exactly where the damage is.

The data is free and open to everyone. So it can inform decisions about where to build, how much water to use, and how to prepare for disasters, possibly in your own community.
