# Thinking Digital 2009: Tom Scott on graphs, Hans Zimmer, Eurovision and tea cosies

## SEGMENT 1: GRAPHS

**[VISUAL: Tom on stage. Behind him, a chart with a wobbling line.]**

**TOM:** Let's start with graphs, because the internet loves graphs. Traffic goes up, signups go up, and somebody in a meeting says "hockey stick" and everyone gets a little bit excited.

But a graph is an argument wearing a lab coat. It looks like pure fact, yet somebody chose what to put on it. Somebody chose where the axis starts. Somebody chose the time window. Chop the bottom off a bar chart and a two percent difference looks like a landslide.

**[VISUAL: The same data shown twice. One chart starts at zero and looks flat. The other is truncated and looks dramatic.]**

**TOM:** Same numbers. Completely different story. And the web makes this worse, because making a graph is now trivially easy. You can produce a beautiful, authoritative-looking chart in thirty seconds, and nobody checks it, because it's pretty.

So rule one: when you see a graph, ask what's been left out. Rule two: when you make one, be the person who puts it back.

---

## SEGMENT 2: HANS ZIMMER

**[VISUAL: Slide change. A film-score waveform. A dramatic orchestral sting.]**

**TOM:** Now, Hans Zimmer. Stay with me.

Zimmer is the composer behind The Lion King, Gladiator, Pirates of the Caribbean, The Dark Knight. You know his sound even if you don't know his name: big, slow-building, lots of strings, and a feeling that something enormous is about to happen.

What interests me is *how* that music gets made. He works with big teams, and he works with technology. Synthesisers, samples and software all sit alongside a real orchestra. Nobody walks out of the cinema saying, "Wow, what a sampler." They say, "I felt things."

**[VISUAL: Cutaway to a studio mixing desk, faders moving.]**

**TOM:** That's the point I want to make about digital tools in general. The best technology disappears. When it works, the audience doesn't see the software. They see the result.

If your website makes people admire your content management system, something has gone wrong. Be the score, not the sampler.

---

## SEGMENT 3: EUROVISION

**[VISUAL: Glitter. A scoreboard graphic covered in flags.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):** Which brings us, inevitably, to Eurovision.

**TOM:** This year Norway won, with Alexander Rybak, a violin, and a record-breaking score. Fantastic. But the real show is the voting.

And here's where graphs come back. Plot who votes for whom and the patterns jump out. Neighbours vote for neighbours. Countries with shared history swap points like trading cards. People call it politics. It's also just data, and a very clean example of how a crowd behaves when you ask it to rate things.

**[VISUAL: A network diagram of arrows between countries, thickening between neighbours.]**

**TOM:** Now think about every rating system on the web. Star ratings, upvotes, likes. They don't measure quality. They measure quality, plus who's voting, plus what those people already liked.

If you build anything with a voting button, congratulations: you've built a little Eurovision. Plan for the bloc voting.

**[VISUAL: Audience laughter. Tom grins.]**

**TOM:** And plan for the one country that gives you zero points and never lets you forget it.

---

## SEGMENT 4: TEA COSIES

**[VISUAL: A knitted tea cosy appears on the slide. The audience laughs.]**

**TOM:** And finally: tea cosies.

I know. You've been waiting for me to explain how this fits.

A tea cosy does one job. It keeps the pot warm. It's cheap, it's been around forever, and nobody has ever had to update its firmware. It has no login. It has no subscription tier. It has never asked for your email address.

**[BEAT.]**

**TOM:** In digital, we're obsessed with the new thing. We reach for the framework, the platform, the app. But a lot of the best solutions are boring, old, and brilliant at exactly one task.

So before you build something, ask yourself honestly: do I need an app, or do I need a tea cosy?

---

## SEGMENT 5: JOINING IT UP

**[VISUAL: Four icons on screen: a graph, a film score, a scoreboard, a tea cosy. Lines connect them one by one.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):** So what do these four things have in common?

**TOM:** More than you'd think. Graphs remind us that presentation is persuasion, so be honest about what you show. Zimmer reminds us that the technology should serve the experience, not star in it. Eurovision reminds us that crowds are messy, and any system that counts votes will be shaped by the people casting them. And the tea cosy reminds us that simple, well-made things last.

Put together: be honest, be invisible, expect people to behave like people, and don't overcomplicate it.

**[VISUAL: The opening graph returns. This time the axes fill in with labels, and the line wobbles realistically.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):** The graph we started with? It turns out it was measuring tea consumption per head in the north of England. It goes up. Nobody is surprised.

**[VISUAL: Audience laughs. Tom takes a bow.]**
