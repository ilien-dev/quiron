# The Fake-British Ghost Town In China: Thames Town

**[COLD OPEN - 0:00]**

*(Visual: drone shot of red brick rooftops, a church spire, and a red phone box under a grey sky)*

NARRATOR:
This looks like an English market town. Cobbled streets. Tudor-style pubs. A red telephone box on the corner. A church with a tall stone spire.

But you're not in England. You're about 30 kilometers outside Shanghai, and this entire town was built from scratch, to look like somewhere it isn't.

This is Thames Town. And for years, almost nobody lived here.

**[TITLE CARD - 0:30]**

**[SECTION 1: THE BIG IDEA - 0:35]**

*(Visual: map of Shanghai, zooming into Songjiang district)*

NARRATOR:
To understand Thames Town, you have to go back to the year 2001.

Shanghai was growing at a speed that's hard to imagine. The city was packed, and the government wanted to move people out into new satellite towns. So it launched an ambitious plan called "One City, Nine Towns."

The idea was simple, and a little strange. Each new town would have its own foreign theme. There would be an Italian town, a Dutch town, a Scandinavian town, a German town, a Spanish-style town, and more.

And one of them would be British.

**[SECTION 2: BUILDING BRITAIN - 1:30]**

*(Visual: architectural renderings, construction footage, photos of the finished streets)*

NARRATOR:
Thames Town sits in Songjiang, a district southwest of central Shanghai. The master plan was drawn up by Atkins, a British design firm, and construction wrapped up around 2006.

The goal was to house around 10,000 people in a place that felt like a quaint English town. And the designers committed to the bit.

There's a market street with half-timbered buildings. There are brick terraces, pubs, and shopfronts. There's a town square with statues, and a church that looks like it was lifted out of an English city, complete with a bell tower and a cobbled plaza out front.

There was even a stretch of water meant to evoke the River Thames, which is where the town gets its name.

It wasn't a theme park. It was supposed to be a real place, with real homes, real shops, and real neighbors.

**[SECTION 3: THE EMPTY STREETS - 3:00]**

*(Visual: empty plazas, shuttered shopfronts, a lone cyclist)*

NARRATOR:
But when the town opened, something was missing. People.

The homes were expensive for what they were. They were far from the city center, and the commute into Shanghai was long. Many of the apartments were bought as investments, not as places to live. In China's property boom, that was common. Buy now, hold, and wait for prices to rise.

So the lights stayed off. Shops stayed empty. Journalists and photographers arrived and started calling it a ghost town. Pictures of the deserted pubs and silent squares spread around the world.

And Thames Town became a symbol, not of British charm, but of overbuilding.

**[SECTION 4: THE WEDDING CAPITAL - 4:30]**

*(Visual: brides and grooms posing in front of the church, photographers with reflectors)*

NARRATOR:
Then the town found an unexpected purpose.

If you walk through Thames Town on a weekend, you'll often see something that has nothing to do with property markets: wedding photography.

Couples travel here for their wedding photos, sometimes dozens at a time, each with their own photographer, stylist, and assistant. The church steps, the cobbled lanes, and the red brick facades make a backdrop that's hard to find anywhere else nearby.

For a lot of couples, it's far cheaper than flying to Europe. You get the look of an English village, without the visa and the plane ticket.

So the "fake" town found a real economy. A town built to be lived in became a town built to be photographed.

**[SECTION 5: IS IT REALLY FAKE? - 6:00]**

*(Visual: close-ups of details, a plaque, a pub sign, a statue)*

NARRATOR:
Here's where it gets interesting. Is Thames Town really a copy?

Visit in person and you notice that it's more like an interpretation. The details are close, but not exact. The proportions are a little off. The streets are wider and cleaner than any real English town. It looks like Britain as imagined from a distance, a greatest-hits version of brick, timber, and spires.

And that's the point. Thames Town isn't trying to recreate a specific place. It's recreating an idea of England that exists in postcards, films, and books.

In a way, it says as much about how China sees the world as it does about Britain itself.

**[SECTION 6: THE BIGGER PICTURE - 7:15]**

*(Visual: images of the other themed towns: Dutch windmills, Italian piazzas)*

NARRATOR:
Thames Town wasn't alone. Other themed developments around Shanghai faced similar problems. Several were far from the center, hard to commute from, and sold more as investments than homes.

Some of them struggled for years. Others slowly filled up as the city grew outward and transport links improved.

And that's a pattern worth noticing. A "ghost town" isn't always a failure. Sometimes it's just early. Cities grow into the places built for them, and a town that looks empty one year can look different a decade later.

Thames Town has seen some of that change, with residents, businesses, and visitors trickling in over time, though it's never become the bustling English market town its designers imagined.

**[SECTION 7: WHY IT STILL FASCINATES US - 8:15]**

*(Visual: sunset over the town, warm light on the brick)*

NARRATOR:
So why do we keep talking about Thames Town?

Maybe because it feels like a dream that got a little lost. A whole town, designed with care, waiting for a crowd that took its time arriving.

Maybe because it asks a strange question: what makes a place feel real? Is it the age of the bricks? The people who live there? The history behind it?

Thames Town has none of the first and, for a long time, little of the second. But today, there are couples making memories on those steps, kids riding bikes on those streets, and people calling it home.

Maybe that's how every place becomes real. Not by being old, but by being used.

**[OUTRO - 9:15]**

*(Visual: slow pullback from the town, ending on the church spire)*

NARRATOR:
Thames Town is one of the most surreal places in modern China. Part experiment, part cautionary tale, part film set.
