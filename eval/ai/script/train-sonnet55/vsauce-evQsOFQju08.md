# Is Your Red The Same as My Red?

**Target length: ~1,290 words | Runtime: ~8-9 minutes**

---

## COLD OPEN

**[VISUAL: Close-up of a ripe tomato on a white background]**

Here's a question that sounds like something you'd ask at 2 a.m. on a dorm room floor.

When you look at this tomato and say "that's red," and I look at the same tomato and say "that's red," are we actually seeing the same thing?

Not the same tomato. The same *experience*.

Because here's the unsettling part: there is no way for me to check. I can't climb inside your head. I can't borrow your eyes for ten seconds. All I have is the word "red," and we both learned to attach it to the same objects.

Today we're going to dig into one of the oldest puzzles in philosophy, and see what modern science can, and can't, tell us about it.

## PART 1: THE PUZZLE

**[VISUAL: Animated illustration of two heads, each with a colored swirl inside]**

Philosophers call this the problem of the *inverted spectrum*. It's tied to a bigger idea called **qualia**: the raw, private feel of an experience. The redness of red. The sweetness of sugar. The sting of a paper cut.

The philosopher John Locke floated this idea back in 1690. Imagine, he said, that the sensation your mind produces when you look at a violet is the same one mine produces when I look at a marigold. And vice versa.

Would we ever find out?

Think about it. You'd call violets "purple." So would I. We'd both stop at the same red light. We'd both agree that red and orange feel warm, and blue feels cool. Every test we could possibly run would come out identical, because we both learned our color words from the same shared world.

The inside could be swapped, and the outside would never show it.

That's what makes the question so slippery. It isn't just that we *haven't* checked. It's that it's hard to imagine what checking would even look like.

---

## PART 2: WHAT'S ACTUALLY HAPPENING IN YOUR EYE

**[VISUAL: Diagram of the eye, zooming into the retina]**

So let's get practical. What does science say?

First, a surprising fact: **color doesn't exist out in the world.** Not the way we experience it.

What exists is light, electromagnetic radiation at different wavelengths. Around 700 nanometers, that's the long end of the visible range. Around 450, that's the short end. But a wavelength isn't "red." A wavelength is just a number. Redness is something your brain *does* with that number.

Here's how. At the back of your eye you have three types of cone cells, each tuned to a different range of wavelengths: long, medium, and short. Light comes in, and each cone type responds by a different amount. Your brain compares those three signals, and out comes a color.

Red isn't a wavelength. Red is the brain's *interpretation* of a particular pattern across three channels.

And that gives us our first real clue. Because those cones aren't identical from person to person.

---

## PART 3: WE'RE NOT ALL WIRED THE SAME

**[VISUAL: Ishihara color-blindness test plate]**

About 1 in 12 men, and 1 in 200 women, have some form of color vision deficiency. For many of them, the "red" and "green" cones overlap so much that certain reds and greens become nearly indistinguishable.

So already, we know that two people can look at the same object and get *measurably* different information. Your red and a color-blind person's red are not the same signal, whatever they feel like.

But it goes the other way too.

Some people, mostly women, carry a genetic variant that gives them a **fourth** type of cone. Researchers call them tetrachromats. In theory, they can distinguish colors the rest of us would lump together. Their world may literally have more shades in it.

And even among people with "normal" vision, there's variation. Studies on what shade people call "pure green," the point where it looks neither yellowish nor bluish, show a surprisingly wide spread between individuals.

**[VISUAL: The famous "dress" photo, blue/black vs. white/gold]**

And then there was 2015, when the internet nearly tore itself apart over one dress. Some people saw blue and black. Others saw white and gold. Same photo, same pixels. Their brains were making different guesses about the lighting, and delivering different colors.

So, is your red the same as my red? At the level of biology, probably *close*, but almost certainly not identical.

---

## PART 4: THE WORDS WE USE

**[VISUAL: A color wheel, with labels changing across languages]**

Now let's add language, because language shapes this more than you'd think.

Russian has two separate basic words for blue: *goluboy* for light blue and *siniy* for dark blue. Experiments suggest Russian speakers are slightly faster at telling apart shades that fall on either side of that line. English speakers don't show the same boost.

The Himba people of Namibia have a different way of carving up the color space. In one well-known study, Himba participants had difficulty picking out a blue square among green ones, since their language groups those together. But they were *better* than English speakers at spotting subtle differences between shades of green that we would call identical.

Does that mean they *experience* color differently? Or just that they pay attention differently? Honestly, researchers still argue about it.

But it shows that "red" isn't just a raw signal. It's a signal wrapped in attention, memory, and culture.

---

## PART 5: THE HARD PART

**[VISUAL: A black-and-white room with a figure, Mary, looking at a screen]**

Still, none of that touches the deepest version of the question. Even if we mapped every cone and every neuron, would we know what red is *like*?

In 1982, the philosopher Frank Jackson proposed a thought experiment. Meet Mary. Mary is a brilliant scientist who has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room. She has learned everything there is to know about the physics and neuroscience of color vision. Every wavelength. Every neural pathway.

One day, she walks out and sees a red rose.

Does she learn something new?

If you think yes, that there's something about *seeing* red that all the facts left out, then you're sensing what philosopher David Chalmers calls **the hard problem of consciousness**. We can explain how the brain sorts wavelengths. We can't yet explain why any of it *feels* like anything.

Others disagree. Some philosophers argue Mary learns nothing truly new, just a new way of knowing the same facts. Others say the question dissolves once we understand the brain well enough.

Nobody has settled it.

---

## PART 6: SO, WHAT'S THE ANSWER?

**[VISUAL: Return to the tomato]**

Let's pull it together.

Is your red the same as my red? Here's the honest breakdown.

**The inputs** are mostly the same. We share the same physics and, mostly, the same kind of eyes. But there are real, measurable differences between us.

**The labels** are shared, because we learned them from the same people pointing at the same things.

**The inner feel**, the qualia itself, is the part we can't measure. It might be identical. It might be different. We don't have a tool that can tell.

But here's something I find oddly comforting. Even if our inner experiences differ, they're *structured* the same way. Red feels more like orange than like blue. Red feels warm, intense, urgent. Those relationships seem to hold across people. Which suggests that whatever is happening inside our heads, the *shape* of it is shared.

Maybe that's enough. Maybe the fact that we can look at a sunset together and both say "wow" is the real connection, whatever's underneath.

So the next time someone hands you a red apple, take a moment. Somewhere behind your eyes, three kinds of cells are firing, a brain is making a call, and something *shows up* in your experience that no one else can ever directly see.

And that's remarkable. Not because we've solved it, but because every one of us is carrying around a private universe, and we've somehow still figured out how to talk to each other about it.
