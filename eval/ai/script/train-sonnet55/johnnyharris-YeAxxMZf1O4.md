# How Switzerland Stayed Neutral

**Runtime: ~16 minutes | Target length: ~2,460 words**

## COLD OPEN

**[VISUAL: Aerial shot of the Swiss Alps. Slow push toward a mountainside that looks like ordinary rock.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In the summer of 1940, a Swiss general gathered his senior officers in a meadow above a lake. France had just fallen. Germany stood on three of Switzerland's four borders, and Italy, Hitler's ally, held the fourth.

Switzerland was a small country of four million people, surrounded, with no allies to call on.

And yet for the next five years, no German tank crossed the border to occupy it.

**[VISUAL: Archival footage of European troops in 1940. Cut to a quiet Swiss village.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
How did that happen? The popular answer is that the Swiss are just neutral, as if it were a personality trait. But Swiss neutrality is not a trait. It is a strategy, it was built over five centuries, and it has been tested, bent, and sometimes badly compromised along the way.

This is how Switzerland stayed neutral.

## PART ONE: THE MERCENARY NATION

**[VISUAL: Medieval map of the Swiss Confederation. Illustrations of pikemen in square formations.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Let's start with a surprise. Switzerland was not always peaceful. For a time, it was one of the most feared military powers in Europe.

The country traces its origins to 1291, when three alpine communities pledged to defend one another against outside rulers. Over the next two centuries, that small alliance grew into the Swiss Confederation. Swiss infantry, armed with long pikes and fighting in tight, disciplined formations, defeated armies of knights that should have crushed them. They beat the Austrians, then the Burgundians, and their reputation spread across the continent.

Kings noticed. Soon the Swiss were Europe's most sought-after soldiers, hired by France, by the Pope, and by anyone who could pay. To this day, the Pope's Swiss Guard is a surviving trace of that era.

But the Swiss weren't only hired out. They were also expanding, pushing south into northern Italy.

**[VISUAL: Painting of the Battle of Marignano, 1515.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
That ended in September 1515 at the Battle of Marignano, near Milan. Facing a French army with modern artillery and cavalry, the Swiss fought for two days and lost thousands of men. It was a brutal lesson. The pike square could not beat cannon.

After Marignano, the Confederation stepped back from foreign conquest. Historians still argue about how deliberate this was. It was probably less a noble decision than a practical one, since the Confederation was also divided at home. But it set a pattern, and the pattern is important: Switzerland learned, early and painfully, that it could not win a war of expansion, and that its best asset was a defensible home territory.

Then, a century later, came the Reformation, which cut the Confederation in two.

---

## PART TWO: HOLDING TOGETHER

**[VISUAL: Split map: Catholic and Protestant cantons in different colors.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
This is a part of the story people skip, but it matters. Neutrality abroad depended on peace at home.

The Reformation split the Swiss cantons. Some turned Protestant, others stayed Catholic, and there were real wars between them. Yet while the rest of Europe tore itself apart in the Thirty Years' War, which killed millions, the Swiss largely stayed out of it. Why? Because the cantons understood that if they chose sides abroad, their own alliance would shatter.

That's the core logic of Swiss neutrality. It isn't mainly about other countries. It's about holding together a country that is divided by language, religion, and geography. Today Switzerland has four national languages: German, French, Italian, and Romansh. If it picked a side in a foreign war, the German speakers might sympathize with one camp and the French speakers with the other. Neutrality kept the peace inside the house.

In 1648, the Peace of Westphalia ended the Thirty Years' War and formally recognized Switzerland's independence from the Holy Roman Empire. Neutrality wasn't yet law, but it was becoming habit.

---

## PART THREE: NAPOLEON AND THE GREAT POWERS' BARGAIN

**[VISUAL: Napoleonic troops marching into Switzerland, 1798. Paintings of the Helvetic Republic.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Then came the test that nearly ended everything.

In 1798, French revolutionary armies invaded and swept aside the old Confederation. They imposed the Helvetic Republic, a centralized state modeled on France. It was unpopular, chaotic, and a puppet of Paris. Switzerland became a battlefield, with French, Austrian, and Russian armies fighting across its valleys.

Napoleon eventually restored a looser federal structure in 1803, but Switzerland remained under French influence until he fell.

And here is the moment that changed everything. In 1815, at the Congress of Vienna, the great powers of Europe were redrawing the map after Napoleon. They asked themselves a practical question: what should we do with Switzerland?

The answer was surprisingly clever. Switzerland sits atop the Alpine passes, the routes between Germany, France, and Italy. Whoever controlled those passes held an advantage. No power wanted a rival to have them. So the powers agreed that it was better for everyone if no one had them.

**[VISUAL: Document: the 1815 declaration recognizing Swiss neutrality.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In November 1815, Austria, France, Britain, Russia, Prussia, and others formally recognized the "perpetual neutrality" of Switzerland and guaranteed the inviolability of its territory.

Notice what this was. It was not a gift. It was a bargain rooted in self-interest. Switzerland was more useful to Europe as a buffer than as a prize. Swiss neutrality survived because the great powers needed it to.

---

## PART FOUR: BUILDING A STATE WORTH LEAVING ALONE

**[VISUAL: 1840s lithographs. The Sonderbund War. Then the 1848 constitution.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
But a guarantee from other countries is only as good as your ability to defend it. Switzerland still had to prove it was stable.

In 1847, the country fought a short civil war, the Sonderbund War, between liberal, mostly Protestant cantons and a league of conservative Catholic ones. It lasted under a month and killed fewer than a hundred soldiers. By the standards of civil wars, it was almost gentle.

The winners did something wise. They didn't crush the losers. In 1848, they wrote a federal constitution that balanced central authority with strong cantonal powers, borrowing ideas from the United States. That structure, including its tradition of referendums and local autonomy, is still the backbone of Swiss politics.

The result was a country in which no region could easily be dominated by another. That internal balance is a quiet reason neutrality held. A divided country invites foreign meddling, and a cohesive one resists it.

Then, in the second half of the 1800s, Switzerland found a new way to make itself valuable.

**[VISUAL: Henry Dunant, the first Red Cross emblem, the Geneva Convention.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In 1859, a Swiss businessman named Henry Dunant witnessed the aftermath of the Battle of Solferino in Italy, where thousands of wounded soldiers lay without care. He was haunted by it. His campaign led, in 1863, to the founding of the International Committee of the Red Cross in Geneva, and in 1864 to the first Geneva Convention.

Think about what that meant. A neutral country could do what a belligerent never could: act as a trusted go-between. Switzerland became a place where enemies could send messages, exchange prisoners, and negotiate. Its neutrality turned into a service that the world wanted to keep running.

In the Franco-Prussian War of 1870 to 1871, a defeated French army of nearly 90,000 men crossed into Switzerland to escape capture. The Swiss disarmed, fed, and sheltered them. It was a demonstration of what neutrality could look like in practice.

---

## PART FIVE: THE FIRST WORLD WAR

**[VISUAL: Swiss border guards in 1914. Trenches ending at the Swiss frontier.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
When war broke out in 1914, the Swiss mobilized their army, a militia drawn from ordinary citizens, and declared neutrality. The Western Front's trenches ran from the English Channel almost to the Swiss border and then stopped. Both sides could see Swiss territory, and neither crossed it.

Neutrality wasn't easy, though. The country's German and French speakers leaned toward different sides, and the tension was real. Swiss officers were caught passing information, and a political scandal in 1917 involving a Swiss politician and secret peace feelers to Germany nearly cost the government its credibility.

The economy suffered as well. Switzerland has few natural resources and depends on imports, and both sides used blockades to pressure it. Hardship led to unrest, and in 1918 a general strike brought the country close to crisis.

Switzerland came through, and it kept its neutrality. But the war taught a hard lesson: neutrality demands constant work, and it is not a place you can simply stand still.

---

## PART SIX: THE HEDGEHOG

**[VISUAL: Europe in 1940. Axis map showing Switzerland surrounded.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Now we come back to that meadow in 1940.

By mid-1940, Switzerland was an island in an Axis sea. German planners drew up invasion studies. And the Swiss knew it.

Their commander was General Henri Guisan, a tall, formal officer who understood symbols as well as strategy. On July 25, 1940, he assembled his senior officers at the Rütli meadow, the legendary birthplace of the Confederation, and announced a new defensive plan.

**[VISUAL: Guisan at the Rütli. Maps of the National Redoubt.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
It was called the Réduit, or National Redoubt. The idea was this: Switzerland could not hold its flat, populated lowlands against the German army. So the army would withdraw into the Alps, into a fortress of tunnels, bunkers, and prepared demolitions. The bridges, tunnels, and rail lines through the Alpine passes were wired to be destroyed.

The calculation was simple and cruel. If Germany invaded, it could take the cities and the factories, but it would lose the very Alpine transit routes it wanted, and it would face a long, costly fight in terrain that favored the defender.

This is deterrence. The goal wasn't to beat Germany. It was to make invasion more expensive than it was worth.

The Swiss backed this up with real numbers. They mobilized hundreds of thousands of soldiers, a huge share of the male population. Swiss pilots shot down German aircraft that violated their airspace in 1940, and they fought some Allied planes as well. The message was consistent: we will defend the border against anyone.

---

## PART SEVEN: THE UNCOMFORTABLE TRUTH

**[VISUAL: Swiss bank vaults. Gold bars. Then archival photos of refugees at a border post.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Now, a good script has to be honest, and the honest version of this story is not entirely flattering.

Swiss deterrence worked partly because of the army. But it also worked because Switzerland made itself useful to Nazi Germany.

Germany needed a place to convert looted and seized gold into hard currency to buy war materials, and Swiss banks and the Swiss National Bank provided that. Swiss factories exported arms, machinery, and precision parts to the Axis, and also to the Allies. Rail lines through the Alps carried German and Italian freight. Switzerland also extended credit to Germany. Here is the uncomfortable math: a country that is profitable to the aggressor is a country the aggressor is less eager to invade.

There was also a human cost. Switzerland sheltered tens of thousands of refugees, but it also turned many away at the border, including Jewish refugees. In 1942 the government tightened its rules, and officials used the phrase "the boat is full." Some of those turned back were later murdered.

After the war, Switzerland spent decades avoiding that history. In the 1990s, pressure over dormant Jewish bank accounts and wartime gold led to an independent historical commission, which published its findings in 2002. It documented both the real defensive achievement and the moral failures. Swiss banks eventually agreed to a settlement of over a billion dollars.

So the full answer to our question is more complicated than "the Swiss were brave." They were armed, they were lucky, and they were useful. And at times they were complicit.

---

## PART EIGHT: THE COLD WAR AND ARMED NEUTRALITY

**[VISUAL: Cold War era. Swiss soldiers with rifles at home. Concrete bunkers disguised as barns.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
After 1945, Switzerland did not relax. It doubled down.

The doctrine became known as armed neutrality. The country kept a militia army where most men served and kept their rifles at home. It built a vast system of civil defense. By law, residents had to have access to a nuclear fallout shelter, and the country built enough space to shelter its entire population. Bridges and tunnels were designed with demolition chambers. Even some hillsides concealed aircraft hangars and artillery.

At the same time, Switzerland stayed outside the alliances that defined the Cold War. It didn't join NATO. It didn't join the Warsaw Pact. It did not join the United Nations until 2002, and then only after a close referendum. It has never joined the European Union, though it trades with it extensively through bilateral treaties.

Neutrality also let it do what it had done since Dunant. Geneva became a hub for diplomacy, arms control talks, and international organizations. Swiss diplomats represented the interests of countries that had no formal relations with each other, acting as protecting power for the United States in Cuba and Iran, for instance.

Neutrality had become Switzerland's brand, and its business model.

---

## PART NINE: NEUTRALITY UNDER PRESSURE

**[VISUAL: 2022 news footage. Ukraine war. Swiss flag beside the EU flag.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Which brings us to today, because neutrality is once again being tested.

When Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, Switzerland did something that surprised many people. It adopted European Union sanctions against Russia, freezing assets and restricting trade. Critics said that was a break with neutrality. The government said that neutrality in law means not joining military conflicts, and that economic sanctions in response to a clear violation of international law are compatible with it.

But the harder issue was weapons. Swiss law bans the re-export of Swiss-made arms to countries at war. So when allies like Germany and Denmark asked to send Swiss-origin ammunition to Ukraine, Switzerland refused. That frustrated many European partners and set off a fierce debate at home. Some Swiss argue that the rule is a principled safeguard. Others argue that it makes Switzerland look like a free rider on European security.

There is a second pressure too. Switzerland has quietly deepened its cooperation with NATO through the Partnership for Peace program and joint exercises, and has discussed closer defense ties. Defenders say that's pragmatic. Critics say that neutrality is being hollowed out.

So the Swiss are asking themselves a question again, with real stakes: is neutrality a fixed principle, or a tool that has to adapt?

---

## PART TEN: WHAT THE STORY ACTUALLY TEACHES

**[VISUAL: Return to the Alpine landscape from the open. Slow pull-back.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Let's pull the threads together. How did Switzerland stay neutral?

**First, geography.** The Alps gave it a natural fortress, and the mountain passes gave it strategic value. Defending mountains is far easier than conquering them.

**Second, a lesson learned early.** After Marignano, Switzerland understood its limits. It stopped trying to be a power and focused on being un-conquerable.

**Third, great-power interest.** In 1815, Europe's powers decided that a neutral Switzerland was better than a Switzerland owned by any of them. Neutrality survived because it served others.

**Fourth, internal cohesion.** A federal system, with referendums and strong local government, let a country of four languages and two religions hold together. Foreign powers couldn't easily split it.

**Fifth, credible defense.** Neutrality without an army is just a request. Switzerland made invasion expensive, through militia, fortifications, and a willingness to destroy its own infrastructure.

**Sixth, usefulness.** Switzerland offered banking, diplomacy, humanitarian work, and trade routes. That made it valuable to every side, and sometimes that value had a dark side.

**[VISUAL: Montage: Red Cross flag, Geneva skyline, a bunker door closing, a Swiss voting booth.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
So Swiss neutrality was never one thing. It was geography, politics, deterrence, and commerce, all working together, helped along by some luck, and stained by some real moral failures.

And it was never passive. Neutrality is a decision that has to be made again with every crisis. Sometimes the Swiss made it well, and sometimes they didn't.

The country that stood quietly in the middle of Europe's wars wasn't standing still. It was working, constantly, to stay out of them.

Whether that can continue in a world of cyberattacks, economic warfare, and fragile alliances is the question the Swiss are debating right now. And the answer will say a lot about whether neutrality is a relic of the past, or a strategy that still has a future.
