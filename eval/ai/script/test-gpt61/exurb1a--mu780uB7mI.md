# Sleep is Just Death Being Shy

Every night, you willingly lie down, close your eyes, and trust that the universe will still be here when you get back.

That is an insane thing to do.

You would never walk into a restaurant, hand a stranger your belongings, and say, “I’m going to become completely unresponsive for eight hours. Please make sure nothing weird happens.”

But put a mattress under that situation, and suddenly it’s self-care.

We even buy special clothes for it.

Little outfits for disappearing.

And the strangest part isn’t that we do this every night.

It’s that we get annoyed when we can’t.

You lie there at two in the morning thinking, “Come on. Stop being conscious. I have things to do tomorrow.”

Which is why I’ve developed a theory.

Sleep is just death being shy.

***

Now, before someone in the comments starts typing with the intensity of a courtroom stenographer: yes, sleep and death are different.

One involves waking up and checking your phone.

The other, presumably, has better boundaries.

I’m talking about the resemblance. The atmosphere. The suspicious branding.

Sleep feels like death showed up to the party but didn’t want to make a whole thing of it.

“Hey, I’m just going to take your awareness for a little while.”

“For how long?”

“Oh, nothing serious. Until seven.”

“And what happens during that time?”

“Honestly? You might be a dentist in a shopping mall that’s also your childhood home.”

“Okay. Seems reasonable.”

Then we turn off the light.

***

Think about your bedtime routine.

You lock the doors. You close the curtains. You put your possessions in familiar places.

You arrange the room so everything will be ready for the person who finds you in the morning.

That person is usually you.

But still.

There’s something quietly ceremonial about it.

We lower the lights. We ask for silence. We stretch out beneath a sheet.

And then we choose the position in which we would like to stop participating.

Some people fold their hands across their chest, which feels a little too committed to the theme.

Others sleep facedown, like they’ve been eliminated from a detective show.

Personally, I rotate until the bed accepts me.

***

But you never actually catch yourself falling asleep.

You can watch yourself get tired. You can feel your thoughts become slippery.

One minute you’re thinking about tomorrow’s errands.

Then you’re wondering whether a horse would understand a revolving door.

Then the horse is your manager.

And then—

Morning.

There is no little notification that says, “You are now unconscious.”

No loading bar.

No polite announcement asking you to save your work.

You simply stop being there to notice.

Trying to observe the exact moment you fall asleep is like trying to sneak up on your own absence.

The observer leaves with the thing being observed.

Very inconvenient design.

***

And when you wake up, you accept the gap immediately.

You don’t sit upright screaming, “WHERE DID THE NIGHT GO?”

You say, “Already?”

Eight hours can vanish without so much as a receipt, and your main complaint is that you wanted more.

Imagine that happening during lunch.

You take a bite of a sandwich, blink, and suddenly it’s evening.

You would demand an investigation.

At night, you blame the alarm.

The alarm is the only device we purchase specifically to interrupt something we enjoy, then hate for doing its job.

Every morning it says, “Good news. You’re alive.”

And we say, “Give me nine minutes.”

***

That’s what makes sleep such a strange rehearsal.

Not a rehearsal for being dead, exactly.

A rehearsal for the world continuing without your supervision.

For a few hours, you surrender the extremely demanding position of being yourself.

You stop managing your expression.

You stop explaining what you meant in that text.

You stop trying to determine whether everyone at dinner noticed the weird thing you did with your hands.

The world keeps turning.

People board planes. Rain falls on empty parking lots. Somewhere, a printer refuses to work.

And none of it requires your input.

Which should be comforting.

Somehow, it’s also a little insulting.

***

During the day, consciousness acts like an anxious middle manager.

It wants updates.

It needs to know why that person used a period instead of an exclamation point.

It has scheduled an emergency meeting about a conversation from 2014.

Then bedtime arrives, and you ask this creature to clock out.

Naturally, it refuses.

“Oh, we’re relaxing? Great. I’ve prepared a presentation.”

The presentation is titled:

**EVERYTHING THAT COULD GO WRONG, INCLUDING SEVERAL THINGS THAT ALREADY DID.**

There are no slides.

Only feelings.

And for some reason, a song you haven’t heard since elementary school.

Your brain can’t remember why you entered the kitchen, but it has preserved a carpet commercial with archival precision.

***

Maybe that’s why drifting off can feel so merciful.

You aren’t necessarily desperate to stop existing.

You’re desperate to stop narrating.

There’s a difference between being alive and having to provide live commentary on the experience.

Sometimes the exhausting part of the day isn’t what happened.

It’s the second shift afterward.

Was I awkward?

Did I waste my time?

Am I falling behind?

What does “behind” even mean?

Who established the schedule?

Why does everyone else appear to have received an email I missed?

Sleep interrupts the panel discussion.

Nobody wins the argument.

The microphones simply turn off.

And for once, that is enough.

***

Except, of course, your brain sometimes keeps producing content.

Dreams are what happens when the person responsible for quality control goes home.

You’re back in school.

There’s an exam.

You haven’t attended class all semester.

Also, you are forty-three.

Also, the teacher is a refrigerator.

None of this strikes you as unusual.

But if the refrigerator says the exam starts at eleven instead of ten, now you have questions.

Dream logic is incredibly generous about reality and weirdly strict about logistics.

You can breathe underwater, but you’re worried about parking.

You can visit another planet, but you still have to apologize for being late.

Apparently even imagination has administrative overhead.

***

And dreams make the whole death comparison less dignified.

Because if sleep is death being shy, dreaming is death trying to make conversation.

“So, uh… what if your teeth fell out?”

“I’d rather not.”

“Okay. What if you could fly, but only badly?”

“Better.”

“Your ex is here.”

“Why?”

“They own the airport.”

There is something almost touching about it.

The lights go down, the sensible world disappears, and your mind starts building little theaters out of whatever it has lying around.

A staircase.

An old friend.

A fear wearing somebody else’s face.

Most of the performance is forgotten before breakfast.

Still, there was a performance.

***

Then there’s the vulnerability of the whole arrangement.

Sleep requires trust, even when we don’t call it that.

Trust in the room.

Trust in the lock.

Trust in whoever is beside you.

Falling asleep near someone is a quiet admission: I believe I can stop paying attention here.

That’s a bigger compliment than most things we say out loud.

Flowers are nice.

But “I’m willing to become useless in your presence” is intimate.

It’s why a sleeping dog leaning against your leg can feel like an honor.

This little creature has assessed the situation.

Apparently you are safe enough to go offline beside.

Try earning a better review than that.

***

Of course, humans have complicated this beautiful act by inventing bedtime.

As children, we resist it like an unjust sentence.

“I’m not tired.”

You are visibly dissolving.

Your eyes are closing independently.

You’ve just cried because a banana broke.

But you’re convinced the adults are sending you away because something incredible happens after nine.

Then you become an adult and discover what happens after nine.

Dishes.

Looking for a charger.

Standing in the kitchen eating something directly from its container.

Childhood is fighting sleep because you’re afraid of missing life.

Adulthood is trying to finish life’s paperwork so you can finally go to bed.

***

And yet we keep postponing it.

One more episode.

One more scroll.

One more completely unnecessary investigation into what happened to an actor from a movie we barely remember.

Sometimes staying awake feels like keeping a little piece of the day for yourself.

Work got the morning.

Responsibilities got the evening.

Surely these last forty minutes belong to you.

So you spend them watching a stranger pressure-wash a driveway.

There’s a small rebellion in it.

You can’t control much, but you can refuse to close your eyes.

Tomorrow’s version of you will handle the consequences.

Tomorrow’s version of you has filed several complaints.

***

There’s also something unsettling about ending a day that didn’t become what you hoped.

Going to bed makes it feel official.

You meant to call someone.

You meant to make something.

You meant to be a slightly different person by now.

But the dishes are still there, and you’re still you, and the clock has become rude.

So you linger.

As if staying awake might leave the day open for revisions.

As if at 12:47 a.m. you might suddenly turn the whole thing around.

Sometimes you do write the message.

Usually you just change apps.

Eventually, you have to let an unfinished day end.

***

That may be the part of sleep that resembles death most closely.

It asks you to put things down before everything is resolved.

The email can remain unanswered.

The argument can remain complicated.

You can leave the book halfway through a sentence.

This is difficult for a creature that would prefer closure, certainty, and perhaps one additional snack.

But every night you leave a life in progress.

You don’t become finished enough to rest.

You rest while unfinished.

There’s something useful in that.

A day doesn’t have to justify its existence before it can end.

And you don’t have to solve your entire personality before you’re allowed a pillow.

***

I think that’s why the phrase “rest in peace” has such weight.

Peace sounds simple until you try lying quietly in a dark room with your own mind.

Then you discover how much furniture is being dragged around upstairs.

Rest can be surprisingly hard to accept.

We turn it into a reward.

After the work.

After the improvement.

After we’ve become the kind of person who deserves an evening without guilt.

But the body has limited interest in your personal mythology.

You can tell yourself you’re indispensable.

Eventually, you will fall asleep during something.

Possibly your own argument about how indispensable you are.

The body has excellent comic timing.

***

And morning is strange in its own right.

You return gradually.

First the room.

Then your name, your plans, your unfinished business.

For a brief moment, you might simply be warm beneath a blanket.

Then memory clocks in.

“Oh, right. I have a dentist appointment.”

The entire machinery of your life starts up again.

You inherit yesterday’s concerns with remarkable efficiency.

But sometimes something has loosened.

The problem is still there, yet it takes up less of the room.

You have a different sentence available.

You can make the call.

You can laugh at the thing that felt unbearable at midnight.

Nothing magical happened.

Still, you came back with another morning.

***

That’s where the title starts to break down.

Death is final in a way sleep usually isn’t.

Sleep has a return address.

It leaves your shoes by the door.

It allows you to make absurdly confident plans about breakfast.

And that expectation changes everything.

You set an alarm because you assume there will be someone here to hear it.

You charge your phone because tomorrow apparently needs you reachable.

You put a glass of water beside the bed for a future self you have never met.

These are tiny acts of faith performed by people who would never describe themselves as especially hopeful.

We prepare to return.

***

So maybe sleep is death being shy.

Or maybe that’s just the joke we make because ordinary life becomes unsettling when you look at it long enough.

We spend all day trying to hold ourselves together.

Then, at night, we loosen our grip.

The room goes dark.

Our thoughts lose their shape.

Somewhere along the way, without noticing, we let go.

And most mornings, there we are again.

Bad hair. Dry mouth. A strong objection to the time.

Still here.

Still unfinished.

Still capable of doing something with the day.

For now, turn over. Find the cool side of the pillow.

The world can manage until morning.
