# Why the Suez Canal is so Insanely Important.. (and Nukes)

[OPEN: Satellite view of Earth. Zoom toward Egypt, then a container ship wedged diagonally across the Suez Canal.]

In March 2021, a ship got stuck.

Usually, this is a problem for the ship.

This time, it became a problem for factories, supermarkets, shipping companies, and entire economies.

The Ever Given had blocked the Suez Canal, and suddenly, one of the most important shortcuts on Earth was unavailable.

Ships queued. Deliveries slipped. The internet became obsessed with a photograph of an excavator digging beside a vessel approximately the size of a skyscraper.

But decades before that excavator became famous, American scientists had considered a rather different approach to digging a canal.

Nuclear bombs.

Hundreds of them.

Because the Suez Canal is important enough that someone looked at a desert, looked at a nuclear arsenal, and thought: there might be a business opportunity here.

To understand how we reached that point, we need to start with a surprisingly expensive geographical inconvenience.

Africa.

[VISUAL: Draw a shipping route from Asia toward Europe, stopping at Egypt.]

Imagine you operate a shipping company.

You have a vessel full of goods in Singapore, and customers waiting in Rotterdam.

Your ship needs to travel west across the Indian Ocean and then reach Europe.

Unfortunately, an entire continent occupies the space where you would ideally put the rest of your route.

Without Suez, you sail around southern Africa, pass the Cape of Good Hope, and head north through the Atlantic.

It works. Ships still do it.

But it adds thousands of nautical miles.

The Suez Canal Authority’s comparison puts Singapore to Rotterdam at roughly 8,300 nautical miles through the canal, versus 11,800 around Africa.

That saves approximately 3,500 nautical miles, or almost thirty percent of the longer journey. [Source: Suez Canal Authority](https://www.suezcanal.gov.eg/English/About/Pages/WhySuezCanal.aspx)

At sea, distance becomes money.

More sailing means more fuel, more operating expenses, and more time before a ship can collect its next load.

The cargo also spends longer earning nobody any money.

A container full of washing machines crossing the ocean is inventory somebody has paid for, but cannot yet sell.

Multiply that by thousands of containers, across thousands of voyages, and a line through Egypt starts looking extraordinarily valuable.

[VISUAL: Highlight the Mediterranean, Suez Canal, Red Sea, and Indian Ocean.]

The canal connects the Mediterranean Sea with the Red Sea, creating a direct maritime passage between Europe and the Indian Ocean.

Its modern length is about 193 kilometers.

A transit normally takes roughly twelve to sixteen hours.

And unlike the Panama Canal, Suez has no locks lifting ships over higher ground. Vessels pass through a channel at sea level. [Source: Suez Canal Authority](https://www.suezcanal.gov.eg/English/Pages/FAQ.aspx)

That makes the basic idea beautifully simple.

Dig through a narrow strip of land, and save ships a journey around a continent.

Of course, actually digging it was considerably less simple.

[VISUAL: Archival construction photographs, dredgers, workers, nineteenth-century maps.]

Construction began in 1859, and the canal officially opened in November 1869.

The project became associated with French diplomat Ferdinand de Lesseps, but Egyptian workers performed the immense labor needed to turn the proposal into a waterway.

Early construction relied heavily on forced labor, under brutal conditions, before mechanized excavation took a larger role.

Behind the elegant lines on the map were people moving earth, enduring disease, and paying the human cost of an enormous engineering project. [Source: Suez Canal Authority’s historical account](https://www.suezcanal.gov.eg/English/About/SuezCanal/Pages/CanalHistory.aspx)

Once open, the canal changed the economic meaning of distance.

Ports, shipping schedules, imperial communications, and commercial networks could reorganize around a much shorter journey.

Think about what a shortcut actually does.

It does not merely make an existing trip faster.

It can make previously expensive trade profitable. It can change which suppliers a business chooses and which ports become important.

Eventually, companies build their expectations around that shortcut.

The saved time becomes part of the system.

And once that happens, losing the shortcut becomes much more painful than simply returning to the old route.

[VISUAL: Modern container terminals, factories, supermarket shelves.]

Today, maritime transport carries more than eighty percent of world trade by volume.

Before the major Red Sea disruptions, Suez normally handled around fifteen percent of global maritime trade volume, according to the IMF.

The exact share varies with the year and what is being measured, but the scale is unmistakable.

An extraordinary amount of commerce passes through a very small piece of Egypt. [Sources: UNCTAD](https://unctad.org/publication/review-maritime-transport-2024), [IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2024/03/07/red-sea-attacks-disrupt-global-trade)

And those ships carry much more than finished products.

They carry fuel, industrial materials, machinery, and components that other businesses need before they can produce anything themselves.

Imagine a factory waiting for one relatively inexpensive part.

The part’s price might be tiny compared with the value of the finished product.

But if production cannot continue without it, a delayed container can cause losses far beyond the cargo’s value.

That is why shipping disruption spreads so effectively.

The canal connects production schedules as well as places.

[VISUAL: Animated calendar. A delayed ship causes factory and delivery dates to slide.]

There is another problem: ships themselves are a limited resource.

Suppose a vessel normally completes a round trip in sixty days.

Now imagine diversions push that to seventy.

The ship still exists. Its carrying capacity has not changed.

But it completes fewer trips each year.

To move the same amount of cargo on the same schedule, the shipping company needs additional vessels.

Containers also remain occupied longer.

So a longer route can tighten shipping capacity even without a single vessel being destroyed.

That pressure can raise freight rates elsewhere, including on routes that never touch Egypt.

This is how a narrow canal acquires a much wider economic footprint.

Even a business whose shipments travel across the Pacific can face consequences when carriers rearrange vessels to cover longer voyages elsewhere.

[VISUAL: Return to Ever Given, March 2021.]

The Ever Given demonstrated this vulnerability with almost embarrassing clarity.

On March 23, 2021, the roughly four-hundred-meter container ship ran aground and blocked the canal.

It remained stuck for six days.

Shipping companies had to make an uncomfortable decision: wait for an uncertain reopening, or commit to a much longer journey around Africa. [Sources: World Bank](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099536303102340994/pdf/IDU0396b42e60ffd104db30ac110b3f271e29f77.pdf), [Maersk](https://www.maersk.com/news/articles/2021/03/24/vessel-blockage-in-the-suez-canal)

Neither option came with a helpful refund button.

Waiting burned time.

Diverting burned time and fuel.

And the consequences continued after salvage teams freed the ship.

A queue at sea becomes a surge of arrivals at ports. That surge competes for berths, cranes, truck appointments, and warehouse space.

Meanwhile, empty containers arrive late for their next customers.

Reopening a canal restores movement, but it does not instantly restore the schedule.

Think of a canceled flight.

Getting the aircraft operational again is only part of the problem. The crew, passengers, airport slots, and connecting journeys also need to line up.

Now replace the passengers with enormous quantities of industrial supplies.

[VISUAL: On-screen text: “Cargo delayed ≠ cargo destroyed.”]

You may have seen estimates that the blockage held up around ten billion dollars in trade each day.

That describes the approximate value of goods delayed, rather than ten billion dollars disappearing daily.

Much of that cargo eventually arrived.

The economic damage came through delays, extra expenses, disrupted production, and missed opportunities.

The distinction matters.

Suez does not need to destroy goods to impose enormous costs.

It only needs to interrupt the timetable.

And long before giant container ships existed, governments understood that the timetable was a source of power.

[VISUAL: Archival footage of British ships and the canal zone.]

For Britain, the canal helped shorten the maritime connection with India and other parts of its empire.

A commercial shortcut also served imperial administration and naval strategy.

That combination made control of Suez intensely political.

In 1956, Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal Company, which had operated under British and French ownership.

For Egypt, this was a major assertion of sovereignty over infrastructure inside its own territory.

Britain and France saw a threat to their influence and interests.

They secretly coordinated with Israel on military action.

Israel invaded Egypt’s Sinai Peninsula in October. Britain and France then intervened, presenting their operation as an effort to protect the canal. [Source: U.S. Office of the Historian](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1953-1960/suez)

[VISUAL: Map of the invasion, followed by United Nations footage.]

The military operation produced a political disaster for Britain and France.

The United States opposed the invasion and pressured its allies to accept a ceasefire.

International pressure forced a withdrawal.

Egypt retained control of the canal, while Britain and France suffered a severe blow to their prestige.

The crisis exposed how much the balance of global power had changed since the canal’s nineteenth-century beginnings. [Source: National Army Museum](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/suez-crisis)

For our story, it also established a recurring concern.

If so much trade depends on one passage, and that passage can become entangled in war, what happens when it closes?

In 1967, the question became painfully concrete.

The canal closed during the Six-Day War and remained shut until June 1975.

Eight years. [Source: Suez Canal Authority](https://www.suezcanal.gov.eg/English/About/SuezCanal/Pages/CanalCharacteristics.aspx)

Ships went around Africa.

Trade continued, but using longer routes became a sustained operating reality.

The world could survive without Suez.

Doing so was expensive.

And that distinction helps explain why people became interested in building an alternative.

Which brings us to the nuclear bombs.

[VISUAL: Cheerful 1950s promotional imagery. Music shifts.]

During the early Cold War, American scientists explored a remarkable proposition: perhaps nuclear explosives could become tools for civilian construction.

The United States established Project Plowshare in 1957 to investigate peaceful applications of nuclear explosions.

Among the possibilities were excavating harbors and canals.

The attraction was concentrated energy.

Moving huge quantities of rock requires huge amounts of work. Nuclear devices could release enormous energy from a comparatively small package.

To enthusiasts, that suggested a way to make enormous earthmoving projects faster and cheaper. [Source: Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory](https://st.llnl.gov/news/look-back/sedan-event-project-plowshare)

You can almost hear the pitch meeting.

“We need to move a mountain.”

“How large is your equipment budget?”

“How flexible are your feelings about the mountain?”

This was a real research program, operating in an era when technological optimism could run far ahead of environmental judgment.

[VISUAL: Display the title of the 1963 Negev canal memorandum.]

In 1963, a memorandum from the Livermore laboratory explored using nuclear explosives to excavate a sea-level canal across Israel’s Negev Desert.

The proposed passage would connect the Mediterranean with the Gulf of Aqaba, offering an alternative to the Suez Canal.

The estimate involved approximately 520 nuclear explosions along a route roughly 160 miles long.

Five hundred and twenty.

That was the proposed answer to excessive dependence on one Egyptian waterway. [Source: Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/were-going-to-work-miracles/)

Now, the status of this idea matters.

It was a speculative feasibility study.

It never became a construction project.

The document demonstrates that researchers examined the concept; it does not establish an approved government program to build the canal.

Even with that qualification, it is an astonishing historical artifact.

A shipping shortcut was valuable enough to inspire calculations about reshaping an entire corridor of land with nuclear explosions.

[VISUAL: Nevada desert, then archival footage of the Sedan test.]

And nuclear excavation itself progressed beyond paper.

In July 1962, the United States conducted the Sedan test in Nevada.

The approximately hundred-kiloton explosion created a crater around twelve hundred feet across and more than three hundred feet deep.

The hole is still there.

So the central physical premise worked: a nuclear explosion could move a tremendous amount of earth.

But creating a crater is a much simpler achievement than delivering a safe, usable commercial waterway. [Source: Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory](https://st.llnl.gov/news/look-back/sedan-event-project-plowshare)

A canal requires stable banks, suitable depths, usable entrances, and predictable operating conditions.

It needs workers, maintenance, pilots, and ships willing to enter.

It also needs an environment where all those people can safely work.

Nuclear excavation brings radioactive contamination into that calculation, alongside blast effects and enormous uncertainty about cleanup.

A favorable estimate for breaking rock does not automatically become a favorable estimate for operating a shipping business.

The crater is only the beginning of the accounting.

[VISUAL: Proposed canal map, with surrounding countries and waterways highlighted.]

The political calculation was equally difficult.

A second canal would move some dependencies to a different territory.

Access through the Gulf of Aqaba and the wider Red Sea would still matter.

So would relations with neighboring countries.

Commercial users would need confidence that the route could operate reliably through future crises.

Engineering can change a map.

It cannot guarantee the political conditions around the new line.

The broader Plowshare program eventually faded amid environmental concerns and opposition.

The Negev canal remained an idea on paper. [Source: Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory](https://st.llnl.gov/news/look-back/sedan-event-project-plowshare)

Still, it reveals something important about Suez.

The economic incentive to escape a chokepoint can be enormous.

The challenge is finding an alternative whose complete costs make sense.

[VISUAL: Modern Red Sea map. Highlight Bab el-Mandeb.]

There is also a geographical catch.

The canal is only one part of the route.

Ships traveling between Suez and the Indian Ocean generally pass through the Red Sea and the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, near Yemen.

Keeping the canal open cannot ensure that the entire voyage is safe.

The attacks on commercial shipping that escalated in late 2023 demonstrated this.

Houthi forces in Yemen threatened vessels along the Red Sea route, prompting major shipping companies to divert around Africa.

The canal itself could remain navigable while the commercial attractiveness of using it deteriorated.

According to the IMF, trade volume through Suez fell fifty percent year over year during the first two months of 2024. [Source: IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2024/03/07/red-sea-attacks-disrupt-global-trade)

That episode exposed a different kind of vulnerability.

An obstruction physically prevents passage.

A security threat changes the decision to attempt passage.

Insurance, crew safety, potential damage, and schedule uncertainty all enter the calculation.

At some point, the longer route becomes the preferable commercial choice.

The shortcut still exists.

Using it costs too much risk.

[VISUAL: Split screen: Egyptian canal operations and ships rounding southern Africa.]

For Egypt, fewer transits also mean less toll revenue.

The canal earns foreign currency, making disruption a national economic issue as well as an international shipping problem.

For countries elsewhere, longer voyages can increase the delivered cost of fuel, food, and manufactured goods.

The impact is uneven.

Some companies hold enough inventory to absorb delays.

Others operate with little room for error.

Some consumers can absorb price increases.

Others already spend much of their income on essentials.

UNCTAD has warned that maritime disruptions can put particular pressure on food security and vulnerable economies. [Source: UNCTAD](https://unctad.org/press-material/vulnerability-supply-chains-exposed-global-maritime-chokepoints-come-under-pressure)

[VISUAL: Ports, railways, warehouses, alternate shipping routes.]

There are ways to reduce dependence.

Businesses can diversify suppliers, hold additional inventory, and plan alternative transport routes.

Shipping authorities can improve traffic management, maintenance, and emergency response.

Ports can prepare for irregular arrivals.

But resilience has a price.

Extra stock requires storage and working capital. Additional routes may cost more. Spare transport capacity must be paid for even when unused.

The same efficiencies that make global trade affordable can also concentrate its vulnerabilities.

Suez illustrates that bargain unusually clearly.

A narrow passage saves thousands of miles, so businesses organize around it.

The more useful it becomes, the more disruption matters.

[VISUAL: Pull back from the canal to show the entire world.]

That is why this strip of water has drawn engineers, imperial powers, presidents, shipping giants, and nuclear scientists.

It changes the practical distance between major parts of the world.

When it works, the benefits become routine: shorter journeys, lower costs, faster deliveries.

When access falters, those benefits suddenly become visible.

A grounded ship exposes the schedules.

A war exposes the politics.

A forgotten nuclear memorandum exposes just how far people once considered going to create another route.

And somewhere inside that enormous story is an excavator beside a stranded container ship, doing its very best.

Fortunately, that particular repair job did not require five hundred nuclear bombs.
