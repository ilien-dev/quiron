# The Eight Spiders

The first spider appeared on a Monday.

It was sitting in the kitchen sink, perfectly still, beside a mug I hadn’t washed since my mother’s funeral.

I remember thinking it looked unfinished. Seven legs instead of eight. A pale, swollen body. A black mark on its back shaped almost like a keyhole.

I put a glass over it.

Then I slid a piece of paper underneath, carried it outside, and released it beside the drain.

That night, someone knocked on my bedroom door.

Three soft taps.

I lived alone.

***

My mother left me two things: her house and a sealed envelope.

The solicitor said she’d insisted I open the envelope only after spending seven nights in the house.

“She was very particular about that.”

My mother had been particular about everything.

Curtains closed before sunset. Bedroom doors left open. No mirrors in the upstairs hall.

And spiders were never to be killed.

As a child, I thought that last rule was kindness.

If she found one in the bath, she’d lower a towel over the edge and wait until it climbed out.

But once, when I was nine, I crushed a spider with a slipper.

She slapped the slipper out of my hand.

Then she spent an hour on her knees, searching the floor.

She wasn’t looking for the spider.

She was counting its legs.

***

On Tuesday, there were two spiders in the sink.

Both had seven legs.

Both had the same mark.

I checked the drain, expecting an egg sac or a nest. Nothing.

I took them outside.

That evening, I heard my mother coughing upstairs.

Not a sound that reminded me of her.

Her cough.

The little breath she took afterward.

I stood at the bottom of the stairs until my legs ached.

Eventually, I called, “Mum?”

The coughing stopped.

Something moved across the landing.

A slow scrape, followed by a pause.

Then another scrape.

I slept on the sofa with the television on.

***

By Wednesday morning, I’d convinced myself that grief had a sound.

People heard dead relatives all the time. A familiar voice in running water. Footsteps in an empty room.

Three spiders waited in the sink.

This time, I photographed them.

When I enlarged the picture, I noticed something I hadn’t seen before.

The marks on their backs weren’t keyholes.

They were tiny faces.

Not detailed faces. Just suggestions: two dark indentations, a mouth stretched vertically.

One looked as though it was screaming.

I deleted the picture.

Then I checked the recently deleted folder and erased it again.

I used a plastic container to carry all three outside.

As I tipped them into the garden, one caught the rim.

For a moment, it hung there.

Its missing leg faced me.

The stump was pink.

Almost human.

***

On Thursday, I found four.

I left them in the sink.

I had a work call at nine and spent most of it watching the kitchen doorway.

At eleven, something knocked beneath my desk.

Three soft taps.

I pushed my chair back.

Nothing was there.

Then my phone rang.

The screen said Mum.

Her number had been disconnected the previous week.

I watched it ring until it stopped.

A voicemail appeared.

For nearly a minute, there was only breathing.

Then she whispered, “Don’t put them outside.”

I packed a bag.

Wallet, charger, clean clothes. I left everything else.

But when I opened the front door, the garden was gone.

Beyond the threshold was our upstairs landing.

Same faded carpet.

Same narrow window.

Same patch of wallpaper my mother had never repaired.

At the far end stood her bedroom door.

Closed.

From behind it, she said, “You’ve let four go.”

I shut the front door.

When I opened it again, the garden was back.

I stayed.

I wish I could explain why.

But fear doesn’t always make you run.

Sometimes it makes the world very small.

One kitchen.

One sink.

Four things you don’t dare touch.

***

On Friday, there were five.

I counted them from the doorway.

Then I counted again.

One had eight legs.

It was larger than the others, with a dark, wet-looking stripe down its abdomen.

While I watched, it climbed onto another spider.

There was no struggle.

It pressed its mouth against the smaller one’s missing limb.

A thread emerged.

White, glossy, impossibly thick.

The spider pulled backward until the thread stretched between them.

Then it began to sew.

I heard the needle.

That’s the only way I can describe it.

A delicate clicking against porcelain.

I ran upstairs for the envelope.

Seven nights, the solicitor had said.

I’d spent only four.

I tore it open.

Inside was a sheet of paper covered in my mother’s cramped handwriting.

*If there are fewer than eight, you still have time.*

Below that:

*They are not coming into the house.*

*They are leaving me.*

***

The letter said my mother had found the first one after my father disappeared.

I’d always been told he left us.

No note. No explanation.

Just a suitcase missing from the wardrobe.

According to the letter, he’d gone into the cellar after hearing someone call his name.

My mother found him sitting against the far wall.

Alive.

Smiling.

With something moving beneath his tongue.

She called an ambulance.

Before it arrived, he opened his mouth.

Eight spiders came out.

Then, she wrote, he unfolded.

There were several lines after that crossed out so violently the paper had torn.

The next readable sentence was:

*I thought burning him would stop it.*

She’d spent thirty-one years keeping whatever remained of my father beneath the house.

The spiders came every year.

Seven nights.

One more each morning.

Each missing a leg.

“They need eight whole bodies,” she wrote. “But they cannot grow the last part themselves.”

Every year, she gave them something.

A fingernail.

A tooth.

A strip of skin.

Enough to complete their little bodies.

Enough to make them return downstairs.

Her final sentence ran crookedly into the margin.

*I am sorry I won’t be here to teach you how much.*

***

I called the police.

I didn’t mention spiders.

I said someone was in the house.

The operator asked for my address.

When I gave it, she went quiet.

Then she asked, “Is your mother there?”

“She’s dead.”

“I know.”

The line clicked.

My mother’s voice said, very gently, “You shouldn’t have sent them outside.”

I threw the phone.

From the kitchen came the sound of something heavy dropping into the sink.

***

On Saturday morning, there were six.

Two had eight legs.

The others were crowded around something pink.

I stayed far enough away that I couldn’t identify it.

I found my mother’s sewing box in the sitting room.

Under the buttons were folded gauze, surgical scissors, and a small bottle of antiseptic.

There were also thirty-one little envelopes.

Each held a photograph.

My mother, once a year, standing in the kitchen.

In the earliest picture, she was younger than I am now.

By the tenth, she never smiled.

By the twentieth, she wore gloves.

In the last, taken three months before she died, a bandage covered her left eye.

I sat on the floor until darkness filled the windows.

Then I heard the cellar bolt slide open.

I hadn’t known there was a cellar.

Behind the refrigerator, something knocked.

Three soft taps.

And my father said my name.

I don’t remember his voice.

I was too young when he disappeared.

But I knew it anyway.

***

On Sunday, seven spiders waited.

Six had eight legs.

The last one dragged itself in circles around the drain.

I searched the house for another letter, another explanation.

In my mother’s room, beneath the mattress, I found a notebook.

Most pages contained dates and measurements.

Then, near the end:

*It isn’t satisfied with pieces anymore.*

And below:

*It asks about him.*

My name followed.

Written over and over.

On the final page, she had pressed so hard the words marked the sheets underneath.

*Do not answer when it uses my voice.*

Downstairs, my mother began to cry.

She sounded exhausted.

She said she was cold.

She said she’d been waiting so long.

I put both hands over my mouth.

Because I almost answered.

***

The eighth spider appeared before dawn.

It was in my bed.

On the pillow beside my face.

Seven legs.

A tiny, pale body.

No marking on its back.

Just smooth skin.

Downstairs, water began running into the sink.

Then came footsteps.

Not on the stairs.

Inside the walls.

Moving toward my room.

I understood, finally, why my mother had kept the bedroom doors open.

Why there were no mirrors in the hall.

Why she counted the legs.

She’d been watching it learn to make a person.

The footsteps stopped outside my door.

My mother whispered, “One little piece.”

The spider lifted its front legs.

And opened its mouth.

***

They found me in the garden after sunrise.

Barefoot.

Bleeding.

Holding a pair of surgical scissors.

The house was empty.

The police asked what happened to my left hand.

I told them I’d caught it in a door.

I sold the house six months later.

The buyers renovated everything.

Last week, they sent me a message asking about the locked space behind the refrigerator.

I haven’t replied.

This morning, I found a spider in my sink.

Eight legs.

And on its back, a tiny white scar.

Exactly where my finger used to be.
