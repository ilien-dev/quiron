“Without the Electoral College, a handful of big cities would choose the president.”

You’ve probably heard that argument. It sounds intuitive: cities have lots of people, rural areas have fewer, and the Electoral College supposedly keeps one from overpowering the other.

But that explanation confuses three different things: cities, metropolitan areas, and states.

And once we separate them, the argument starts falling apart.

First, what counts as a city?

When someone says “Los Angeles,” do they mean the municipality? Los Angeles County? Or the larger metropolitan area, including neighboring communities?

Those are very different populations.

A city boundary is a legal line. Cross it, and you might enter another municipality without noticing any change in the buildings, traffic, or economy.

Metropolitan areas try to capture those connections: the central city, its suburbs, and surrounding communities linked by commuting.

So when a map shows America’s population concentrated around cities, it doesn’t mean everyone lives downtown, shares the same lifestyle, or votes the same way.

It includes apartment dwellers, suburban homeowners, warehouse workers, commuters, and people living near the edge of farmland.

Calling all of them “the cities” hides more than it explains.

Now consider the claim that a few cities could dominate a national popular vote.

Large cities contain millions of voters. But the country contains hundreds of millions of people, spread across thousands of communities.

Even the biggest cities account for only a fraction of the national population.

And population isn’t the same as votes. Some residents are children. Some aren’t eligible to vote. Some don’t turn out.

Most importantly, cities aren’t unanimous.

A candidate doesn’t win every vote in Chicago simply by winning Chicago. Under a national popular vote, an opposing vote there would count exactly as much as a supporting vote somewhere else.

Which brings us to what the Electoral College actually does.

Each state receives electors equal to its representation in Congress: its House seats, plus two senators. Washington, D.C., also receives three electors.

Almost every state awards all its electors to the candidate who wins the statewide vote.

That system doesn’t balance cities against rural communities directly.

It groups voters by state.

A rural voter in California and an urban voter in California are part of the same statewide contest. If their candidate loses that contest, neither contributes to that candidate’s electoral total.

Meanwhile, a narrow victory in a competitive state can deliver its entire electoral delegation.

That creates the system’s central campaign incentive: focus on states where additional votes might change the electoral outcome.

Not necessarily the smallest states.

Not necessarily the most rural states.

The competitive states.

Wyoming’s small population gives it more electors per resident than California. But that doesn’t automatically make Wyoming a campaign priority. A predictable result offers little reason to spend scarce campaign time there.

A competitive state offers much more.

And inside that state, campaigns pursue whatever combination of urban, suburban, and rural voters can get them across the line.

So the Electoral College doesn’t remove geographic concentration from campaigning. It changes which geographic concentrations matter.

There’s also a deeper question here: what exactly are we trying to represent?

The United States is a federal system. States have important constitutional roles, and the Senate gives every state equal representation.

But the presidency is one office exercising power over the entire country.

Should choosing that office give equal weight to individual voters? Or should state boundaries alter how their votes translate into victory?

That’s a real disagreement about political design.

“Big cities would control everything” is a poor substitute for having it.

A national popular vote would create different incentives. Candidates could gain useful votes anywhere, including places they currently expect to lose.

It wouldn’t guarantee equal attention to every community. Campaigns would still consider advertising costs, turnout, and persuasion.

But a vote’s contribution to the result wouldn’t depend on whether it happened to be cast in a closely divided state.

The argument, then, isn’t cities versus the countryside.

It’s whether we should choose a president by adding up individual votes nationwide, or by converting state results into electoral votes.

Whatever your answer, start with the system we actually have.

Because maps show where people live.

They don’t tell us how much their voices should count.
