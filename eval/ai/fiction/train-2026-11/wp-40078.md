# The Need Is Constant

The press conference was supposed to be about a hurricane relief fund. I had the remarks printed in fourteen-point font, a lectern with the logo centered, and a glass of water I had no intention of drinking.

Then a junior reporter from a podcast nobody had heard of raised a hand and asked why there were no photographs of me taken before 1881.

I could have lied. I've had practice. But the room was already buzzing, someone had leaked the sealed Geneva charter, and frankly, after a century and a half of night shifts, I was tired.

"Because," I said, "I didn't own a camera."

That was eleven days ago. The world has handled it about as well as you'd expect.

---

Let me explain what nobody seems willing to hear, because the cable news panels are too busy drawing diagrams of my canines.

Before the Red Cross, there was hunting. Alleys. Necks. Panic. Every one of us carried the memory of someone we'd taken too much from, someone who didn't get up. It was ugly, and it was unsustainable, and the smart ones among us knew we were one bad decade away from torches and pitchforks becoming a coordinated policy.

So we built a system.

Clara Barton was the best partner I ever had. Clara knew from the first meeting, and Clara didn't flinch. Clara looked at me across a candlelit table in a boarding house in Dansville and said, "You need blood. People need blood. Why is everyone being so dramatic about it?"

We wrote the bylaws that night. Voluntary donation only. Never more than a pint. Juice and cookies afterward, mandatory, because a donor who faints is a donor who doesn't come back. Ninety-five percent of every unit goes to hospitals. Five percent goes to us. That's the tithe, and it has never once changed.

Do you know how many humans have been killed by a vampire since 1881? I do. I keep the ledger. It's a short ledger, and every name on it was investigated and punished by our own tribunal, which is more than most institutions can say.

---

The protests outside headquarters are loud but polite. One sign reads *GIVE BLOOD, NOT YOUR SOUL*. Another reads *IS THE SNACK TABLE A METAPHOR*. (It is not. The cookies are just cookies. People like cookies.)

What surprised me was the other line. The one that wraps around the block every morning.

Donors.

More than we've seen in decades. A retired schoolteacher told one of my phlebotomists, a lovely woman named Ines who was turned in 1953 and still hums Perry Como, that she'd been giving blood for forty years and was thrilled to finally know where the cookie money went. A college kid asked if he could request that his pint go "to someone cool." A man in a suit asked, very quietly, whether we were hiring.

We are not, by the way. Not like that.

---

Tonight the Senate committee wants testimony. They've scheduled it for nine p.m., which I appreciate as a courtesy, even though I suspect someone just wants the ratings.

They'll ask whether we've been manipulating the blood supply. We have, in the sense that we built it, staffed it, and kept it running through two world wars, a pandemic, and every flood and fire you can name.

They'll ask what we want. I'll tell them the truth: exactly what we've always had. A pint, freely given. A cot, a bandage, a cookie, a thank-you.

They'll ask whether people should be afraid.

And I'll smile, carefully, with my mouth closed, and say what we've printed on every poster since before their grandparents were born:

*The need is constant.*

It's never been a threat. It's just honest.
