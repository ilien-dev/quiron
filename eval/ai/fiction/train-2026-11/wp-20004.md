# The Long Way Around

The kid was the one who started it, which figured. I'd been a talker at eight. I'd stopped somewhere around fourteen and never really started again.

"Which one of you is Dad?"

He said it from the middle of the back seat, wedged between the two older versions of me like a duffel bag nobody wanted to claim. He had a Band-Aid on his chin. I remembered that Band-Aid. I'd gotten it jumping off the Hendersons' shed to see if an umbrella would work. It hadn't.

Nobody answered him. The highway hummed. Somewhere past Barstow the desert had gone the color of weak tea, and the radio had given up on everything but static and one gospel station we'd all silently agreed to ignore.

"I'm serious," the kid said. "He's Dad." He pointed at the man on his left, the one I'd been avoiding in the rearview mirror for two hundred miles.

Fifty-two, I'd guessed, when we all got in the car. Maybe fifty-five. Gray at the temples, heavier through the middle, a pair of reading glasses hooked in the collar of a flannel shirt that I recognized with a small cold shock as the one hanging in my closet right now. He had our father's jaw. Everybody said I had our father's jaw, but on me it was a rumor. On him it was a confession.

"I'm not Dad," he said. His voice was gentle. "I'm you. Just later."

"You look like Dad."

"Yeah." He looked out the window. "That happens."

On the kid's other side, the teenager snorted. Seventeen, all elbows and contempt, earbuds in but not plugged into anything, because the phone we'd had at seventeen had died in the first hour and he'd refused to borrow a charger from any of us on principle. He'd spent most of the drive with his forehead against the glass, performing boredom so hard it looked like a religious practice.

"That's not a compliment," the teenager said.

"Nobody said it was," said the man in the flannel.

"He was an asshole."

"Language," said the old man in the passenger seat, without looking up from the map.

The old man was the one who scared me most, though not for the reasons I'd have expected. He was maybe eighty. Thin, spotted hands. Hearing aid in the left ear. He'd insisted on the paper map even though the car had navigation, and he was following our route with one fingertip like a man reading Braille. He didn't scare me because he was old. He scared me because he seemed so *peaceful*, and I couldn't imagine what it would take to get from me to him.

"He *was* an asshole," the teenager repeated, quieter.

"He was," the man in flannel agreed. "He was also scared out of his mind most of the time. Both things."

"Oh, here we go."

"I'm just saying."

"You're *defending* him."

"I'm not—" The man in flannel stopped. Took off his reading glasses, put them back in his collar. A gesture so precisely our father's that I felt the wheel shiver under my hands. "I'm not defending him. I'm telling you what I found out. Later. When I had a kid of my own and realized I didn't know what I was doing either."

The car went very quiet.

I kept my eyes on the road. The lines went by, white and white and white.

"We have a kid?" I said.

I hadn't meant to talk. I was thirty-four, I was the driver, and I'd figured my job was to get us where we were going and not think too hard about why I was the one holding the wheel. But it came out of me before I could stop it, and then it was hanging there.

The man in flannel met my eyes in the mirror. "You don't know yet?"

"I don't—we haven't—Dana and I talked about it, but—"

"Dana," the teenager said, sitting up. "Who's Dana?"

"You'll like her," I told him. "She'll think your poetry's terrible but she'll read all of it anyway."

He went red to the ears. "I don't write poetry."

"Sure."

"I *don't*."

"The notebook under the mattress," said the man in flannel, mildly, and the teenager made a sound like a kettle and put his forehead back against the window.

The kid was looking back and forth between us all, delighted. "We write *poetry*?"

"Badly," said three of us at once.

The old man laughed. It was a good laugh, dry and surprised, and it turned into a cough that went on a little too long. We all waited, the way you wait for weather to pass. When he was done he folded the map in his lap and turned around in his seat, slow, his seatbelt cutting across his thin chest.

"Her name is June," he said. "The kid. You'll want to name her after Grandma, and Dana will say no, and you'll compromise on June because she was born in June, and you'll both pretend that was the plan all along."

I had to pull over.

I didn't decide to. My hands just did it. The car crunched onto the gravel shoulder and stopped, and the desert went on being enormous and indifferent on both sides of us, and I sat there gripping the wheel with my hazards ticking like a second heartbeat.

"Is she okay?" I asked. "Is she—am I—"

I couldn't finish it. *Am I him.* That was the question. That had been the question for thirty-four years, under everything, the way groundwater is under everything.

The man in flannel leaned forward between the front seats. Up close he smelled like coffee and cedar and something I didn't recognize yet, some aftershave I hadn't bought.

"You're going to yell at her once," he said. "She's going to be four. She's going to spill grape juice on a work thing, a big work thing, and you're going to yell, and she's going to flinch. And you're going to see her flinch and it's going to be the worst moment of your life."

The kid had gone very still. He knew about flinching.

"And then what?" the teenager asked. Not sarcastic. Not performing. Just seventeen and asking.

"And then you get down on the floor," said the man in flannel. "Right there in the grape juice. And you tell her you're sorry. You tell her it wasn't her fault, it was yours, grown-ups make mistakes too. And she pats your face with her sticky hand and says *it's okay, Daddy*, and you go into the bathroom and cry for twenty minutes."

"Dad never said sorry," the kid said.

"No," said the man in flannel. "He didn't. That's the difference. That's the whole difference. It's not that you never mess up. It's that you get down on the floor."

The old man reached back and put his hand on the kid's knee. The kid looked at the spotted hand for a long moment and then put his own small hand on top of it. Eight and eighty. Same knuckles.

"Where are we going?" the kid asked. "Nobody ever said."

I looked at the old man. He looked at the map in his lap, then out at the road, the long straight shimmer of it running off toward mountains that didn't seem to get any closer.

"Same place as always," he said. "Just taking the long way around."

"Is it far?"

"It's far," the old man said. "But it's a nice drive. You'll see."

I put the car in gear. Checked the mirror. Behind me the teenager had pulled out one earbud and was looking at the kid with an expression I had never once in my life seen on my own face: something like tenderness, embarrassed of itself.

"Hey," the teenager said. "You wanna hear a poem? It's bad."

"Yeah," said the kid.

I pulled back onto the highway. The man in flannel sat back and closed his eyes. The old man unfolded the map again and found our place on it with one finger.

And the teenager started to read.
