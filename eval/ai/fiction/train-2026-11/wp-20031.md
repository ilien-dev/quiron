**The Night the Lights Went Out**

The blackout hit Calle Olmo at 9:14 on a Thursday in July, somewhere between the second rerun of the telenovela and Mrs. Ruiz's third attempt to microwave leftover arroz. One moment the streetlights hummed, and the next the whole block sank into a soft, startled dark.

Marco was the first one out on the balcony. "Oh, come on," he said to nobody. Then he heard Lupe's door slam downstairs, and her voice rising over the rattle of her cart. "Who's got candles? Anybody? I know somebody has candles."

Within ten minutes, the entire building had congregated on the roof. Nobody remembered who suggested it. Mr. Okafor carried up the folding chairs he kept for his grandchildren's visits. The Ferreira twins dragged a cooler of warm soda and a bag of oranges. Someone found a battery radio that picked up only static and a faint Sunday-league football match from a country that wasn't this one. Lupe brought a box of birthday candles, which turned out to be the most useful thing anyone had brought.

What I remember most is how quiet the city became. Without the hum of refrigerators and the glow from a thousand windows, the sky seemed to lean down toward us. Stars showed up in places I had never noticed them, thick and uneven, like someone had spilled sugar across black cloth.

"I can't see my hand," said little Sofía, holding her hand up in front of her face anyway, as if making a point.

"That's because you're holding it too close," her grandfather told her, and she giggled so hard she had to sit down.

We started telling stories without quite deciding to. Mr. Okafor described the power cut he'd lived through in Lagos when he was nine, how his mother had sung to keep everyone calm while the rain drummed on the tin roof. Mrs. Ruiz told a long, winding tale about a bet she'd lost in 1979 that involved a goat, a bus driver, and a wedding cake, and nobody was entirely sure it was true, which made it better. The Ferreira twins argued about whether the Big Dipper was really a dipper or just a very confident spoon. Lupe didn't tell a story. She just laughed, loud and unselfconscious, the way she did when she had decided a moment was worth it.

Somewhere in the middle of that, Mr. Okafor produced a harmonica from his shirt pocket. It was missing two notes, but he played it anyway, and Mrs. Ruiz began to sing an old bolero in a voice that cracked on the high parts. Then Marco's neighbor, a quiet accountant named Tomás who never spoke at the building meetings, began to hum along, and before long we were all singing badly and happily, passing the oranges back and forth.

I don't know how long it lasted. An hour, maybe two. Long enough that the candles burned low and someone had to fetch more from the pantry. Long enough that the twins fell asleep on a pile of cushions, and Sofía insisted on keeping watch for shooting stars, and then, improbably, one appeared. She screamed, delighted, and everyone looked up at once.

"Did you see that?" she demanded. "Make a wish! Everybody make a wish!"

We all went quiet, the way people do when they're asked for something private in front of others. I don't remember what I wished for. I remember looking around at the faces lit gold by a ring of birthday candles: Lupe, wiping her eyes from laughing too hard; Mr. Okafor, tapping his foot to a tune only he could hear; Mrs. Ruiz, who had argued with me about parking for three years and was now holding my hand without any explanation.

At 11:42, the lights came back on all at once, with a clunk and a buzz, and the whole block cheered from the windows. Someone downstairs was still playing music on a phone. The stars vanished into the orange haze of the city, and we all blinked, squinting, as if waking from a dream.

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Lupe stood, brushed off her skirt, and said, "Same time next blackout?"

"Let's hope it's not for another ten years," Mr. Okafor said.

"Ten years is too long," Mrs. Ruiz replied, already gathering the chairs. "Next time, the bolero is my choice."

We carried everything back downstairs together, the candles, the cooler, the sleeping twins slung over tired shoulders, and I thought, not for the first time, that we were a strange and lucky collection of people. We had lived side by side for years without ever really knowing one another. It took one dark night and a box of birthday candles to turn us into something like a family.
