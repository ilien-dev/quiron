**The Pockets**

Everyone on Ironbridge Street thinks I'm a nosy old woman, and they're right, but nobody ever asks why I check every pocket before a load goes into the dryer. My husband Ray used to say I'd find a lottery ticket in a church collection plate. He wasn't wrong. Once I found a fifty-dollar bill folded into a tiny square inside a child's sock, and I still keep it taped under the register like a relic.

I'm sixty-eight. I wear my reading glasses on a beaded chain my granddaughter made, and I sing along to the Spanish radio station even though I can't carry a tune across a parking lot. I don't own a smartphone. I think the internet is a rumor that got out of hand. Every morning at six I unlock the laundromat, start the coffee maker that predates the Carter administration, and tell the pigeons on the sill that they are not welcome.

Last Tuesday a boy came in around eleven at night. He was maybe nineteen, skinny, with his hood up even though it was eighty degrees outside. He had one duffel bag and a plastic grocery sack of wet clothes that smelled like the river. He had no quarters. He had a crumpled five and a look I recognized from my own son's face the year he stopped calling.

"Ma'am," he said, reading the hand-lettered sign taped to my window: PLEASE DON'T ASK FOR CREDIT. WE ARE NOT A BANK. "Can I wash these? I'll pay you tomorrow."

I could have said no. Ray would have told me to say no. But I looked at that sack and thought about the girl I was at nineteen, when I took a bus from Porto with one suitcase and a phone number written on my palm. A woman in Newark gave me a job and a bed for six months without asking for a single document. Nobody gave me anything for free, but somebody gave me the chance to pay it back.

"Quarters are in the jar," I said. "Don't mix the whites with that red shirt. It will ruin everything."

He nodded like I'd handed him a prize. The machines hummed their old song, the one with the rattle in the third dryer I keep meaning to fix. I pulled out the folding chair and sat with him. He told me his name was Daniel, that he'd been sleeping in his car since March, and that the car was now in a tow lot across town. He didn't cry. Boys like him don't cry in front of strangers. They just stare at the spinning drum as if it might explain something.

When his clothes were done, I found a folded paper in the back pocket of his jeans. It was a letter addressed to someone named Marisol, never sent. I didn't read it. I put it back on the folding table and said, "Pocket check. Always." He laughed, the first real laugh of the night, and I felt something loosen in my chest.

Here's what I didn't tell him. My son, Tiago, is thirty-four and lives in Atlanta. He sends a text every Sunday that says "Hi Ma, all good." Four words. I count them like rosary beads. For years I thought those four words were a wall. Lately I think they're a door he's holding open with his foot, not sure whether to let it swing shut or leave it cracked.

Daniel left at midnight with a clean duffel, a bag of warm towels, and a free coffee in a paper cup. I gave him the tip jar money, eleven dollars and some change, and he tried to refuse it. I made him take it. Pride is a luxury, and it costs more than the dryer.

Tomorrow I'll check the pockets again. Maybe he'll come back for the letter. Maybe he won't. Either way, the fifty stays under the register, and when someone asks why I'm so nosy, I'll tell them the truth: sometimes the most important thing you find is what someone forgot to take out.
