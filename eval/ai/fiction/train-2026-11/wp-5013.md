# Compliance

The case file landed on my desk with a thud that meant trouble. Thin files are always trouble.

"Filing cabinet," said Rookie Okafor, reading over my shoulder. "Steelcase Model 4B, putty beige. Discontinued 1987."

"And?"

"And it's the last one. Basement of a credit union in Dayton. Scheduled for scrap at nine tomorrow morning." Okafor swallowed. "Zero coverage, sir. Not a sketch. Not a limerick. Nothing."

I set down my coffee. In twenty-two years with the Bureau, I'd seen us close gaps on everything. The 1994 Toyota Tercel. The concept of escrow. That one specific cloud over Nebraska that looked a little like Grover Cleveland. Somebody always got there first. The internet is vast, and it is horny, and mostly it does our job for us.

But every so often something slips through. Something so beige that nobody, anywhere, has ever looked at it and felt anything.

"If it gets scrapped uncovered," Okafor said, "that's a breach."

"That's a *precedent*," I said. "You know what happens when the Rule has an exception?"

Okafor didn't. Nobody does. That's the point. We don't find out.

---

We drove to Gloria's place in the rain.

Gloria Vasquez-Pruitt had been the Bureau's artist of last resort since before digital tablets. She'd covered the Hoover Dam. She'd covered the Treaty of Ghent. Legend said she'd once covered the color teal, and three people in the room had needed to sit down.

She answered the door in a bathrobe, holding a watering can.

"I'm retired, Frank."

"It's a filing cabinet, Gloria."

She paused. "What kind?"

"Steelcase 4B. Putty."

Something flickered behind her bifocals. Professional pride, maybe. Or pity.

"Four drawers or three?"

"Four."

She sighed the sigh of a woman who has been asked to save the world one too many times, set down the watering can, and went to find her pencils.

---

We waited in her kitchen for three hours. Okafor kept trying to peek. I kept slapping their hand away.

"Rule one of the Bureau, Rookie. We enforce. We don't *consume*."

"Then how do we verify compliance?"

I tapped the clipboard. "Gloria signs. I countersign. The form goes to Records. Nobody looks."

"Nobody?"

"Somebody in Records looks," I admitted. "We don't talk about Dennis."

At 2:14 a.m., Gloria walked out holding a single sheet of paper face-down against her chest. Her hands were shaking slightly. She looked like someone who had seen the shape of a filing cabinet in a way that God had not intended.

"The bottom drawer," she said quietly. "It sticks. You have to jiggle it."

"Gloria, I don't need to know—"

"*You have to jiggle it, Frank.*"

I signed the form. She signed the form. Okafor stared at the ceiling and hummed.

At 8:51 a.m., the Dayton credit union's Model 4B went into the crusher, fully compliant. Somewhere, the universe exhaled.

---

Back at the office, Okafor was quiet for a long time.

"Sir. Can I ask something?"

"Shoot."

"The Rule says *if it exists*. No exceptions."

"That's right."

"Well, the Bureau exists." Okafor looked around: the fluorescent lights, the cubicles, the motivational poster of an eagle. "*We* exist. So doesn't that mean..."

I didn't answer. I just stood, walked to the corner of the room, and opened the bottom drawer of our own filing cabinet. I had to jiggle it.

Inside was a single, very thick folder. The tab read: **BUREAU OF RULE 34 ENFORCEMENT — SELF-COVERAGE. FILED 2004. CLOSED.**

Okafor reached for it. I shut the drawer.

"Don't," I said. "Especially not page twelve."

"What's on page twelve?"

I picked up my coffee and looked out the window at the rain.

"The eagle poster," I said. "Gloria was very thorough."
