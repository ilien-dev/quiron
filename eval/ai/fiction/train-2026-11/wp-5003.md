When the bite came, everyone stopped.

Not because it hurt. Tommy had barely gotten his teeth through the sleeve of Ben’s sweatshirt, and anyway, biting for real was against the rules.

Everyone stopped because Ben’s mother was standing on the back porch.

“Benjamin! Inside, please!”

Ben looked at the porch, then at us.

“No,” he whispered.

Across the yard, Tommy let go of his arm.

“Dude,” he said. “You’ve been bitten.”

“Shut up. I know.”

We’d survived the fall of the shed. We’d escaped the horde behind the Millers’ garage. When our supplies ran low, Ben had crawled through the kitchen window and brought back six granola bars and a whole sleeve of crackers.

He was our leader.

He couldn’t leave on a Thursday.

“Benjamin, your dad’s almost home. You need to set the table.”

Ben pulled his sleeve over his wrist. “How long do I have?”

I checked my watch. It had been broken since July, but I looked anyway.

“Ten minutes. Maybe less.”

“That’s what you said about Marcus.”

Nobody looked at the empty lawn chair beside the fence.

Marcus had gone down Tuesday. Piano lessons. His grandmother had picked him up in a silver Honda, and he’d waved through the back window while Tommy shuffled after the car.

“We can cut it off,” Sophie said.

She raised the orange plastic saw from her doctor kit.

Ben studied his arm. Then the saw.

“No. It’s too late.”

“It’s not too late,” I said.

From the porch came the sound of the screen door opening.

“Ben. Don’t make me come out there.”

He flinched. We all did.

Somewhere beyond the fence, a dog barked.

Ben took off his backpack and handed it to Sophie.

“You know the route to the creek.”

She nodded.

“Stay away from the Patels’ yard. Their sprinkler comes on at six.”

“What about you?” she asked.

He didn’t answer.

Instead, he reached into his pocket and gave me the compass we’d found in a cereal box. The needle pointed wherever you tilted it.

“You get them there.”

I closed my fingers around it.

“I can’t.”

“You have to.”

“You’re just setting the table,” Tommy said.

Sophie turned on him so fast her ponytail hit his face.

“Stay in character.”

“Benjamin!”

“COMING!” Ben shouted.

Then, quieter, to us: “It’s getting worse.”

He staggered backward, clutching the bite. His heel caught the garden hose, but he turned the stumble into a fall and landed on one knee.

Sophie made a small noise.

I almost laughed. Then Ben looked up at me, and I didn’t want to ruin it.

He’d rubbed dirt across his cheek sometime during the afternoon. There was a leaf stuck in his hair. He looked like he’d come a long way.

“Promise me something,” he said.

“What?”

“Don’t let me turn.”

Tommy lifted his Nerf gun.

Sophie pushed it down.

“Not yet.”

Ben’s mother folded her arms.

He saw her and hurried.

“Tell my brother—”

“Tell him yourself,” I said. “Tomorrow.”

For a second, he was just Ben again.

“I might have to go to my dad’s tomorrow.”

“Oh.”

He looked down at the grass and picked up a stick.

“Take care of them, okay?”

I nodded.

He lay back, spread his arms, and closed his eyes.

We stood around him until his mother said, “Ben, seriously.”

Then he got up.

He brushed off his jeans, left the backpack with Sophie, and walked toward the house without looking back.

The screen door slammed.

Through the kitchen window, we watched him wash his hands.

Tommy lowered his gun.

“So,” he said. “Creek?”

Sophie shouldered the backpack.

I opened the compass.

“Creek.”

Behind us, Ben appeared at the window and made a horrible face, fingers hooked like claws.

Sophie waved him down.

“You’re dead,” she mouthed.

He grinned and ducked out of sight.
