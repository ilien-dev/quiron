**The Letter Nobody Asked For**

*Transcribed from a cracked slate recovered in the Vell Archive, Year 214 of the Open Calendar. Translator's note: the author's margins are full of corrections, which I have kept.*

To whoever finds this, and especially to whoever is living in what you call the present:

I know how this sounds. You will read it on a device that still feels like a miracle to you, and you will think I am being dramatic about your little habits. Please give me a few minutes anyway. My grandmother's grandmother lived where you live, and she wrote things like this too, and nobody listened to her either.

Start with sleep. In your time, people brag about how little of it they get. They wear exhaustion like a medal and call it "hustle." A child who sleeps nine hours is called lazy by adults who have never once asked the child why they are so tired. We have records of your era's schools starting before dawn, and of workers who could not stop to eat. You called this discipline. We call it a slow, self-inflicted injury that your institutions were glad to have people perform on themselves for free.

Then there is the way you measured a person's worth. Your question, when you met someone, was "What do you do?" Not "What are you like?" Not "What makes you laugh?" What they *produced*. A grandmother who raised six children and tended a garden was, in your accounting, a person who "doesn't work." A man who was dying and could no longer earn wages was treated as a burden to be quietly managed. You had a word for people like this, and the word was "unproductive," as if a human being were a machine that had gone out of warranty.

Medicine worst of all. I have read the accounts. Families in your time sold their houses to pay for treatments they had been told would save a life, and sometimes those treatments were withheld anyway, because the insurer had calculated that the patient was not worth the expense. People went bankrupt from being sick. Some chose to die at home rather than leave their children with debts that would follow them for decades. You had enough wealth to end that suffering, and you chose to ration it by bank balance. I cannot make that sound reasonable no matter how I phrase it.

Consider also how you treated the animals you ate. You kept billions of creatures in buildings where they never saw the sky, never stood on grass, never did a single thing their bodies were built for. You knew this. Your own researchers published it. And you shrugged, because the price per pound was low and the habit was old. The people who will read this later will find that hard to believe, so I will say it plainly: you knew, and you kept doing it, because stopping was inconvenient.

And the loneliness. You had devices that could connect you to almost anyone on Earth, and many of you felt more alone than any generation before you. You built machines that were designed to keep your attention, not to care whether you were well, and you scrolled through them at night while your friendships thinned out like paint on an old wall. I think some part of you knew something was wrong. People in your time kept saying, "I should call my mother," and then did not. The sentence is so common in your archives that it reads like a confession.

I want to be fair, because my teachers warned me against assuming that our ancestors were fools. You were not stupid. Many of you were kind. The woman who lived next door to me in the old records, whose letters survived, spent her evenings feeding strays and writing to prisoners she had never met. Plenty of people in your time fought hard against every one of these habits, and some of them won pieces of the fight. The norms I am describing were not universal. They were simply accepted, which is a different thing, and far more dangerous.

What I most want you to understand is that none of this was inevitable. The people who will one day build the world I live in were once people like you, reading things like this one, deciding whether it was worth changing their habits. Some of them decided it was. They started small: taking an afternoon off without apologizing, refusing to rank a stranger by income, asking the sick what they needed instead of what they could afford.

So here is my request. When someone tells you that exhaustion is virtue, or that a person's value is their output, or that a cage is simply how things are done, please remember that your descendants are watching what you choose to accept. We are not better than you because we are wiser. We are better because someone before us decided that "that's just how it is" was not an answer.

Sleep well, if you can.

*—written in the margin of a ruined city, by someone who hopes you are still reading.*
