# The Defendant

The last thing I remember about being alive is the bus.

It was the 4:10 out of St. Agnes, the one that smells like wet umbrellas, and I'd just come off a double in pediatrics. My head had been aching since noon. The old man in the seat beside me, cardigan and wool cap and the kind of hands that had done real work, offered me a lemon drop from a crinkled bag. I said thank you. Then the ache turned into something white and enormous behind my left eye, and the world folded in half.

He held my hand. I remember that. A stranger on a bus held my hand and said, *It's all right, it's all right, I've got you,* and he said it like he meant something larger than the bus.

Then I was standing in a hall.

---

I'd always imagined judgment as a line. A gate, a book, a bearded man checking names. This was more like a coliseum built by someone who had heard of architecture but never seen it. Tiers rose upward farther than my eyes could follow, packed with the dead. Not just human dead. I saw beings made of slow-turning glass, things like jellyfish that pulsed in colors I didn't have names for, a whole section of what looked like sentient moss.

A small, bored figure in gray appeared at my elbow. It had a clipboard. I found that weirdly comforting.

"First time," it said. It wasn't a question. "You're not on trial. Relax."

"Then what am I doing here?"

"Jury." It gestured at the floor of the arena, far below, where a single raised platform stood under a column of light. "Every age, the gods answer for their worlds. Fairness. Kindness. Whether it was worth living in. The dead decide. Who else would know?"

I opened my mouth. Nothing came out.

"Your section's over there," it said, and was gone.

---

I watched three trials before ours.

The first god was beautiful in a way that hurt to look at, a tower of gold and soft music. The Assessor, a vast shape at the arena's head that seemed made mostly of patience, read out its record: a world with no hunger, no disease, no death before ten thousand years, and then only a gentle fading by choice. Its dead rose in their section and sang. I've never heard anything like it. The verdict came down like a blessing: *Well made.*

The second god had built a world where its creatures suffered on purpose, because it enjoyed watching. I won't describe what the Assessor read. When the time came for its dead to speak, they didn't. They just stood, every one of them, and turned their backs. The god screamed as the light took it apart. Nobody in the hall looked away.

The third was middling. A world of decent weather and boring politics. *Adequate,* said the Assessor, and the god looked relieved.

Then the Assessor said, "The god of the third planet of the yellow star at the edge of the Orion Spur," and every human in my section went quiet.

---

I had expected thunder. I think we all had. I'd expected an old man with a beard, or a blinding wheel of eyes, or at least something tall.

What stepped into the light was a man in a cardigan.

Not tall. Not glowing. Gray stubble, wool cap held in both hands like he was at a funeral. He looked tired in the specific way that I knew from the faces of residents at four in the morning: not sleepy, but *used.*

I knew him. I'd watched him peel open a bag of lemon drops twenty minutes ago, or a hundred years ago, or however time worked here.

The Assessor began to read.

It read the plagues first. The Black Death. Smallpox. Cholera in the slums of a thousand cities. It read earthquakes and the names of the towns they swallowed. It read famines. It read the wars, and that took a long time, and the human section shifted and murmured, because we'd all known, but we had never heard it read *all at once.*

Then it read the small things. That was worse. A boy born with a heart that didn't close. A woman whose mind went out like a candle at fifty-two. A girl named Priya, seven years old, room 4B, who'd asked me on her last night whether it was going to hurt, and I had lied to her.

I had not known I was crying until I tasted it.

The god in the cardigan didn't argue. He didn't explain free will, or mysterious ways, or the necessity of contrast. He just stood there and listened to every word, and I watched his lips move, and realized he was saying the names along with the Assessor. He knew them. He knew all of them.

"The dead of this world will choose a speaker," said the Assessor.

There was a pause. Then, gently, the gray figure with the clipboard was at my elbow again.

"You were the most recent," it said, apologetic. "That's the rule."

---

The floor of the arena was cold under bare feet. Up close, he smelled like lemon drops and rain.

"Speaker," said the Assessor. "Was this world fair?"

I thought about Priya. I thought about the mothers in the waiting room, the ones who did everything right. I thought about the man who'd beaten his son into my ER and walked out without a scratch.

"No," I said. "It wasn't fair. Not even close."

"Was it pleasant?"

I almost said no again. But I thought about the lemon drop. I thought about Priya's laugh when I did the voice for her stuffed giraffe. I thought about dawn over the parking garage after a night when we saved someone.

"Sometimes," I said. "Not enough."

The Assessor considered this. Then it read one more line, and its voice was different.

"Prior sentence, from the last age: to dwell among its own creatures, as one of them, for a single lifespan. Sentence served." A pause. "Defendant requested extension. Granted. Defendant requested again. Granted." It kept going. "And again."

I looked at him. "How many times?"

He turned the cap over in his hands. "I lost count after the Bronze Age," he said. His voice was soft and hoarse. "I was a midwife in Ur. A ditch digger in Kyoto. I was in a cattle car once." He swallowed. "I was on a bus today."

"You were *punished* by living with us."

"That was the first time." He finally looked up at me, and his eyes were the oldest things I'd ever seen. "After that, it was the only way I could think of to learn what I'd done. You can't fix what you only see from above. I'm not good at this. I know I'm not good at this. But I'm trying to get better, and I can't do it from up there."

The hall was silent. A trillion dead, from a thousand worlds, waiting.

"Speaker," said the Assessor. "What sentence do the dead of Earth recommend?"

I thought I'd been ready to condemn him. I'd had a lifetime of reasons. I still had them.

But he'd held my hand.

"Guilty," I said. Behind me, the human section murmured. I didn't turn around. "It wasn't fair, and it should have been, and he knows it. So send him back. Let him keep learning."

The god in the cardigan closed his eyes. Something in his shoulders let go.

"And this time," I added, "he works nights in pediatrics. Somebody's got to cover my shift."

For the first time, the god of Earth smiled.

"Room 4B," he said quietly. "I'll take it."
