**The Apartment on Delmar Street**

The first rule, according to Five, was that nobody could touch the cereal without him.

"It's *my* cereal," he said, standing on a kitchen chair, pointing a spoon at the other two like a tiny judge.

"It's literally all of our cereal," said Fifteen, not looking up from his phone. "We share a bank account."

"What's a bank?"

"Where Twenty-Five keeps the money she's always stressed about."

Twenty-Five came in from the hallway holding a laundry basket and a coffee, wearing the lanyard she hadn't taken off since yesterday. "I'm not stressed. I'm *budgeting*."

"You've been budgeting at the ceiling for three hours," said Fifteen.

She didn't argue. She set the basket down and looked at the two of them, and something in her chest did a complicated thing.

Fifteen was all elbows and hoodie, hair deliberately in his eyes. She remembered that hair. She remembered the ten-minute mirror ritual to get it just wrong enough. He had a way of slouching against doorframes as though gravity were an opinion he was entertaining. He was also, she knew, awake until 2 a.m. most nights, convinced everyone else had received a manual for being a person.

Five was missing a front tooth. He'd announced it proudly at breakfast, wiggling the gap. He still believed that adults knew things.

"Okay," she said. "House meeting."

"Ugh," said Fifteen.

"Yay!" said Five, who didn't know what it was.

They sat on the couch, Five in the middle, feet sticking straight out. Twenty-Five took the armchair, because someone had to be across from them.

"Here's the thing," she began. "I've got a job interview Thursday. I need the apartment quiet, and I need—"

"Do you want the job?" Fifteen asked.

She paused. "It's a good job."

"That's not what I asked."

Five looked between them. "Do you want the job?"

"It pays well. It's stable. It's—" She heard herself, the rehearsed rhythm of it, and stopped. "I don't know."

Fifteen finally put his phone down. "You used to want to make stuff. Like, actual stuff. You had that notebook."

"The notebook was a phase."

"It was *three years*."

"I have *drawn* a dinosaur," Five offered.

"It was a good dinosaur," Twenty-Five said automatically, because it had been. She'd found it last month in a box, a green stegosaurus with too many spikes and a sun in the corner wearing sunglasses. She'd cried a little, standing in the closet, and told herself it was allergies.

Fifteen drew his knees up. "Can I say something and you don't make it weird?"

"I'll try."

"I thought by now you'd have figured it out. Like, I thought twenty-five meant you knew."

The room went quiet. Five, sensing weather, leaned on Fifteen's arm.

"I don't," Twenty-Five said. "I really don't. I just got better at looking like I do."

"That's so *bad*," said Fifteen, but he was almost smiling.

"I know."

"Is that what being big is?" Five asked. "Pretending?"

"Sometimes," she said. "And sometimes it's making dinner and paying the electric bill and still being scared. You do it anyway."

"Like swimming without the floaties," said Five solemnly.

"Exactly like that."

Fifteen stared at the ceiling. "For the record, you're doing okay. The apartment's nice. You didn't give up the cat thing." He paused. "You still sleep with the hallway light on, though."

"Don't."

"I'm just saying, I noticed."

"*I* like the light," said Five.

She laughed, and it came out wet. Fifteen rolled his eyes and shoved a pillow at her, which was how he said *it's fine*. She'd forgotten that was his language.

"Okay," she said, wiping her face. "New plan. I'll go to the interview, because I should. But Saturday, we get a sketchbook. Real paper. Nobody's allowed to judge it."

"Not even me?" said Fifteen.

"*Especially* you."

"Fine."

"Can I draw the dinosaur again?"

"You can draw the dinosaur."

Five bounced off the couch and made for the kitchen. "Now can we have cereal?"

"Yes," she said.

"Together," said Five, as if it were law.

They stood in the small bright kitchen, three bowls, one box, a carton of milk with two days left. Fifteen poured too much. Five spilled some. Twenty-Five let both slide.

Outside, the city kept on with its business. Inside, nobody had it figured out, and for one morning that felt like enough.
