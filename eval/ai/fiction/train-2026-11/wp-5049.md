# The Kitchen

The card says LANTERN.

Odile Marchetti reads it once, folds it in half, and hands it back to the official, a nervous young woman with a dampener collar around her throat so she can't accidentally broadcast what she's carrying. The card goes into a lead-lined envelope. The envelope goes into a box. The box goes under the table between the two players' chairs, where it will stay until somebody cracks.

Across the table, Teo Valdez is already gone.

Not physically. He's nineteen and slouched in his chair with his hood up, but behind his eyes there's nothing at all. The commentators call it *the Blank*. Teo is the best Blank anyone has ever seen. In the semifinal, forty-two thousand readers spent twenty-three minutes searching his mind and came back with nothing but a sensation one fan described on the broadcast as "like licking a window in winter."

Odile is forty-one. She's the oldest player left in professional Vault by eleven years, and she's never once gone blank.

"Players ready," says the referee.

Teo nods without moving his face. Odile smiles at the referee, because she was raised to smile at people who are just doing their jobs.

"Readers," the referee says, and her amplified voice rolls around the stadium. "You may begin."

---

The rules of Vault fit on an index card, which is part of why it took over the world so fast.

Two players. One secret word each. A stadium full of readers, ordinary people with ordinary, middling telepathy, the kind that showed up in about a third of the population during the Bloom. Most of them can't read much on their own. A surface feeling, a color, the shape of a nearby thought. Forty thousand of them together are something else.

Each reader holds a little handset. When they think they've found the word, they type it in. If thirty percent of the stadium submits the correct word, the player is cracked and the round is over.

Last vault standing wins. That's the whole game.

Strategies came fast in the early years. Noise players fill their heads with songs, multiplication tables, and the alphabet backward in four languages, hoping the word drowns. Labyrinth players build false words, decoys and mirrors, a hundred LANTERNs that are really LATTICE and LANTANA and LANTERN-JAWED. Blanks like Teo simply aren't home. They empty out until the readers find only weather.

Then there's Odile, who in twenty years of competition has never used any of them. The press calls her technique *the Kitchen*. She has never once explained it.

---

At minute two, Odile feels them arrive.

It's always like this, a pressure at the back of the skull, like when someone is standing behind you in line too close. Forty-two thousand people reaching at once. The new players hate it. Some of them throw up in the first round. Odile has always thought it felt a little like being hugged by a crowd.

She lets them in, and she takes them to the kitchen.

It's her grandmother's kitchen in Trieste, the summer she was seven. Afternoon, a strip of sun across the tile. There's a pot of beans going on the back burner, and that smell, bay leaf and pork fat and something faintly burnt because Nonna always let the bottom catch a little. The radio is playing a station from across the border, a man singing in Slovenian about something sad. There's a cat named Ugo asleep in the fruit bowl, which is not allowed. The cat is in the fruit bowl anyway.

Odile shows them all of it. The chip in the blue enamel of the sink. The crossword on the table with *Nonna's* handwriting, which is all capitals. The weight of the wooden spoon when she was seven and allowed to stir.

She does not hide the word. That's the thing nobody has ever figured out.

The lantern is right there. It hangs over the kitchen table, a brass oil lantern her grandfather brought home from the war and never once lit because Nonna said it would make the ceiling black. It's the brightest object in the room. If you looked up, you'd see it. Anyone could.

On the big screen, Teo's crack meter reads 2%. Hers reads 0.4%.

---

At minute nine, Teo's meter jumps to 11%.

Odile glances over. He's still slouched and his face is still empty, but his left hand has started tapping on the arm of his chair. Tap-tap. Tap-tap. A Blank shouldn't tap. A Blank shouldn't have anything to tap about.

The commentators have noticed. Even through the soundproof glass, she can see the booth leaning forward.

Odile knows what's happening, because she's watched it happen to every great Blank in history. Blank isn't nothing. Blank is a wall, and you have to hold a wall up. You hold it up with fear. Fear of being seen, of being found, of forty thousand strangers walking around in the one place that is supposed to be yours. The longer the round goes, the heavier the wall gets. Then the readers stop looking at the wall and start looking at what's holding it up.

13%. 17%.

In the kitchen, Ugo the cat wakes up and stretches and goes back to sleep. Someone in the stadium laughs out loud. Odile hears it through the glass, one bright bark of a laugh, and she feels them settle deeper. They've stopped searching. They're just there. Someone is reading the crossword. Someone else is standing by the stove, breathing in.

Her meter: 1.1%.

---

At minute fourteen, Teo cracks.

It goes fast once it goes, from 22% to 31% in four seconds, the sound of forty thousand handsets clicking like rain on a tin roof. The board flashes red. The official pulls his envelope from the box and reads it aloud: "HARBOR." The stadium erupts, half cheering, half groaning.

Teo doesn't move. Then he pulls his hood further down, and his shoulders shake once, and Odile has to look away because it isn't hers to see.

But the round isn't over. In championship Vault, the survivor still has to finish the clock. Six more minutes. If the readers crack her before time runs out, it goes to sudden death.

They're all on her now. Every mind in the building. Forty-two thousand people in a seven-year-old's kitchen in Trieste.

Somewhere in there, she can feel them looking up.

Of course they are. It's the brightest thing in the room. Brass, catching the sun, never lit. She feels the recognition ripple through them, *oh, there it is, it's right there.* She feels a thousand thumbs hovering over a thousand handsets.

Her meter reads 1.3%.

Then 1.3%.

Then 1.2%.

Because nobody wants to press it. Pressing it ends the round, and ending the round means leaving. Back to the plastic seats and the overpriced beer and the parking lot traffic and whatever they came here to forget for an afternoon. Here, the beans are almost done. Nonna's going to come back in a minute. The man on the radio is still singing his sad song, and the sun is still on the tile, and the cat is still in the fruit bowl.

The clock runs out at 1.4%.

---

Afterward, in the tunnel, Teo is sitting on an equipment crate with his hood down. His eyes are red. He looks about twelve.

"How," he says. Not angry. Just asking.

Odile sits next to him. She's tired the way she's always tired after a match, as if she's hosted a very long dinner party.

"You keep them out," she says. "I just give them somewhere better to be."

"That's not a strategy."

"No," she agrees. "It's a kitchen."

He's quiet for a long time. Then: "Was it real? The lantern?"

"It's in my apartment now. I still haven't lit it." She stands, knees cracking. "Come by sometime. My beans are worse than hers, but you can see it."
