The clock read 00:00 when Marta picked up the pencil, the way it always did now. Three months of unpaid bills sat in a stack by the door, and the landlord's last note was still taped to the fridge: *Friday.*

She had found the trick by accident in August. A sketch of a lemon, drawn in the dark, had finished itself at 00:24 with a soft pop, and there it was on the windowsill: a real lemon, cool and faintly fragrant, sitting next to a cup of cold tea. She had stared at it for an hour before she touched it.

Since then, she had made a few things. A tin kettle. A pair of reading glasses that fit perfectly. A small brass bird that sang when she tapped its beak. Each one was a little wrong in a way she liked, a faint asymmetry, a line that wandered where she hadn't meant it to. She sold none of them. Selling felt like lying.

Tonight she drew a window.

It was not a grand idea. She just wanted one that opened onto something other than her own four walls. She worked fast, the pencil scratching in the quiet, and at 00:24 the sketchbook gave its familiar pop.

The window was there, set into the wall beside her bed, its frame painted a faded green. Through the glass was not the alley behind her building. It was a meadow, gold in the late light, with a river curling through it and a single tree leaning over the water.

Marta stood up very slowly. Her knees ached. She pressed her palm to the glass, and it was warm.

She could hear birds.

For a long moment she simply breathed. Then, without thinking, she reached for the sketchbook again, flipped to a clean page, and started to draw a door.

Her hand was shaking. At 00:00 she began, and at 00:24 the door appeared beside the window, its brass handle cold under her fingers when she turned it. Beyond it was a narrow wooden staircase leading down into the light.

She sat on the top step with the sketchbook in her lap and laughed until she cried.

On Thursday morning the landlord knocked. Marta opened the door with paint on her cheek and a sheaf of drawings under her arm: a bowl of plums, a stack of books, a chair with a sensible back.

"Friday," he said, not unkindly.

"I know," she said. "Come in. I want to show you something."

He stepped inside, and his eyes went immediately to the window, then the door, then the meadow beyond the glass. He opened his mouth and closed it again.

"Sit down," Marta said, and for the first time in months she felt the floor steady beneath her. "I think we need to talk about what you're going to accept as rent."

He sat. Outside, the birds kept singing.
