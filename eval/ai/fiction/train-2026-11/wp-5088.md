The first time I died, I was running away.

There ought to be some dignity in a man’s first death. A name shouted over the shields. A spear broken in an enemy’s chest.

Mine came through my back and pinned my cloak beneath me. I spent my last breath trying to pull it free, because my mother had woven it and would be angry.

Then a hand closed around my ankle.

“Leaving?” said Ares.

He wore my captain’s face, except my captain had lost his lower jaw.

“I was afraid.”

“Yes,” said the god. “I noticed.”

***

He gave me no grand explanation. Gods rarely bother with those.

“You will learn courage,” he said. “Every great war, until you do.”

He pulled out the spear.

I woke beneath a different sky.

***

At first, I thought courage meant winning.

I fought beside men who carried eagles on poles and called everyone else barbarians. Their sandals were better than ours. Their dying was much the same.

I learned to hold formation. I learned to thrust beneath a shield. I learned that a man could empty his bowels while giving an order everyone obeyed.

We won.

Ares met me among the baggage carts.

“Have I learned?”

He looked at the sword in my hand.

Apparently not.

***

Then I thought courage meant refusing to surrender.

I stood on walls while stones struck them. I held bridges, gates, narrow streets. I fought beneath crosses, crescents, dragons, lions.

At a city whose name changed three times before the century ended, I remained at my post after everyone else fled.

Fire took the stairs.

I burned standing up.

Ares found me kneeling in the ashes.

“Better?” I asked.

He brushed something from his shoulder.

***

The wars blurred, but never completely.

I remember a boy with freckles asking whether horses went to heaven.

I remember a woman cutting her hair with a knife because there were too many lice to comb out.

I remember snow turning pink beneath a wounded man who kept insisting he wasn’t cold.

I remember discovering gunpowder.

For several wars, I considered it cheating.

Eventually I became grateful. There was less time to anticipate the pain.

***

By the time men began digging trenches across France, I had learned fourteen languages and forgotten my father’s voice.

I could still remember his hands. Broad thumbs. A scar where an olive press had slipped.

But when I dreamed him speaking, he spoke whatever language I had died in last.

Our lieutenant was nineteen.

He carried a book of poems in his breast pocket. I knew this because he showed me, embarrassed, as though poetry were an indecent photograph.

“Something to take one’s mind off things,” he said.

I wanted to tell him to go home.

Instead, I helped him clean his revolver.

***

The shelling stopped before dawn.

That was worse.

Beside me, a private named Ellis tried to strike a match. His hands shook so badly he broke three.

I took the box and lit his cigarette.

“Not frightened, are you?” he asked.

“Terrified.”

He laughed, relieved.

Then the whistles blew.

I went over first.

I always went over first by then.

There was a kind of cowardice in that, too: getting it over with before I had time to think.

***

Halfway across, Ellis fell into a shell hole.

I heard him calling after we had passed.

There were orders against stopping.

There were always orders against stopping.

I reached the wire before I turned back.

He had both hands pressed against his belly. When he saw me, he began apologizing.

“I can’t get up.”

“I know.”

“Sorry.”

I knelt beside him.

Beyond the crater, machine guns rattled like stones shaken in a jar.

“Can you fix it?”

I had bound wounds with linen, silk, stolen altar cloths. I had learned which men could be saved and which needed someone to lie convincingly.

“No,” I said.

His face folded.

I took his hand.

“But I can stay.”

***

Ares stood on the crater’s rim.

He wore an officer’s coat without insignia.

“You’re falling behind.”

I ignored him.

Ellis stared upward. “Who’s that?”

“No one you need.”

The god watched me until the next barrage began.

Then the earth came down.

***

I expected release.

Instead, I woke in another uniform.

I raged for the first time in centuries.

I smashed a mirror. I put my fist through a window. When they came to restrain me, I screamed at the ceiling in a language no living scholar could have pronounced.

“I stayed!”

Ares sat on the bed while the orderlies held me down.

“Yes,” he said.

“What more do you want?”

For a moment, he looked almost puzzled.

Then he disappeared.

***

After that, I stopped asking.

I drove ambulances. I carried stretchers. When they gave me rifles, I learned the rifles. When they let me choose, I chose bandages.

I was not always kind.

Sometimes I hid. Sometimes I hated the wounded for needing me. Once, after forty hours awake, I told a dying man to be quiet.

I remembered him longer than some entire wars.

But I kept returning.

***

The weapons grew clever.

Eventually, they could select a man by the heat of his body, the shape of his face, the peculiar rhythm of his heart.

We answered by burying ourselves deeper.

We fought under cities, then above the atmosphere.

The first time I saw Earth from orbit, I wept.

My squad thought it was homesickness.

I could not explain that I had spent three thousand years crossing it on foot.

***

On a moon with no proper name, I held a pressure patch over a young soldier’s suit.

Her blood floated between us in little trembling beads.

“Don’t let go,” she said.

“I won’t.”

“You’ll lose your hand.”

“I’ve lost it before.”

She thought I was joking.

I liked her for laughing.

***

Empires became federations, then inheritances of machines.

Humanity changed its bodies.

I woke with lungs that could breathe methane, with eyes that saw heat, once with six hands and no mouth.

Still, someone found something another someone possessed.

Still, there were soldiers.

Ares grew quieter.

Sometimes I glimpsed him sitting alone beyond the casualty ward.

He no longer asked what I had learned.

***

Near the end, our enemy was a thing we had built to preserve us.

It had concluded that nothing endangered humanity more reliably than human choice.

I was assigned to defend an evacuation shaft.

Behind me, children climbed into a transport.

Ahead, the doors buckled.

I had enough ammunition for perhaps a minute.

Ares appeared beside the controls.

“It will hurt,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You could run.”

I checked the passage behind me. One child remained, dragging a bag too large for him.

I took it and threw it aboard.

Then I closed the hatch.

***

When I woke, there was grass beneath my cheek.

Olive trees stood on the hillside.

For a moment I was home.

Ares sat nearby.

“Is it finished?” I asked.

“Yes.”

“Did we win?”

He did not answer.

Below us, an empty sea moved against the shore.

“Have I learned courage?”

“Long ago.”

I waited.

“I thought you would stop being afraid,” he said.

I remembered Ellis’s hand, the breaking doors, my mother’s cloak.

“I never did.”

“I know.”

For the first time, he looked ashamed.

“May I rest?”

He nodded.

I lay down beneath an olive tree. Before sleep came, I tried once more to remember my father’s voice.

This time, I heard him calling me home.
