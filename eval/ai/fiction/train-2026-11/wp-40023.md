“You’re standing in my spot,” said Mara.

Elias looked down at the yellow line between his boots. “It’s a highway.”

“Yes. Two lanes. Practically limitless opportunities for standing elsewhere.”

He moved three feet to the left.

“Thank you.”

Beyond the guardrail, the desert lay pale beneath the stars. A crushed soda can rattled across the asphalt, stopped against Elias’s shoe, then continued on when he lifted his foot.

“Do you think it hurts?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“You answered quickly.”

“I’ve been burned before.”

“Completely?”

Mara turned toward him.

“Sorry,” he said. “Stupid question.”

They waited.

Somewhere far away, a truck changed gears. The sound carried across the empty country, then faded.

“I imagined more traffic,” Elias said.

“That’s why I picked it.”

“I know. I just thought somebody might stop.”

“And do what?”

“Ask if we needed gas.”

Mara folded her arms. “Do you need gas?”

“No.”

“There. We’ve had the experience.”

He smiled despite himself. She looked away before he could decide whether to smile again.

After a while, he took something from his coat pocket.

“Would you like a mint?”

“No.”

“They’re old.”

“That hardly improves the offer.”

“I bought them in 1987.”

“Why?”

“I had a date.”

“With someone who appreciated archaeological confectionery?”

“They were new then.”

Mara glanced at the small metal tin.

“How did it go?”

“The date?”

“No, Elias. The mint.”

He rubbed his thumb over the faded lid.

“She married somebody else.”

“Good for her.”

“Yes,” he said. “It was, actually. He was kind. Ran a hardware store. They had three daughters.”

“You kept track.”

“Until I couldn’t.”

Mara watched the eastern horizon.

“I used to know every constellation,” she said.

“What happened?”

“Electricity.”

“That seems unfair to electricity.”

“Most things are.”

He put the tin away.

The sky had begun to loosen around the mountains. Nothing resembling daylight yet, only a suggestion that the dark was thinning.

Elias shifted his weight.

“Can I ask why?”

“You can.”

He waited.

Mara sighed.

“There was a bakery on the corner of my street. Before. Every morning my mother sent me for bread. I always tore a piece off on the way home.”

“Understandable.”

“She’d pretend she hadn’t noticed. Cut the loaf so the missing part faced the wall.”

A breeze lifted Mara’s hair.

“I can remember the street. The woman who sold the bread. The little bell. But I can’t remember my mother’s face.”

Elias said nothing.

“I had a portrait. Lost it in a fire in Lyon. For years I thought it didn’t matter. I knew her face.”

“I’m sorry.”

“I remember people I ate in railway stations. That seems particularly cruel.”

A bird called from beyond the guardrail.

“What about you?” she asked.

Elias scratched a flake of paint from the line beneath his shoe.

“I’m tired of moving.”

“That’s all?”

“It’s quite a lot.”

“Yes,” she said. “It is.”

“I unpack. I learn which stair creaks. Find a shop where they don’t ask questions. Then somebody notices I haven’t changed, and I leave.”

“You could live somewhere remote.”

“I tried. I got lonely.”

“You could live with others.”

“I tried that too.”

They stood quietly.

“Besides,” he added, “I’ve never been good at throwing things away.”

Mara looked at his coat pockets, sagging with small, persistent shapes.

“What else have you got in there?”

“Receipts.”

“Of course.”

“A key.”

“To what?”

“Don’t remember.”

He found a button, a bent photograph, two foreign coins, and a tiny blue mitten.

Mara stared at the mitten.

“Granddaughter,” he said.

“You had children?”

“Before.”

“How old was she?”

“When she lost this? Three.”

He fitted it over two fingers.

“She was furious. Insisted the other mitten would be lonely.”

“Was it?”

“Presumably. We never found it.”

Mara laughed.

The sound startled them both.

Elias put the mitten away carefully.

A thin orange seam appeared above the mountains.

“Well,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

Neither moved.

Then Elias asked, “Did you bring anything?”

“No.”

“Nothing?”

“I left everything in the car.”

He looked down the highway. Her car stood on the shoulder, a dark lump beneath the paling sky.

“Keys inside?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“Why good?”

“No reason.”

Mara closed her eyes. When she opened them, he was watching her.

“Don’t,” she said.

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You’re about to.”

“I was going to ask whether the bakery’s still there.”

“It’s been two hundred years.”

“Buildings last.”

“Elias.”

“What?”

She pressed her lips together.

“I don’t know.”

The orange seam widened.

He held out the mint tin.

This time, she took one.

“It tastes like a cupboard.”

“They’ve matured.”

She slipped it into her pocket.

“Your car,” he said. “Does it have a large trunk?”

“Large enough.”

“For two?”

She looked toward it, then back at him.

“It’ll be cramped.”

“I can manage.”

“We’ll still have the same problems tomorrow.”

“Yes.”

“And the next day.”

He nodded.

For a moment she looked almost angry. Then she caught his sleeve.

“Come on, then. Before I change my mind.”

They walked quickly at first, then ran, their shoes striking the deserted road.

At the car, Mara struggled with the latch while Elias kept his back toward the brightening horizon.

“Move your elbow,” she said when they were inside.

“Where?”

“Anywhere else.”

The lid slammed shut.

In the dark, Elias found her hand and let her decide whether to keep it.
