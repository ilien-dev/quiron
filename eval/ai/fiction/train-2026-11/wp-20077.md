**The Last Signal**

I still keep the headset in the drawer beside my bed. The foam has gone yellow and the left earcup cracks when I touch it, but I can't throw it out. Some mistakes you have to keep within arm's reach.

It was the winter of 1987, and I was nineteen, the youngest night operator the Harbor Rescue Station had ever hired. Old Marguerite trained me for three weeks. She had a voice like gravel in warm tea, and she told me the one thing she thought mattered. "You don't hang up first," she said. "Whatever happens, you stay on the line until the water takes it or the boat comes."

I said I understood. I didn't, not really.

The storm came in on a Tuesday. By midnight the radio room smelled of burnt coffee and wet wool, and the windows hummed against the wind. Marguerite had gone home sick that afternoon, which left me alone with the board, the logbook, and a heater that ticked like a dying clock.

At 1:14 a.m. the distress call came through.

"Mayday, mayday. This is the *Annalise*. We're taking on water."

A man's voice, steady in the way people are when they're working hard to be steady. I grabbed the pencil and wrote down everything: his name was Tomas, there were three aboard, his daughter and his brother-in-law and himself, and they were somewhere east of the breakwater, though he couldn't say how far. The fog had swallowed the lights.

"Stay with me," I said. "Tell me what you can see."

"Nothing. Gray. Wait." A pause, static, and then the thin, bright sound of a child asking a question. "My girl says she can hear the bell buoy."

I knew that buoy. There were two of them along that stretch, one at the north channel and one at the old quarry shoals. They rang at different pitches, and Marguerite had made me memorize both, drilling me until I could tell them apart in my sleep.

"What does it sound like?" I asked. "High or low?"

He held the microphone out. I heard the wind, the groaning of the hull, and under it a bell. Low, I thought. A slow, heavy note.

"Low," I told him. "Tomas, you're near the north channel. Steer west-northwest. You'll find the breakwater."

"West-northwest. Thank you. Thank you."

I dispatched the cutter with those coordinates. I remember feeling proud. I remember leaning back in the chair, thinking, *there, I did it,* and actually smiling in that empty room.

The bell I'd heard was the quarry shoals buoy, not the north channel. Over a bad connection, with the wind scraping the signal, the two notes had blurred together. Marguerite would have caught it. She would have asked him to hold the microphone out again, and again after that, until she was certain. I didn't ask a second time, because I wanted to be right.

The cutter searched the north channel for two hours and found nothing but chop and fog. By the time they thought to widen the search, the *Annalise* had already driven west-northwest, exactly as I had told him to, straight onto the shoals.

I stayed on the line. That much I did. Tomas's voice got fainter and the child's questions stopped, and I kept saying *I'm here, I'm still here,* long after the carrier tone had gone flat and there was only the hiss. I didn't hang up first. It was the one instruction I managed to follow.

Two of them were pulled out alive at dawn: the brother-in-law and the girl. Tomas wasn't.

Marguerite came to the station that morning, though she was still sick, and sat beside me while I filled in the logbook. She read my entry twice. She didn't say it wasn't my fault, and I've been grateful for that ever since, because I would have known she was lying. She said, "Now you know what the work is."

I worked the board for another thirty-one years. I asked every caller to repeat themselves. I made them hold the microphone out three times, four, five. I was known for it, a little famous for being slow and careful.

Last spring a woman wrote to me. She was grown now, with a daughter of her own, and she'd found my name in an old report. She didn't write to forgive me or to blame me. She only wanted to say that she remembered a voice on the radio that kept saying *I'm here.*

I haven't answered yet. I keep the letter with the headset, and some nights I take them both out and sit with them a while.
