**The Last Speaker**

The morning it happened, the whole world woke up arguing with itself.

I lay in bed listening to the neighbors through the wall. Mrs. Okafor was shouting in something guttural and rolling, and her husband answered in a language that sounded like birdsong. On the street, someone wept in Sumerian. Someone else was reciting Linear B with perfect confidence, though no one had heard it spoken in three thousand years.

My phone buzzed with a news alert, and I read it without thinking. Then I realized the alert was in English, and I had read it in English, and *also* it had been written in a script I had never seen, flowing underneath the English like a second current.

I sat up.

Everyone, the broadcasters said, had received one language. Random, unrequested, complete. A banker in Osaka woke up fluent in Old Norse. A toddler in Lagos was cooing in Etruscan. Linguists wept openly on live television, cradling microphones, begging strangers to say anything, anything at all.

I tried to see which one was mine.

"Hello," I said to the empty room.

The word came out wrong. It came out *right*, but not English. The air shivered. The glass on my nightstand rang like a struck bell, and the water inside it rose in a slow, curious column and hung there, trembling, waiting.

I did not breathe.

"Down," I whispered, and the word was a different word, older, shaped like a hand pressing on a lid. The water fell. It splashed across the nightstand and soaked my alarm clock.

I put my hand over my mouth.

---

By noon the internet had sorted itself into a carnival. People with Akkadian were suddenly very popular. A woman with Gothic was offered a book deal. Someone with Proto-Indo-European, the reconstructed ancestor of half the world's tongues, gave a tearful interview in which they said *I think I understand what the first people were afraid of.*

I kept my mouth shut.

I took the bus downtown, and sat with my hands tucked under my thighs, because I had discovered that my language did not need a speaker's *intent*, only a speaker's *attention*. The bus driver's radio crackled. A man's coffee began to steam harder. Every thought I formed had a word waiting for it, and every word had weight, and I had to be careful about which thoughts I let finish.

I found the university library nearly deserted. Everyone was out in the streets, trading words like contraband. I climbed to the fourth floor, where the dead languages lived, and pulled down a book of undeciphered inscriptions, the kind scholars had argued over for centuries.

I could read all of it.

Not the inscriptions. Those were something else. But in the margins, where the earlier scholars had pencilled their frustrated guesses, I saw the old script I had woken up knowing, rising through the paper like a watermark. And I realized I'd been wrong about what I'd been given.

It was not a language that *described* things.

It was a language that *asked* them. Politely, once, and very precisely. The world used to listen, because the world had been listening for a long time before we arrived. The old speakers had not been wizards. They had been the only people who knew how to say *please* in a way that mattered.

My hands shook. I closed the book.

"You can read that?"

A girl stood at the end of the aisle, maybe nineteen, backpack hanging off one shoulder. She held a notebook against her chest.

"No," I said, in English, carefully. "I mean, no."

"Okay." She came closer anyway. "I got Hittite. Which is great, right, very useful, I've already been asked to translate four hundred clay tablets and a guy's tattoo." She paused. "But I felt something this morning, when the whole world woke up. Like a door opening. And then a different door, farther off. Like someone *else* got a bigger one."

I said nothing.

"The water glass in my kitchen climbed up the wall," she said quietly. "I didn't say anything. I just *thought* in Hittite, and it climbed."

"That's not Hittite doing that."

"I know." She looked at me. "That's the one you have. Isn't it?"

Very slowly, I nodded.

She let out a breath that shook a little at the end. "Okay. Okay. So what do we do?"

"I don't know. I haven't tried anything. Except putting a glass of water back down."

"Can you teach it?"

I considered that. "I don't think it's taught. I think it's *remembered*. Everyone woke up with a language. Maybe mine just remembers a little louder."

---

We walked out onto the quad. A crowd had gathered around a man speaking Latin to a pigeon. The pigeon, to everyone's delight, was listening.

"There will be others," the girl said. "People like us. Whatever the others got, someone got this."

"And someone will use it badly."

"Probably." She shrugged, a small helpless gesture. "People did before. It's why it got lost, right? Somebody asked for the wrong thing."

I looked up at the sky, huge and gray and patient. Somewhere, I could feel it, the way you feel a person standing behind you in a quiet room. The world was awake, and paying attention, and waiting to see what we would say.

I had been given the oldest language there was. The one that moved water and bent light and made things *obey*.

And what I understood, standing there with a stranger and a thousand murmuring voices, was that the old speakers had not lost it because it was dangerous.

They had lost it because it was *polite*, and the world had gotten tired of being asked.

I took a breath. The girl watched me. The pigeon, on its pedestal, turned its head.

I spoke one word. Quietly. The way you'd knock on a door you hoped was open.

The clouds parted, just a little, just enough for a line of light to fall across the quad. And in a hundred languages, all at once, the crowd gasped.

Then, very faintly, from somewhere beyond the sky, the world answered.

It said: *Finally.*
