# Priority One

Nobody in the Eastside Mutual Aid Collective called themselves a gang. Dana Okafor said it once, in the back of Medic 9, and Teo Brandt laughed so hard he had to sit on the bumper.

"We're a crew," Teo said. "Gangs have colors. We have shift schedules."

That was true. Six people covered every ambulance, engine, and dispatch console within eleven blocks of the river. Captain Marisol Reyes ran the engine house on Calloway Street. Teo drove. Priya Nair worked dispatch nights and heard everything on the radio before the city finished saying it. Old Hollis Vance, a retired ER nurse, stitched people up in the basement of a laundromat with no questions and no records. Lucas Ferreira and Jo Kimura ran the rescue truck and could open a door faster than a locksmith could find his keys.

Dana had been a paramedic for four years and a crew member for three months. She kept the books.

The money came the way it comes to people who are first on scene: a shooting on Harlan Avenue, a car fire under the overpass, a man who'd fallen down the stairs of a check-cashing place with a bag of cash still under his arm. The crew always arrived first. Priya made sure of that. The rules lived on a laminated card in every truck:

*One. Treat everyone the same. Two. Never leave anyone behind. Three. Never lie on a run report. Four. Whatever you take off a scene goes to someone who needs it more.*

Rule four paid the bills. There was a standing list: a dialysis clinic that ran short every month, a shelter that needed insulin in the fridge, a girl named Sofia whose mother worked two jobs and couldn't afford the inhaler that kept her out of the ER. Dana had handed that mother the envelope. The woman had cried and asked, "Who are you people?" Dana had said, "Just paramedics," which was almost true.

The trouble came on a Tuesday, a little after three in the morning.

Priya's voice crackled in Dana's ear. "Medic 9, Dunmore and 14th. Shots fired, multiple victims. Police are four minutes out."

Four minutes. Dana looked at Teo. Teo looked at the bag of cash under the seat, the one they'd been meaning to drop at the shelter since Friday.

"Go," Dana said.

They arrived in ninety seconds. A boy, maybe seventeen, lay on the sidewalk with a wound in his thigh and both hands pressed flat over it, as if he were trying to hold himself together. A second kid sat against a dumpster, hyperventilating. A third was already gone.

Dana knelt. Tourniquet high and tight. Pressure. Count the breaths. Keep talking.

"Hey. I'm Dana. What's your name?"

"Marcus." His voice cracked. "Is my brother dead?"

Dana didn't look toward the dumpster. "I need you to stay with me, Marcus. Tell me about your brother."

Jo ran in with the second bag. Lucas brought the stretcher. Hollis's voice came over the radio from the laundromat, calm as ever. "If the hospital's forty minutes out, bring him here. I've got a table."

That was what outsiders never understood. They weren't heroes, and they weren't criminals in the way the news meant it. They were people who had stood in too many hallways where the wrong person died because the right person arrived late. Every dollar they took came from someone's worst night, and every one of them had made peace with that by deciding the money had to go somewhere that counted.

Police arrived at 3:04. By then Marcus was on the stretcher, conscious, furious, alive. The dead boy's brother was stabilized and riding to Mercy General under Teo's hands. The officers asked what happened. Dana gave them the report, every line accurate, nothing added. Teo gave them a nod. Four miles away, Priya muted her mic and let out a breath she'd held since the first call.

Back at the engine house, Marisol poured coffee into paper cups and read the run sheet twice.

"Shooting, two transported, one pronounced," she said. "Clean."

"Clean," Dana said.

"Good." Marisol set the bag of cash on the table between them. "Sofia's inhaler is first. Pharmacy opens at nine."

Dana looked at the bag and thought about the boy with his hands pressed against the wound, and about Marcus asking whether his brother was dead.

"We should add a line," she said. "Under rule four."

"What line?"

"The people who need it most aren't always the ones bleeding in front of us."

Marisol considered that for a long moment. Then she picked up a marker, uncapped it, and wrote beneath the rules in small, tidy letters: *Five. Nobody decides who's worth the trip.*

The radio on the wall clicked. Priya's voice came through, steady and unhurried.

"Medic 9, Harlan and Ninth. Possible overdose. Police are six minutes out."

Dana was already on her feet.
