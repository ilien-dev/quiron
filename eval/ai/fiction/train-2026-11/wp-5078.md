**The Tax on Bread**

The palace kitchens smelled of butter and cloves, and Wren had stopped noticing. Three years ago she had been Wren of Dunmere Lane, who sold eggs from a basket and slept with two sisters under a roof that leaked on the left side. Now she had a room with a window that didn't rattle, and a husband who said her name as though it were a gift.

Prince Aldric had found her at the spring market, where she was haggling over a cracked crock of lard. He had laughed at her nerve and paid the difference without being asked. She had thought it was the kindest thing anyone had ever done.

The first crack appeared in the autumn, during the Council of Grain.

She had gone down to the lower hall to deliver a basket of linens and had lingered by the doorway, where the clerks were reading out figures. The Crown's levy on flour had risen again. The Crown's levy on salt, on firewood, on the carts that carried either to market, had risen too. A clerk read a number and another clerk wrote it down, and nobody in the room looked up, because the number was simply what bread cost now.

Wren knew that number. She had lived inside it. Her mother had once walked four miles to save a penny on a loaf, and had died the winter after the levy on firewood went up.

"Your Highness," she said that night, carefully, "why does the Crown tax flour?"

Aldric was reading by the fire. He didn't look up at first. "To fund the garrisons. The roads. The treasury."

"And the treasury pays the garrisons, and the garrisons keep the roads, and the roads carry the flour that the Crown taxed on the way in." She heard her own voice sharpen and couldn't stop it. "So the family who grows the wheat pays the Crown, the family who grinds it pays the Crown, and the family who buys the loaf pays the Crown three times. Who pays the Crown back?"

He set the book down. For a moment his face was the face from the spring market, open and amused. "Wren. You're making a speech."

"I'm asking a question."

"It's a complicated system."

"It's a simple one," she said. "It just isn't built for us."

He laughed, and she realized, with a coldness that started in her stomach, that he thought she was being charming.

The following months were a slow education. She visited the lower quarter with a lady's maid's excuse and a purse of her own coins. She saw the children of Dunmere Lane, her old neighbors, thinner than they had been. She met a widow named Orla who had been fined for gleaning in a field that had belonged to the Crown since before the widow's grandmother was born. She read the old charters in the palace archive, the ones nobody bothered to lock, and found that every tax had a name, and every name had a royal seal beside it.

She brought the charters to Aldric. She brought the figures. She brought Orla.

He listened, and that was the worst part. He listened the way a man listens to weather. "These things are difficult," he said. "My father set these levies. Changing them would upset the treasury, the lords, the whole balance of the realm."

"Balance," Wren repeated. "Is that what you call it when a widow goes hungry so a lord can keep his hunting land?"

"You don't understand what it takes to rule."

"No," she said. "But I understand what it takes to survive it. And I think I understand what you're for."

That was the closest they came to a real fight. He didn't shout. He simply looked at her as though she had become someone he had to manage, and that look told her more than any speech could.

Wren did not leave the palace that winter. She had nowhere to go but back to a leaking roof, and her sisters had long since married and moved away. But she stopped sleeping beside him. She began sitting with the clerks in the lower hall, learning the figures, learning the names of the levies, learning which seals were stamped on which papers.

In spring, when the market opened, she walked down Dunmere Lane alone, past the crooked houses and the thin children, and bought a cracked crock of lard from a woman who knew her face. She paid full price.

Then she went back up the hill and began to make a list.
