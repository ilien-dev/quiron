# Mile Marker Nine Hundred Something

The sun had been trying to kill me since Barstow. My thumb was the only part of me still doing what I asked. Cars passed in gusts of hot air: a pickup full of sheep, a church van, a man in a Hawaiian shirt who looked at me like I was a rattlesnake and kept going.

I was twenty-six, I had forty-one dollars, and my grandmother was being buried in Tucson on Thursday. I'd slept in a bus station in Needles and missed the morning connection, and I'd promised my mother I'd make it. So I stood on the shoulder with a cardboard sign that said TUCSON and nothing else, because I'd run out of marker and didn't want to waste the last of it on a joke.

The car that stopped was black, long, and older than my mother. A 1970s Lincoln, I think, though the paint was so deep it looked wet. The window rolled down with a whine like a sigh.

"Get in," said the driver. "Before the heat decides for you."

He was handsome the way a good suit is handsome: tailored, a little too perfect, like someone had considered every crease. Gray at the temples. Eyes the color of a match just before it catches. He wore a white shirt with the sleeves rolled, and a small gold ring with a dark stone on one pinky.

I got in. I'm not proud of it. Forty-one dollars buys a lot of nothing in the desert.

"Tucson," he said. "Long way."

"Yeah."

"Funeral?"

I looked at him. "How'd you know?"

"Black shirt, nothing in your hands, the face of someone who's been rehearsing what to say." He pulled onto the highway without checking the mirror, which should have bothered me more than it did. "I'm the Devil, by the way. People usually ask that first."

I laughed, and it came out cracked. "Sure you are."

"Believe what you like. The car doesn't care." He tapped the dashboard. The radio clicked on, playing a steel guitar song I recognized from my grandmother's kitchen. "Go on. Ask me what it costs."

"I'm not asking."

"Everyone asks eventually. Usually around mile forty. You're doing better than most."

The desert slid past, orange and bleached. Joshua trees stood like people who had given up waiting. He wasn't driving fast, but the miles were disappearing faster than they should have.

"Here's the thing," he said, almost conversationally. "You're going to a funeral for a woman who taught you to make tamales and kept a jar of quarters for your birthday wishes. You haven't cried yet. You think if you cry, it makes it true."

My throat closed. I stared out the window.

"I could fix that," he said gently. "Not the death. I'm no miracle worker, whatever the pamphlets say. But I could make Thursday easier. Make the crying come when you want it. Make your mother stop looking at you like you're the one who should have called more."

"No."

"You haven't heard the price."

"I don't want to hear it."

He sighed, and the sound was surprisingly human. "Do you know how many people say that to me every year? Most of them say it after they've already said yes in their heads."

"I'm not them."

"No. You're the first this decade to refuse without asking whether the offer was real." He glanced at me sideways. "It's rude, but interesting."

A sign flicked by: TONOPAH 11. A dead dog lay on the shoulder, and the Devil slowed, just slightly, as if out of courtesy.

"Can I tell you something?" he said. "The job's changed. Used to be I'd spend centuries in taverns and at crossroads, shaking hands over ledgers. Now it's mostly spreadsheets. Everyone signs away their soul in a little pop-up window and nobody reads the terms. Nobody comes to the crossroads anymore. Do you know how lonely it is to sit at a crossroads with a clipboard?"

"That sounds like your problem."

"It is. Which is why I stopped for you." He smiled, and there was something almost sad in it. "I wanted to see if anyone would still pick up a stranger without wanting something back."

I thought about the bus driver in Needles who'd let me ride for half fare after I cried at the counter. I thought about the old woman who'd given me her last apple and said, "Honey, don't pay it forward. Just eat it."

"You're the one who wants something back," I said. "That's your whole thing."

"Oh, absolutely." He tapped the wheel. "From you, I mean. Not your soul. I'd like you to tell me what your grandmother's tamales tasted like. Everyone tells me about their sins. Nobody tells me about the food."

So I did. I told him about masa and lard and the red chile that stained her apron, about the way she hummed off-key while she pressed the dough, about the quarters. My voice shook on the quarters. He didn't interrupt. The car moved so smoothly I forgot to watch the road.

When I finished, we were pulling into a gas station outside Tucson, a sun-faded sign listing prices I didn't read. The clock on the dash said 4:12. It had been nearly noon when I got in.

"You lost a little time," he said. "Don't worry. You didn't lose anything that matters. Bit of a gift, really. You'll cry at the right moment now."

"What do I owe you?"

"Nothing." He looked genuinely surprised. "That's the first time anyone's asked me that without meaning to bargain."

I opened the door. The heat hit me, dry and immediate.

"Hey," I said. "Thanks. I mean it."

He nodded, and for a second I saw something behind his face, something old and tired and oddly kind. "Eat something," he said. "You look like you're about to fall apart, and I have enough paperwork."

The door closed. The Lincoln pulled away with the same whine it had made when it arrived, and by the time I looked up, the road was empty.

I found a payphone outside the station. They still have those in Arizona. I called my mother, and she picked up on the first ring.

"Where are you?" she asked.

"Almost there," I said. And for the first time in a week, I started to cry.
