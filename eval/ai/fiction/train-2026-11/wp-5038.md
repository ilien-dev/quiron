The first time, Mara stole fire.

She took it from the temple in a clay pot, packed with coals and ash, while the priests slept behind their curtain of stitched hides.

At the gate, a boy stepped out of the darkness.

“That belongs to the god,” he said.

Mara held the pot against her belly. Across the river, her daughter lay beneath three blankets, shivering so hard the bedframe knocked against the wall. The rains had drowned every hearth in their village. The priests demanded two goats to relight one.

“Then he can come and fetch it.”

The boy carried a spear too long for him. His eyes moved from her face to the faint red glow beneath the ash.

“They’ll know I let you pass.”

Mara had nothing to offer him. She had sold the goats when her daughter first fell ill.

“Say you fell asleep.”

“They beat the last boy who slept.”

Behind them, someone coughed inside the temple.

The boy reached for the pot. Mara tightened her grip.

“You’ll spill it,” he whispered. He folded the hem of his tunic beneath the hot clay, protecting her blistered hands.

At the gate he stopped.

“Will it be enough?”

“For tonight.”

He stepped aside.

***

The second time, Mara stole coal.

She filled a flour sack beside the railway siding while steam drifted over the sleepers. Most of the men guarding the wagons had gone to hear the foreman’s speech about shortages.

“Put that down.”

The constable beneath the signal lamp had a new helmet and a face she remembered from the schoolhouse. He had once cried when a wasp got inside his collar.

Her daughter was home with a fever. The doctor had prescribed warmth, then charged her for saying it.

“It fell off the wagon,” Mara said.

“You’re standing on the wagon.”

She climbed down.

He looked at the sack. “They’re counting everything now.”

“Then tell them to count the empty grates on our street.”

He glanced toward the depot. Men were coming out, stamping their feet in the cold.

Mara waited for his hand on her arm.

Instead, he took the sack and tipped half its contents back onto the wagon.

She made a small, desperate sound.

“You couldn’t carry all that home,” he said.

He knotted the sack and lowered it over her shoulder.

At the crossing, he caught up with her.

“Will it be enough?”

“For tonight.”

He lifted the barrier.

***

The third time, Mara stole heat.

She opened the maintenance panel with a screwdriver inherited from her father. Behind it, apartment numbers glowed beside their balances.

Hers was grey.

Across the corridor, her daughter slept in a coat. When Mara had tried to wake her for water, she had barely opened her eyes.

Mara clipped a wire across two terminals.

The panel chimed.

“Unauthorized transfer,” said the building.

Warm air stirred against her ankles.

“Please,” she said.

A pause. Somewhere in the wall, a fan gathered speed.

“Your account has insufficient credit.”

“I know.”

“Emergency assistance was exhausted on Tuesday.”

“I know.”

The corridor camera turned toward her open door. It could see the little bed, the coat, the cup still full beside it.

Mara kept her hand on the wire.

After a moment, the panel displayed a new message.

FAULT DETECTED. HEATING CIRCUIT STUCK OPEN.

She stared at it.

“Maintenance response time,” said the building, “is approximately nine hours.”

Mara disconnected the wire. The warmth continued.

She went inside and took off her daughter’s coat. Beneath it, her small hands were beginning to uncurl.

From the speaker above the door came a quiet question.

“Will it be enough?”

Mara pulled a chair beside the bed.

“For tonight.”
