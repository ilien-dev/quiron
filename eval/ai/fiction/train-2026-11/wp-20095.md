At 6:17 a.m., the dead man’s watch began to scream.

I slapped it silent.

My father had worn the thing for twenty-three years, through three factory closures and one marriage. He’d left it to me with a scratched crystal, a failing battery, and an alarm I still didn’t know how to turn off permanently.

Every weekday, it gave me thirteen minutes before the phone joined in.

I lay still beside my wife, who had claimed most of the blanket overnight. Only her nose remained visible. Somewhere beneath the bed, our cat made a small, wet sound.

“No,” I whispered.

The cat made it again, more persuasively.

I moved with a speed that would have astonished my high-school gym teacher. My feet hit the floor. My hand found yesterday’s shirt. I slid it beneath the cat just as he deposited something resembling a thumb in a fur coat.

He stepped over it and went to breakfast.

In the kitchen, the coffee machine displayed DESCALE.

It had been displaying DESCALE since August. Each morning I pressed BREW, and each morning it agreed to postpone the conversation.

While it coughed into the pot, I opened the refrigerator.

A small bowl covered with foil occupied the middle shelf. We had both forgotten what was in it, but neither of us was prepared to find out before work. I reached around it for the milk.

There was enough for one cup.

I poured half.

Behind me, my daughter appeared in the doorway wearing pajamas and one pink mitten.

“Is today a school day?”

“Yes.”

Her face tightened. She was six and had begun to suspect that the world was poorly managed.

“I already went.”

“On Friday.”

“For the whole day.”

“I remember.”

She climbed onto a chair and laid the mitten beside her bowl. I gave her cereal. She ate three pieces, then asked whether worms had birthdays.

I considered this while locating her other mitten inside a rain boot.

“Probably.”

“Who brings the cake?”

“Other worms.”

She nodded. This was satisfactory. Breakfast resumed.

By seven, the house had developed several urgent needs.

My wife needed the bathroom. My daughter needed the shirt with the fox, which was in the wash. The cat needed everyone to know that the center of his food bowl was empty, although the edges remained extravagantly supplied.

I needed forty thousand dollars and another hour of sleep.

I settled for toast.

At 7:12, my wife entered the kitchen in her work clothes and kissed me with toothpaste still bright at the corner of her mouth.

“You’ve got something,” I said, touching my own lip.

She wiped the wrong side.

I wiped it for her.

For a moment, her face rested in my hand. Then the toast sprang up, our daughter shouted that her sock felt angry, and my wife remembered a meeting she was already late for.

Outside, the air smelled of rain and someone else’s bacon.

I strapped my daughter into the car. She held a drawing folded so tightly it had become a small, hard square.

“For your desk,” she said.

“Can I look?”

“At work.”

At the school gate, she ran three steps, came back for the mitten she’d dropped, and ran again. I watched until her pink hat disappeared through the doors.

Then I joined the traffic.

We advanced a car length at a time toward places we had promised to be. A man in the next lane shaved with an electric razor. A woman behind me sang with such conviction that I wished I could hear her.

At the longest red light, I unfolded the drawing.

Three people stood beneath a yellow sun. One was small. One had very long hair.

The third wore an enormous watch.

Above us, in letters that wandered downhill, my daughter had written:

MY FAMILY IS HERE.

The light changed.

Someone honked.

I put the drawing safely on the passenger seat and drove on.
