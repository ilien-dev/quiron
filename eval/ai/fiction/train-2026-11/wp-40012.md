# The Hair Was Never the Point

The aliens arrived on a Tuesday, which the internet agreed was rude.

Forty-one ships, each the size of a small city, settled into orbit and then dropped into the sky over every capital at once. Washington, Beijing, Moscow, Brasília, Lagos. Pyongyang too, though the broadcasters spent a full ten minutes hunting for it on the map.

The first demonstration took thirty seconds. A beam fell over the Pacific and Guam stopped being an island. Then a voice spoke in every language at once, in the tone of someone reading terms and conditions.

*"This planet has been assessed and found suitable. You have one hour to prepare for harvest."*

In the Situation Room, in the Kremlin, in a bunker beneath Zhongnanhai, the most powerful people on Earth did what the powerful do. They called each other. Nobody had a plan, because nobody had ever needed one.

---

Kim Jong Un watched it all from a leather chair in his residence, eating a peach.

His generals were shouting in the next room. His sister had gone gray. A young aide stood in the doorway with his hands shaking, waiting to be told what to say, and Kim could see the boy was more afraid of the silence than of the sky.

"Leave us," Kim said softly. The room emptied.

He finished the peach and set the pit on a saucer. Then he looked at his hands, the ones the whole world had spent fifteen years mocking.

The jokes had been useful. A fat, strange, volatile little man with bad hair and worse missiles. Everyone had a bit about him. Nobody had ever asked the better question: why had a country with no money, no allies, and no oil survived seventy years of everyone wanting it gone?

The sanctions, the summits, the assassination plots that went wrong in ways the CIA still couldn't explain. The satellite that "malfunctioned" over the Yellow Sea in 2017. The one time a drone strike had been scheduled and every drone in the region simply lay down on the ground like tired dogs.

His grandfather had found it first, in 1950, when a bomb fell on a village and didn't go off. His father had hidden it better. Kim had inherited it at twenty-seven, along with the rest of the mess, and the first thing he'd understood was that you cannot be the man who can do anything. If the world knew, it would never stop asking. Fix this famine. End that war. Be our god, or be our weapon.

So he'd chosen to be a joke instead, and had kept the power wrapped up like a coal in a fist.

On the television, the voice said: *"Fifty-one minutes."*

Kim stood. His knees cracked. He was not young anymore.

"All right," he said to the empty room. "Enough."

---

He walked out onto the balcony. Below him the capital had gone quiet, the usual parade-ground hush replaced by something real. Ordinary people stood in the streets with their faces turned up.

The ship above Pyongyang was a black cathedral, humming in a key that made his teeth ache. He had never reached for all of it before. He had always taken small sips, a drone here, a bomb there, a coincidence nudged one inch to the left.

He opened his hand all the way.

It felt like exhaling after seventy years of holding his breath.

The air bent. Every window in the city rang once, like a struck glass. The ship above him stopped humming, and the silence afterward was so total that birds fell quiet in the trees.

Then Kim Jong Un reached up, not with his arm but with the other thing, the old thing, and *pulled*.

The ship over Pyongyang folded like paper. The ones over Washington and Moscow and Lagos folded too, because it turned out distance had never mattered. It had only ever been a rule he'd followed out of caution. Forty-one ships crumpled one after another, until a rain of bright, harmless dust drifted down over six continents. It settled on rooftops and on the upturned faces of the people who had been certain they were about to die.

In orbit, the voice began to say something. Kim looked at the sky with an expression nobody had ever seen on him in public, flat and patient and very tired.

"No," he said, and the voice stopped.

---

By evening, the world had a hundred theories and no explanation. The UN convened. The footage of the balcony went everywhere, one grainy figure with one raised hand.

Kim sat back down in his leather chair. His nose was bleeding a little. His sister brought him a towel and said nothing, which was how he knew she had always known.

"They'll ask now," she said finally. "Everyone. Forever."

"Yes."

"What will you tell them?"

He considered it. Outside, for the first time in his life, he could hear people cheering, and it frightened him more than the ships had.

He picked up the peach pit from the saucer and turned it over in his fingers.

"That I'm available for meetings," he said. "But I want better terms."
