The first thing I learned was that my wife had lied about the kitchen.

“It’s yellow,” she’d told me when we bought the house. “Like butter.”

Through the glasses, the walls were green.

“You said it was yellow.”

Anna stood very still beside the sink.

Then she laughed. “You hated green.”

I laughed too. There were so many other things to look at.

The tomatoes on the windowsill. The blue flame beneath the kettle. Her hair, which contained more colours than I had names for.

And the thin red thread coming out of her chest.

It passed through her cardigan without disturbing the wool and disappeared through the kitchen wall.

I took the glasses off.

The thread vanished.

“What?” she asked.

“Nothing.”

***

Outside, everyone had one.

Some threads lay slack along the pavement. Others stretched taut across streets, through buses, into the sky. They passed through people without tangling.

At the café, a young man’s thread ran across the table into the chest of the woman opposite him.

Hers went out the window.

I watched him reach for her hand.

I went home early.

***

For three days, I followed Anna’s thread.

Not physically. Not at first.

I stood in the kitchen and noted its direction. Upstairs, it pointed downward. In the garden, back toward the house.

By Thursday I understood.

It ended in the little bedroom we kept shut.

I found Anna there that evening, sitting on the bare floor.

The thread ran from her chest into the white cardboard box on her lap.

She looked up at me.

“You can see it,” she said.

I sat beside her.

Inside the box were a hospital bracelet, a knitted hat, and the photograph we’d been given permission to take.

Our daughter had lived for nineteen minutes.

“What colour?” Anna asked.

“Red.”

She nodded, as if that made sense.

“Where does mine go?” I asked.

She looked at my chest, then at my face.

“I can’t see them.”

Of course she couldn’t. I’d forgotten.

***

I found mine in the bathroom mirror.

It was grey.

I thought that meant it was dying.

I pulled at my shirt, but the thread emerged from the skin beneath it. When I turned, it slipped through my ribs without sensation. I couldn’t touch it.

I followed it downstairs.

Past Anna.

Past the little bedroom.

Into the garage, where my old camping equipment sat beneath a blue tarpaulin.

It ended at a sealed plastic storage bin.

I knew what was inside before I lifted the lid.

A length of rope.

I sat down on the concrete.

I had bought it eight months earlier. I had never taken it out of its packaging.

After a while, Anna opened the garage door.

“There you are.”

I couldn’t look at her.

She came closer. Saw the open bin.

“Oh,” she said.

Then she sat down too.

***

I’d thought the threads showed love.

That was close enough to fool me.

They showed what we couldn’t let go of.

The boy at the café. My wife and her small white box. Me, keeping an exit where I could find it.

Anna held my hand while I called my brother. I told him what I’d bought. Then I told him why.

The following morning, he took the bin away.

My thread went with it.

For weeks, I could still see it stretching through the garage wall. Sometimes it frightened me how little the distance mattered.

I kept going to my appointments.

In February, I looked down while making tea and couldn’t find it.

I called Anna into the kitchen. She waited while I checked behind me, then under my shirt.

“It’s gone,” I said.

She put her arms around me.

Hers was still there. I could see it over her shoulder, running upstairs.

I held her carefully, leaving room for it.
