# Inside

The fortune cookie came with the lo mein I ate standing over the sink, the way I'd eaten every dinner since Marcus took the couch, the good knives, and somehow the cat.

I cracked it open out of habit. No lucky numbers. No *You will find happiness in unexpected places.* Just a line of small, careful type:

**1147 Alder Street, Apt. 2B. Thursdays after 7.**

I turned it over. Blank. I checked the bag for a receipt, a flyer, anything. Nothing but soy sauce packets and a fork I'd never use.

It was Thursday. It was 7:40.

I told myself I wasn't going. I told myself that in the shower, and while I put on the jacket that didn't smell like him, and while I typed the address into my phone. Eleven minutes away. It was the kind of distance that makes stupidity feel affordable.

1147 Alder was a brick walk-up with a bakery on the ground floor, closed for the night, its windows fogged with the ghosts of bread. The buzzer for 2B had no name, just a strip of masking tape with a hand-drawn fortune cookie on it.

I pressed it before I could think.

"Door's open," crackled a voice. Old, amused. "It always is."

The stairs creaked. Apartment 2B's door stood ajar, spilling yellow light into the hallway. Inside, it smelled like jasmine tea and old paper. Every surface held books, and every shelf between the books held jars of tiny paper slips, sorted by color.

At a kitchen table sat a woman who might have been eighty or a hundred. Her eyes were milky, her hands spotted and steady around a teacup.

"Sit," she said. "You're new. New ones always stand in the doorway like they're waiting to be arrested."

I sat. "I got your address. In a cookie."

"Mm. Golden Lotus or Happy Garden?"

"Golden Lotus."

"Good. Their cookies are better." She slid a second cup toward me, filled it without spilling a drop. "I'm Mrs. Lin. I wrote fortunes for the Pacific Star factory for thirty-one years. Do you know how many fortunes that is?"

"A lot?"

"Four million, give or take. *You will travel to distant lands. A friend is a present you give yourself.*" She waved a hand. "When I retired, they let me keep a small batch. Twenty cookies a month, my words, my choice. Most months I put in one address."

"Why?"

She tilted her head as if listening for something behind my voice. "Because my husband used to read to me, and then he didn't, and my eyes went the same year. Because I spent three decades telling strangers good things were coming and never once checked if they did." She tapped a stack of envelopes on the table. "These are his letters. From before we married. He wrote very badly and very often. I'd like to hear them again."

So I read.

His handwriting was terrible. His spelling was worse. He'd written to her from a cannery in Alaska in 1961, complaining about the cold and the fish and the foreman, and then, at the bottom of every letter, without fail: *Save me a seat at your table.*

I read for two hours. She laughed at the parts about the foreman. She went quiet at the parts about her. At some point I realized I was crying, and at some point after that I realized she knew and was politely pretending not to.

"Your voice is sad," she said finally. "Fresh sad. Not old sad."

"Breakup."

"Ah." She nodded like a doctor confirming a diagnosis. "Come next Thursday. There are three hundred letters. He was very cold in Alaska."

I came the next Thursday. And the one after. Sometimes others were there: a college kid named Theo who'd gotten the address in October, a retired bus driver who brought almond cookies and argued with Mrs. Lin about poetry. We took turns reading. We stayed late. Nobody stood in the doorway anymore.

In March, Mrs. Lin handed me a small paper slip and a pen.

"My eyes are worse," she said. "My hands too. This month's batch is yours. Write something true."

I sat at her table a long time, thinking about four million fortunes, about strangers cracking open small sweet shells looking for a reason.

Then I wrote: **1147 Alder Street, Apt. 2B. Thursdays after 7. There's a seat saved for you.**
