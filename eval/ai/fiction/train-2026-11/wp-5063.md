**Doorways**

I died on a Tuesday, mid-sentence, which I felt was rude of the universe.

There was no light and no tunnel. I was standing in a kitchen that wasn't mine, and a woman in a powdered wig was eating soup that didn't seem to exist.

"New one," she said. "Sit. Don't touch the walls, they're other people's."

"Am I in Heaven?"

"God, no." She slurped. "Think smaller."

"Hell?"

"Think *smaller*, dear."

It turned out the afterlife is a job. Nobody tells you that. The dead aren't judged or rewarded. We're *assigned*, and the work is what's left of a person once the person is gone. We are the feeling you get when you walk into a room and forget why you came.

"Doorway Duty," said the woman, whose name was Marguerite and who had been dead since 1743. "Someone crosses a threshold, their mind is between one thing and the next, and we slip in. We tug. A little *wait, what was I doing?* It's very delicate work."

"That's it? That's the whole eternity?"

"Oh, no. Sometimes you're the song stuck in someone's head. Sometimes you're the sense that they left the stove on." She leaned in. "Sometimes it's *actually* on. Those are good days."

I wanted to be angry. I'd spent forty-one years expecting either harps or brimstone, and I'd been handed a clipboard, metaphorically. But Marguerite showed me the board, and it wasn't random. Each of us was tied to someone we had loved, or at least someone who had loved us. We were the residue, the leftover pull of a person.

"Who'm I assigned to?" I asked.

She checked. "A Delia Okafor-Reyes. Eight years old. Kitchen, 6:40 a.m., most days."

My daughter.

I hadn't thought I could feel anything without a body, but something in me went tight and bright, like a struck bell.

"What do I do?"

"Wait for a doorway. Then tug. Gently. You get one per crossing, and the living aren't built to hold more." She went back to her soup. "Don't try to say anything. Everyone tries to say something. It comes out as *did I pack my lunch?* and that's the best any of us can do."

I waited. Time here is mostly waiting, which I suppose is why the living call it eternity.

Then the kitchen dissolved, and there was a doorframe, and a small girl in mismatched socks stopping at it, backpack half-zipped, mind already running toward the bus.

I had one tug. I'd had forty-one years of things to say, and I had one tug.

*Don't run*, I thought. *Look at the table.*

Delia paused. She frowned the way I used to frown, with the left eyebrow doing all the work. She turned around, walked back to the table, and picked up the lunch her father had packed, the one with the note in it that said *proud of you, kiddo*.

She stood there a moment, holding it, looking at nothing, at the exact place where I wasn't.

"Weird," she said quietly. "I thought..."

She didn't finish. She smiled, small and puzzled, and put the lunch in her bag.

Then she was gone through the door, and I was back in the not-kitchen, shaking with something that might have been joy.

Marguerite didn't look up. "First one's always the hardest."

"She *felt* me."

"She felt *something*. That's all any of us are, dear. The thing you almost remember." She pushed a bowl of nonexistent soup toward me. "Eat. Tomorrow there's a doorway at 6:40."

I sat down.

Somewhere, a woman I'd never meet walked into a room and forgot why. She stood there, bewildered and a little warm, and a hundred years away, someone who loved her tugged and smiled.
