# Through Margaret's Eyes

I'd never thought about the day I got hired through Margaret's perspective until I found her old journal in the office filing cabinet. It was labelled "2014 Observations" and my heart did a strange thing when I saw my own name on the first page.

Margaret was our HR director, a woman I'd known primarily as a voice from behind a desk, someone who handled terminations and benefits with efficient professionalism. I'd never considered her having opinions about me specifically, much less writing them down.

The entry from my first week was startling:

"New hire, J. Santos—completely wrong person for this job, but exactly the person who *needs* to be here. Comes in with that 'I'm going to work harder than everyone' energy that never works out. Can already see the stress. Is he going to make it? Probably not. Should I tell him now? No. Some people need to learn by discovering."

I'd been anxious that first week. I'd thought I'd hidden it well.

Reading her observations over the months was like looking at myself through a lens I'd never known existed. She saw things I wasn't aware I was projecting. On October 15th, she wrote: "He's going to break something this week. Not physically—emotionally. He's been holding it together through pure momentum and it's running out."

I did break that week. I'd had a panic attack in the bathroom on Thursday and spent Friday feeling hollow.

By November, her tone shifted: "Interesting. Instead of quitting like I predicted, he's integrating. Made actual friends with the team. His file organization system is a disaster but it's *his* disaster now. Also he smiled today like he actually meant it instead of performing 'grateful new employee.' Growth."

I was genuinely surprised by this. I hadn't realized I'd been performing gratitude until she pointed out that I'd stopped.

The following year had more entries. She catalogued my mistakes with surprising gentleness. "Presentation today was terrible. Completely underprepared. But he owned it instead of making excuses. That matters more than the preparation."

She noticed when I was depressed. "He's going through something—not work-related, something personal. Covering it with extra hours. Should tell him he can take time. Will he use it? Probably not. But the offer matters."

The most striking entry came in late 2015: "Thinking about J. today. Two years in. He's not the person I predicted would fail. But he's also not thriving. He's maintaining. Is that good enough? For him, maybe. For the company, definitely. But for *him*? I wonder if he even knows how much smaller he's become since he started. Like he showed up with this enormous internal space and we've built walls around it. He's still in there, but you can see less of him now."

I put the journal down with shaking hands. Because she was right. Somewhere between being the terrified new hire and becoming the competent mid-level employee, I'd compressed. The excitement about possibilities had calcified into routine. The version of myself I'd brought to that first day had slowly been filed away.

I never confronted Margaret about the journal. She passed away six months after I found it—nothing dramatic, just heart problems she hadn't mentioned to anyone. At her funeral I sat in the back and watched people describe her as "efficient" and "professional" without mentioning anything about her capacity for observation, her strange kindness in recognizing people's potential failures before they did.

Reading her entries on me had changed something, though I couldn't quite name what. It was like seeing a photograph of yourself from years ago and realizing how much you'd changed without noticing. But unlike normal nostalgia, I could actually do something about it.

I spent the next six months gradually reversing the compression. I started saying no to things. I revised projects according to my own vision rather than what I assumed was expected. I laughed more readily. I took the vacation days I'd been hoarding. Small acts of remembering myself.

A year later, I quit and started my own consulting firm. Nothing revolutionary—just me working with people like Margaret had described: people who showed up to things with enormous internal space that the world gradually compressed. I try to do what she'd done—see them clearly, document their potential, notice when they're performing versus becoming.

I never would have thought of myself as fundamentally different from Margaret. She was the observer; I was the observed. But maybe that's the most critical perspective shift any of us can make—recognizing that every stranger has the same interior observation apparatus we do. Everyone's running their own continuous commentary on everyone else.

Margaret saw who I was before I knew I'd stopped being her. And somehow, that recognition gave me permission to become someone else entirely.

That's the gift of being truly seen—not judged, but genuinely observed. Everything changes after that.
