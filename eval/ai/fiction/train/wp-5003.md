The dead came at sunset.

They shambled between the rusted swing set and the old picnic tables, faces smeared with mud, arms reaching, mouths making hungry sounds.

“Hold the fence!” Marcus shouted.

The fence was three jump ropes tied between two maple trees. It had protected the settlement for nine days, which was almost three weeks in real time because they weren’t allowed to play on Tuesdays or Thursdays.

Ellie planted her sneakers in the dirt and pulled the middle rope tight. Beyond it, six zombies groaned and grabbed at the air. One of them—Noah from number fourteen—still wore his school backpack. He had joined the horde after his big sister called him away for piano lessons.

“We can’t hold them!” Ellie cried.

“We have to!” Marcus raised his orange plastic blaster. “The little ones are still inside!”

The little ones crouched beneath the playground slide, hugging juice bottles and whispering about how everybody they loved was dead.

Marcus fired. “Pew! Pew!”

Two zombies fell. One complained that Marcus hadn’t actually hit him, but Marcus pointed out that he was the sheriff and had clearly aimed for the head. The zombie accepted this and lay down beside the sandbox.

For one perfect moment, the settlement held.

Then a voice rolled across the wasteland.

“Daniel!”

Everyone froze.

It came again, louder this time, from beyond the hedge.

“Daniel! Dinner!”

A zombie lowered her arms. Someone whispered, “That’s his mom.”

Marcus turned toward Daniel.

Daniel stood beside the picnic table, clutching a foam sword with both hands. He was their best fighter. He had killed twenty-seven zombies, rescued Ellie from the sewer tunnel, and once spent an entire recess trapped behind enemy lines without breaking character.

Now his face went pale.

“No,” Ellie said.

Daniel looked toward the hedge. “I have to go.”

“You can’t.” Marcus lowered his blaster. “We need you.”

“Daniel!” the voice called. “Your food’s getting cold!”

The horde began to groan again, but softly, respectfully.

Daniel swallowed. “I got bit.”

“No, you didn’t,” Ellie said quickly. “Nobody touched you.”

He looked at her, and something passed between them—the knowledge that heroes didn’t simply leave. Heroes fell.

Daniel pulled up his sleeve and stared at a perfectly ordinary patch of elbow. “One got me at the fence.”

Ellie shook her head. “We can cut off your arm.”

“It’s too late.”

The dead closed in as the sun burned orange through the trees.

Daniel handed Marcus his foam sword. It was blue, with a silver handle rubbed white from years of battles.

“Get them to the farmhouse,” he said.

“The farmhouse is just Mr. Alvarez’s shed,” one of the little ones whispered.

“Not anymore,” Marcus told her.

Daniel climbed onto the picnic table. The zombies surrounded him, reaching but not touching. Ellie could hear forks and plates clinking through an open kitchen window somewhere beyond the battlefield.

“Go!” Daniel shouted. “I’ll hold them off!”

Marcus grabbed Ellie’s hand. Together, they led the survivors toward the shed while Daniel swung an invisible weapon and cried out against impossible odds.

At the hedge, he stopped.

“Do I get a last line?” he asked.

Ellie’s eyes stung. She nodded.

Daniel thought for a moment. Then he stood taller.

“Tell my brother I wasn’t scared.”

“You don’t have a brother,” said Noah.

“I might’ve had one before the outbreak.”

“Oh. Right.”

Daniel staggered dramatically. The zombies swarmed him. He fell into the grass, rolled twice, and went still.

“Daniel!” his mother called.

He opened one eye. “Coming!”

Then he jumped up, brushed leaves from his knees, and ducked through the hedge.

The survivors watched him vanish into the warm yellow light of his back door.

Marcus carried the blue sword to the farmhouse. That evening, they buried it beneath the slide and marked the grave with three stones.

Daniel returned the next afternoon.

He stood outside the jump-rope fence, his face streaked with mud, his arms hanging loose at his sides.

Ellie stared at him.

Daniel groaned.

And the settlement began to scream.
