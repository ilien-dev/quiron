# The Review

I died on a Wednesday afternoon, choking on a pretzel, which I'm told is more common than you'd think.

There was no tunnel. No light. One moment I was in my kitchen, and the next I was sitting in a folding chair in what looked like an enormous high school gymnasium, with bleachers stretching up and up into a ceiling I couldn't see.

The bleachers were full. Billions of people, maybe. All of them dead, all of them looking as confused as I felt.

"Excuse me," I said to the woman next to me. She was wearing a hospital gown and a pair of fuzzy slippers. "Is this—"

"Judgment Day," she said. "Apparently."

"Oh," I said. My stomach dropped. I started running through everything I'd ever done wrong. The shoplifting in eighth grade. The things I'd said to my brother. The time I'd pretended not to see a neighbor struggling with groceries.

"Relax," she said. "It's not us."

---

Down on the gymnasium floor, a long table had been set up. Behind it sat seven figures. I couldn't look directly at them. It wasn't that they were bright, exactly. It was that my eyes kept sliding off, like they were too big to fit into a single glance.

In front of the table stood a single chair. Empty.

A voice rang out, and it came from everywhere at once.

"The Assembly will now review the work of the Creator of World 7,241,009. Designation: Earth."

The crowd murmured. And then a figure walked out onto the floor and sat down in the chair.

I'm not sure what I expected God to look like. An old man with a beard, maybe. A burning light. Something.

He looked like a middle-aged guy in a wrinkled cardigan. He had a coffee stain on his shirt. He looked like he hadn't slept in a very long time.

He looked nervous.

---

"Please state your name for the record," said one of the figures at the table.

"Um. Yahweh. Also Allah. Also some others. It depends on who you ask."

"You created World 7,241,009?"

"I did."

"How long did it take?"

He shifted in his chair. "Six days. Plus a day off."

A few of the figures at the table exchanged looks. One of them wrote something down.

"That's very fast," said the one in the middle. Her voice was calm, almost kind, the way a doctor's voice is kind. "Most Creators take several eons."

"I know," he said. "I was excited. I had a lot of ideas."

---

The review went on for what felt like days, though I'm not sure time works the same way there.

They went through everything. Big screens lowered from the ceiling showing Earth from every angle, every era. The Assembly asked about everything.

They asked about the mosquito. He said it was for the ecosystem. They asked what, specifically, in the ecosystem. He didn't have a great answer.

They asked about childhood cancer. He was quiet for a very long time. Then he said, "I know," and put his face in his hands, and the whole gymnasium was silent.

They asked about free will. He perked up a little. He'd clearly prepared for that one. He talked for almost an hour about choice and meaning and how you can't have love without the freedom not to love. Some of the figures nodded. One of them didn't.

"But you gave them free will," said the one who didn't nod, "and then you gave them hunger, and scarcity, and a nervous system designed for fear, and a lifespan of seventy years. And then you told them to be good."

"Yes."

"Do you think that was fair?"

He opened his mouth. Then he closed it. "No," he said finally. "Probably not."

---

They showed the wars. All of them. The plagues. The famines. The camps. It went on for so long that people in the bleachers started crying, and then stopped crying, and then just sat there, numb.

I watched the man in the cardigan. He watched every second of it. He didn't look away once. His face didn't change, but his hands were shaking in his lap.

---

Then the one in the middle, the kind one, raised her hand, and the screens changed.

"We will now review the other evidence," she said.

It started small.

A woman in a kitchen, humming while she cooked. A kid on a bike, going downhill for the first time without training wheels. Two old men playing chess in a park, arguing about nothing. Someone's dog, going absolutely berserk with joy when its owner came home.

Then bigger. A stranger running into a burning building. A nurse holding the hand of a dying man she'd never met, so he wouldn't be alone. A boy in a refugee camp teaching a younger boy to read, using a stick in the dirt.

Music. So much music. Every song anyone had ever sung, it felt like, all at once.

Someone in the bleachers was laughing now, and someone else was sobbing, and I realized I was doing both.

I saw my grandmother on the screen. She was making biscuits. I was there, too, very small, standing on a chair so I could reach the counter, flour on my face.

---

The screens went dark. The gymnasium was quiet.

"The Assembly will now deliberate," said the voice.

The seven figures leaned together. They talked for a long time, voices too low to hear. The man in the cardigan sat alone in his chair and waited. He looked, more than anything, like a student waiting for a grade.

The woman in fuzzy slippers leaned over to me.

"What do you think they'll give him?" she whispered.

"I don't know," I said. I thought about the camps. I thought about my grandmother's kitchen. "I really don't know."

---

Finally the kind one sat back.

"World 7,241,009," she said. "The Assembly finds that its Creator acted with haste. That he failed to anticipate much of the suffering his design would cause. That he gave his creations burdens too heavy for them, and was often absent when they needed him most."

The man in the cardigan nodded slowly. He didn't argue.

"The Assembly also finds," she continued, "that his creations, given every reason to despair, frequently chose not to. That they built hospitals. That they wrote songs. That they fed strangers and loved each other and got back up. The Assembly notes that this was not something the Creator did. This was something they did."

She paused.

"The Assembly will not strike World 7,241,009 from the record. The Creator is ordered to return to it. He is ordered to be more present. To pay closer attention." She looked at him, and her voice softened even further. "And he is ordered to learn from them."

---

He stood up. He looked out at the bleachers, at all of us, billions of faces. For a moment, I swear, he looked right at me.

"Thank you," he said. His voice cracked. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. You were better than I made you."

Then he turned and walked back through the door he'd come from.

The woman next to me let out a long breath.

"Well," she said. "That's something."

"Yeah," I said, and wiped my eyes. "That's something."

Somewhere far below us, I imagined, a world kept turning, and in some kitchen somewhere, someone was making biscuits.
