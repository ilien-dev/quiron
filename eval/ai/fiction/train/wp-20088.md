# The 27th Precinct

Marcus had been a firefighter for fourteen years before he realized that running into burning buildings had somehow become the least dangerous part of his job. The gang formed in the spring when his entire shift came off duty at 6 AM and found three cars blocking the station's exit. Six guys, late twenties, professional-looking despite the prison ink.

"We have a job," their leader said, lean and scarred, showing no visible weapon but everyone could sense it was unnecessary. "You take orders from us now."

It would have been funny if it hadn't been immediately obvious they were serious.

What made the 27th Precinct Gang unique in Metro City's criminal landscape wasn't their structure—it was their membership. They recruited exclusively from emergency services: firefighters, paramedics, police dispatchers, even a few EMTs. Marcus wasn't the first firefighter brought in, but he was the second, which somehow made it worse.

The initial jobs were subtle. A warehouse fire that needed to spread a particular direction. An ambulance that needed to be unavailable at a critical moment. A dispatcher calling paramedics to the wrong address. Small interventions with massive consequences, all performed by people trained to save lives, now executing with the precision of people trained to save lives—except they weren't saving anyone.

"We're good at logistics," the leader explained his strategy one night in an abandoned clinic. "You people know systems, routes, timing. You know how cities actually work. That's valuable."

Marcus learned that the operation had been running for three years. The police unit came in six months after the firefighters, which had added a whole new dimension—actual law enforcement working to circumvent actual law enforcement. Their dispatcher member was crucial; she could route units with precision that made law enforcement coordination look like chaos.

The money was better than being a firefighter. That was harder to think about than it should have been.

The turning point came during a warehouse operation in the industrial sector. Marcus was stationed with Jerome, a paramedic he'd known from the academy, watching the entrance. The job was supposed to be clean—controlled, they'd said. The kind of robbery that looked like a robbery.

But something went wrong. A security guard nobody predicted. Jerome moved without thinking, muscle memory from a decade of emergency response, and the guard went down. Not dead, but not conscious either.

Jerome's hands started shaking.

"This isn't why we took the job," Marcus said, which was stupid because Jerome didn't take the job. Jerome was coerced the same way everyone was coerced. They'd started small with all of them.

The realization that struck Marcus then was less a revelation than a recognition: the gang had genuinely found the ideal criminal workforce. They'd found people whose entire identity was built around emergency response, around thinking quick, around staying calm under pressure. They'd weaponized that commitment.

By the time they decided to leave, three more emergency workers had joined the operation, bringing the total to eighteen. An entire parallel apparatus operating inside the city's infrastructure. The leader had started talking about expanding to other cities, other stations, a proper organization.

Marcus went to his lieutenant first. Then Internal Affairs. Then the FBI field office downtown. He wore a wire for four months while his entire identity splintered. The other members figured it out gradually—Jerome noticed him documenting the schedule, Alice saw him photographing the equipment manifest, someone overheard his phone conversation.

When federal agents raided the operations, they arrested twelve emergency workers and disrupted criminal enterprises totaling seventeen million dollars in direct losses. Marcus was transferred to a different station. He didn't make lieutenant. Some firefighters refused to work his shifts.

Three years later, Marcus rides the same equipment he always did, responds to the same alarms, saves the lives that need saving. But he's painfully aware now of how thin the line is between the people you trust and the people who are using your trust as a tool.

The 27th Precinct Gang, as the press called them, had been broken up years ago. But Marcus knows they'd tried to recruit his new station three times since then. Other cities had caught similar operations. The thing about emergency services is that the exact qualities that make you good at the job—the discipline, the training, the ability to work under pressure—also made you susceptible to manipulation.

He goes to his therapist every other Thursday and doesn't talk about it much. Some things don't need to be discussed to be understood.
