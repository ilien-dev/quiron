When the Persians came to Plataea, I dropped my shield and ran.

There are prettier ways to tell it. I could say the dust blinded me, or that I mistook retreating skirmishers for a general withdrawal. I could mention the arrow that grazed my thigh, the screams of horses, the man beside me whose face vanished beneath a bronze-shod hoof.

But the truth is simple.

I was afraid.

I fled to a grove where the battle sounded like a storm heard from inside a house. There I crouched beneath an olive tree, pressing my forehead to its roots, and prayed to every god except the one who heard me.

The air turned hot. Sap hissed in the bark. A tall man stood between the trees, naked but for a crested helm, his skin streaked with blood too bright to be mortal.

“Why do you hide?” Ares asked.

My mouth shaped lies.

He struck the olive tree with his spear. It split from crown to root.

“Cowardice is not fear,” he said. “All men fear. Cowardice is believing your fear matters more than the man beside you.”

I begged forgiveness.

Ares smiled.

“You shall have time to earn it.”

He touched his spearpoint to my heart.

I awoke at Plataea as the sun rose, young and unscarred, while men fastened armor around me. I fought. I killed two Persians and did not run. When victory came, I thought the curse broken.

Then I died forty-four years later in my bed.

I awoke beneath Roman standards.

At Cannae, I learned that courage does not prevent slaughter. We stood so tightly packed I could not lift my sword. Hannibal’s men closed around us, and the summer air became too thick to breathe. I died upright between strangers, held on my feet by the dead.

I awoke in Gaul.

I awoke outside Jerusalem.

I awoke beneath an eagle, then a cross, then a crescent.

Sometimes I was Greek. Sometimes Roman. Frank. Saxon. Mongol. I spoke every tongue when I opened my mouth, though in dreams I still called my mother mitéra.

Ares kept his promise with a god’s cruelty. Every great war pulled me toward it like iron to a lodestone. If kings gathered armies, I found a sword in my hand. If borders shifted, my boots stood upon them.

At Hastings, an arrow entered my eye.

At Agincourt, mud swallowed men more eagerly than steel.

At Constantinople, cannon fire shook the walls I had once believed eternal. I saw the city fall and thought of the olive tree splitting under Ares’s spear.

I searched for meaning. At first, I believed I had been sent to become brave. So I charged first, volunteered for doomed assaults, stood atop walls when ladders appeared. I collected wounds as though they were coins I might pay to the god.

Still I awoke.

Then I believed I must become skilled. I mastered spear, gladius, longbow, musket, rifle. I learned to load powder in rain and clear a jam with numb fingers. I could judge artillery by its whistle and tell which scream meant “help” and which meant “leave me.”

Still I awoke.

At Waterloo, after the guns stopped, I found a drummer boy calling for his mother. His legs were gone. He asked whether we had won.

I told him yes.

We had not. It seemed important that one of us believe otherwise.

He died holding my hand, and for the first time in centuries I wondered whether courage had anything to do with winning.

In the trenches of the Somme, the rats grew fat on men who had possessed more courage than sense. Our officers blew whistles, and we climbed into machine-gun fire because the soldiers beside us climbed too.

A boy named Thomas shook so badly he could not fix his bayonet.

“I’m a coward,” he whispered.

“No,” I said.

I took his hand and pressed it against my chest. My heart hammered harder than his.

“This is fear. Cowardice comes after.”

We went over the top together.

A shell erased him before he took ten steps.

I awoke in Poland in 1939.

By then, war had learned industry. Men died on schedules. Names became numbers, towns became rubble, and the sky itself carried murder.

I fought in France, Russia, North Africa, the Pacific. At Stalingrad, I ate frozen horsemeat beside a man who kept a photograph of an orchard in spring. At Normandy, the sea turned pink around boys who never reached the sand. In Berlin, a little girl aimed an empty pistol at me from beneath a staircase.

I lowered my rifle.

She pulled the trigger anyway.

Click.

That sound followed me longer than artillery.

Still I awoke.

Korea. Vietnam. Afghanistan. Wars declared and undeclared, televised and forgotten. Uniforms changed. Prayers did not. Every soldier asked the same questions before battle and the same one afterward:

Why him, not me?

I knew the answer.

Because the god of war was not finished.

Centuries passed. Rifles became weapons that saw in darkness. Aircraft lost their pilots. Nations fought through machines, then through machines that chose their own targets.

Earth’s last great war began without a trumpet.

By then, soldiers wore sealed armor and carried enough fire to kill an ancient army. I served aboard the *Thermopylae*, though no one understood why the name made me laugh.

We orbited Mars when Earth vanished.

There was no sound. Only a white wound opening across the blue planet, bright enough to darken the stars. Then the world of my birth became a ring of glowing stone.

The crew stared through the viewing glass.

Some prayed. Some screamed. Captain Ilyan sat down and removed her helmet as though arriving home.

I waited to die.

Nothing happened.

No spearpoint. No new battlefield. No waking beneath another banner.

For the first time since Plataea, there were no armies left to join.

Then the proximity alarm sounded.

An enemy troopship drifted beyond us, crippled by the same blast. Two vessels remained from a dead species, each carrying the last soldiers of rival nations that no longer existed.

Weapons locked automatically.

“Fire,” Captain Ilyan ordered.

Our gunner hesitated.

“Fire!”

Across the void, the enemy ship turned its cannons toward us.

I saw Ares reflected in the glass behind my captain. He wore no bronze now. His armor was black as space, but his smile had not changed.

“This is the last battle,” he said. “Run, and you shall have nowhere left to wake.”

My hand rested on the firing control.

I thought of Plataea. Cannae. The Somme. Thomas trembling beside me. The child beneath the stairs. Ten thousand fields where frightened men had killed one another because someone braver, safer, or more foolish had commanded it.

At last I understood my sentence.

Ares had not condemned me to witness war until I became fearless.

He had condemned me until I learned what courage was for.

I disabled our weapons.

Captain Ilyan drew her pistol. I stepped between her and the gunner.

“That is treason,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You’ll kill us all.”

“Perhaps.”

I was afraid. After three thousand years, I was more afraid than I had been beneath the olive tree. Death was one thing. Trusting an enemy was another.

The captain fired.

The bullet struck below my ribs. I fell against the console, smearing it with blood. Warning lights flared as the enemy cannons charged.

Ares crouched beside me.

“Coward,” he whispered.

Through the glass, the enemy weapons glowed.

Then went dark.

Their ship transmitted two words:

WE YIELD.

Our bridge fell silent.

Ares’s face twisted—not in anger, I realized, but hunger. Without raised swords, without obedient hands, he was only a shadow cast by frightened people.

“You cannot end war,” he said.

“No,” I answered. “But I can end this one.”

The god vanished.

I felt my life draining warmly into my suit. Captain Ilyan knelt beside me, pressing both hands to the wound.

“Stay with us,” she said.

I almost laughed.

I had stayed long enough.

When I closed my eyes, I expected another dawn, another uniform, another weapon waiting beside my hand.

Instead, I heard wind moving through olive leaves.

And somewhere very far away, men laid down their arms.
