# Mind Games

The stadium held eighty thousand people, and not one of them was allowed to think.

That was the rule. You had to wear the dampener, a thin silver band that sat across your forehead and hummed faintly, the whole time you were inside. Otherwise the combined psychic noise of eighty thousand fans would flatten the players like a tidal wave. Back in the early days, before the dampeners, a man in section 112 had gotten so excited during overtime that he'd accidentally lifted the entire opposing team six feet into the air.

The league had not found that as funny as everyone else had.

---

Nadia Osei had been playing professional Deep Chess for four years, and she still threw up before every match.

"You good?" asked her coach, Emmett, handing her a towel through the stall door.

"Fantastic," she said, and threw up again.

Deep Chess wasn't the most popular of the Mind Games. That was Pushball, where two teams of telekinetics fought to shove a two-ton steel sphere across a field using only their minds. Pushball had the big crowds, the big contracts, the sneaker deals. Pushball players were famous.

Deep Chess players were mostly famous for having nosebleeds on live television.

---

The rules were deceptively simple. Two players sat across from each other at an ordinary chessboard. They played an ordinary game of chess.

The only difference was that both of them could read minds.

Which meant that every move you planned, your opponent knew. And every move they planned, you knew. And they knew you knew. And you knew they knew you knew. So the real game wasn't on the board at all. The real game was in how well you could hide your own thoughts, and how deep you could dig into theirs, and how many layers of bluffs and counter-bluffs you could stack before one of you cracked.

In the early days, games had lasted twelve hours. Now there was a clock.

---

Nadia's opponent in the championship final was Viktor Lang, a thirty-eight-year-old Austrian who had held the title for six years running.

Lang was a legend. He was said to have a mind like a hall of mirrors. You'd go digging for his plan and find a plan, and under that plan another one, and under that one a memory of his grandmother's kitchen, perfectly detailed, completely irrelevant, designed to waste your time. Players who'd faced him said it was like trying to read a book where every page was the first page.

"Don't go deep early," Emmett told her in the locker room. "That's what he wants. He'll lure you into the maze and you'll burn yourself out by move fifteen."

"So what do I do?"

"Stay shallow. Protect yourself. Wait for him to get cocky."

Nadia nodded, wiped her mouth, and walked out into the roar of the crowd.

---

The roar faded as she stepped onto the stage. The dampeners did that. It was eerie, eighty thousand people with their mouths open and no sound coming through except a low hum, like being underwater.

Lang was already seated. He smiled at her. It was a kind smile. She didn't trust it for a second.

The referee started the clock.

---

The first ten moves were polite. They both played standard openings, Lang with white, Nadia with black. She kept her thoughts light and surface-level, humming a song in her head, thinking about the grocery list, laying down a thin layer of static.

She could feel him out there, at the edge of her mind. Brushing against it. Testing. Like a hand running along a wall in the dark, looking for a door.

She didn't let him find one.

On move eleven, he moved his knight, and she let herself, just barely, peek.

It was like opening a door into a library the size of a city. Plans in every direction. Some real, some fake. Echoes of games he'd played years ago. A song, too, a slow waltz, played on a piano with one key slightly out of tune.

She pulled back fast, heart hammering. *Don't go deep early.*

Across the board, Lang's smile widened a fraction of an inch.

---

By move twenty-five, Nadia had a headache like a nail driven between her eyes.

She was losing. Not badly. But steadily. Every move she made, he seemed to see coming. Her static wasn't holding. It was like he'd found a crack somewhere and was just quietly listening through it.

She tasted copper. Her nose was bleeding. A medic stepped up with a tissue and she waved him off.

*You're good,* said a voice in her head.

She jumped. It was Lang. Talking directly into her mind was technically legal, but almost nobody did it. It took too much energy.

*Better than I expected,* he said. *But you're scared. I can feel it. You're so busy protecting yourself that you've forgotten how to play.*

*Get out of my head,* she thought back.

*It's a mind game, Ms. Osei.* She could hear the smile in it. *Getting into your head is the whole point.*

---

She sat back in her chair. She closed her eyes.

Emmett had told her to stay shallow. To protect herself. And she'd done that, for twenty-five moves, and it was losing her the match.

So she stopped.

She dropped the static. She dropped the grocery list and the song. She let every wall she'd built fall, all at once, and opened her mind up completely, like throwing open every window in a house.

She heard Lang gasp. Actually gasp, out loud.

Because suddenly he could see everything. Every plan. Every fear. Every half-formed thought she had about the board. Her childhood bedroom. Her mother's voice. The way she threw up before every match. The four moves she was considering, all at once, all equally real, all equally possible, because she genuinely didn't know which one she was going to pick.

There was no maze to navigate. No bluff to see through. It was just Nadia, all of her, completely unguarded.

And Lang, whose whole career had been built on hiding, had no idea what to do with that.

---

She felt him hesitate. Felt him go digging for the trap, the trick, the real plan hiding under the fake one. There wasn't one. He kept looking anyway. He couldn't help it. It was how his mind worked.

She could feel him burning time. Burning energy. His clock ticked down.

On move thirty-one, she moved her bishop, on instinct, without planning it at all.

Lang stared at the board. She felt the moment he saw it. A little cold flash of surprise, deep in that city of a mind.

Six moves later, it was checkmate.

---

The crowd couldn't cheer, because of the dampeners. But eighty thousand people stood up at once, and she could feel it anyway, even through the silver band, a wave of joy so enormous it made her dizzy.

Lang reached across the board and shook her hand. His nose was bleeding too.

*How did you know that would work?* he asked, inside her head.

*I didn't,* she thought back, honestly. *That's why it worked.*

He laughed out loud, and nodded, and let go of her hand.
