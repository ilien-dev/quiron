# Intake

The Good News, the woman at the desk told me, is that there is an afterlife. The Bad News is that it's none of the ones anybody spent two thousand years arguing about, and also that intake takes a while, so I should probably sit down.

I sat down. The chair was a normal chair. That was, somehow, the most unsettling detail so far.

"You're not a ghost," she continued, flipping through a folder with my name on it, though I didn't remember giving anyone my name. "I want to get that out of the way early because it's the first question everyone asks, right after 'am I actually dead,' which, yes. You're not translucent, you're not tethered to your old house, you're not going to spend eternity rattling chains at people who've moved into your bedroom. None of that. You're just — here."

"Where's here."

"That's the thing nobody wrote down correctly, because nobody who came here ever left to tell anyone." She said this with the weary cheer of a DMV clerk who has explained the same form four thousand times. "It's not Heaven. Heaven, as advertised, doesn't exist, sorry. Hell doesn't exist either, which I know sounds like good news, but stick with me. Purgatory was closer, conceptually, but only in the sense that a guess made in the dark is closer to correct than a guess made with your eyes closed and your hands over your ears."

"So what is this."

She set down the folder. "It's an onboarding process. For a job."

I laughed. I want that on the record — I actually laughed, a real laugh, the kind you can't fake, because it was such an absurd thing to say to a woman who I'm fairly sure had died of a heart attack in a grocery store parking lot forty minutes earlier that I hadn't yet processed as real.

She didn't laugh. That's when I stopped laughing.

"There's a lot of universe," she said, "and it turns out the universe needs a certain amount of upkeep. Not divine upkeep, nothing that grand — more like infrastructure. Somebody has to make sure entropy keeps behaving the way it's supposed to in the outer arms of certain galaxies. Somebody has to shepherd particular stars through their collapse so a supernova doesn't happen somewhere it'll wipe out a planet that's fifty years from developing something interesting. It's maintenance work. It's been done, historically, by whichever species dies enough members with enough — she checked the folder again — "aptitude."

"Aptitude for what."

"Patience, mostly. Attention to detail. A tolerance for extremely long stretches of nothing happening punctuated by moments where you personally have to nudge several trillion tons of stellar material by exactly the right amount." She looked up. "You scored unusually well on the intake assessment, for what it's worth. Most people don't."

"I didn't take an assessment."

"You took it your whole life. That's rather the point — we can't exactly administer a test after the fact, so we watch how people actually live instead. How you handled boredom. How you handled precision work nobody was going to thank you for. How you behaved when nothing was watching and nothing depended on getting it right except your own standards." She tapped the folder. "You spent eleven years doing structural inspections nobody ever double-checked, apparently, and you never once let a single measurement slide because you were tired. That's exactly the profile we look for."

I sat with that for a while. Whatever was underneath me that felt like a chair hummed faintly, a sound I hadn't noticed until the silence stretched long enough for me to hear it.

"What happens if I say no."

"Then you rest. Genuinely rest — not punishment, not further tests, just an end, the actual quiet everyone always hoped death would be. That's on the table too, always, no penalty for choosing it. Most people who score like you did take the job anyway, in the end. Not because we make it sound better than it is. It's lonely work and it's long work, longer than any word we have really captures. But there's something in people like you that wants, very badly, to be trusted with something that matters and won't be checked, and this is the largest version of that job that exists."

Outside whatever room this was, if outside meant anything here, I thought I could feel it — not see, feel, the way you feel a large truck pass on a highway before you hear it — an entire universe of unfinished, unglamorous work, waiting for someone patient enough to want it.

"Can I think about it," I said.

She smiled, and for the first time since I'd woken up in this normal chair, in this room that wasn't Heaven or Hell or Purgatory or anything anyone had ever correctly guessed, it felt like a real answer instead of a formality.

"Everyone asks that," she said. "Take all the time you want. Turns out, in here, there's plenty of it."
