# Five Seats

The car was a silver Honda Odyssey, and I was driving it, which was the first strange thing, because I sold my Odyssey in 2019.

The second strange thing was everyone else in it.

In the passenger seat sat a man in his late twenties with a bad haircut and a Nirvana T-shirt, drumming his fingers on the dashboard. Behind him, in the middle row, a seven-year-old kid with a missing front tooth was kicking the back of the seat and reading a Goosebumps book. Next to the kid sat a teenager, maybe fifteen, headphones on, staring out the window with the fixed, dramatic misery that only fifteen-year-olds can manage.

And in the back row, alone, sat an old man. White hair. Cardigan. Hands folded on his lap. He was looking at the back of my head, and he was smiling.

All of them were me.

I knew it the way you know things in dreams, instantly and completely. The kid had my chipped tooth from falling off the monkey bars. The teenager had my acne and my horrible choice of hoodie. The guy in the passenger seat was wearing a shirt I'd lost at a music festival in 2004.

The old man had my father's face. Which, I suppose, meant he had mine.

"Okay," I said, gripping the wheel. The highway stretched ahead of us, flat and endless, through brown hills I didn't recognize. "Okay. Does anybody know what's happening?"

"We're on a road trip," said the seven-year-old, not looking up from his book.

"Where to?"

He shrugged. "Grandma's?"

"Grandma's been dead since—" I stopped. The kid looked up at me with big, alarmed eyes. "Never mind," I said. "Yeah. Grandma's."

---

For a while, nobody talked. It was, I realized, a very accurate simulation of every car trip of my life.

Then the guy in the passenger seat, Twenty-Eight, cleared his throat.

"So," he said. "How's Jess?"

I kept my eyes on the road. "We broke up."

"When?"

"About a year after you. After me. Whatever. 2008."

He deflated. He actually slumped down in the seat. "Seriously? I was going to propose. I had the ring picked out. I was saving."

"I know," I said. "I remember."

"What happened?"

What happened was a lot of small things, and then one big thing, and then a long, quiet stretch where we both knew it was over and neither of us wanted to be the one to say so. What happened was I took the job in Denver. What happened was she didn't want to come.

"It just didn't work," I said. "It's okay. It's actually okay. You'll be okay."

He looked at me, searching my face like there was an answer in it. I tried to look like a man who was okay.

From the back row, the old man chuckled softly.

---

The teenager took his headphones off around the time we passed a sign for a town called Nowhere in Particular, population not listed.

"Do I ever get tall?" he asked.

"Five eleven," I said.

"That's not tall."

"It's fine. It's normal. Nobody cares."

"*Everybody* cares." He crossed his arms. "Do I ever get a girlfriend?"

Twenty-Eight snorted. "Oh, you'll get one."

"Is she hot?"

"Don't," I said to Twenty-Eight.

"Why not? It's the only good news we've got for him."

"Hey," said the teenager, offended.

"Listen," I said, catching his eyes in the rearview mirror. "You're going to have a hard couple of years. I'm not going to lie to you. Dad's going to move out. You're going to fail chemistry. You're going to spend a lot of time in your room listening to music that makes you sadder on purpose."

His jaw tightened. "Great. Thanks."

"But you're going to meet Danny Ruiz in eleventh grade, and he's going to be your best friend for the next twenty-five years. He's going to be the best man at your wedding."

The teenager blinked. "I get married?"

"Twice," said the old man from the back.

We all turned. Well, I didn't. I was driving. But I looked in the mirror.

He was still smiling, that same calm, knowing smile.

"The second one takes," he said. "Don't worry."

---

The seven-year-old had put his book down. He was looking at all of us, from face to face, with a kind of slow, dawning horror.

"Are you all me?"

"Yeah, buddy," I said.

He looked at the teenager, with his acne and his black hoodie. He looked at Twenty-Eight, with his heartbreak and his Nirvana shirt. He looked at me, forty-three, tired, with gray starting at my temples.

Then he turned around and looked at the old man in the back seat for a long, long time.

"Do we get old?" he asked quietly.

"We do," said the old man.

"Does it hurt?"

The old man considered this. He unfolded his hands and looked at them, the spots and the veins and the swollen knuckles.

"Sometimes," he said. "The knees, mostly." He leaned forward. "But you know what? You get to see a lot of things. You get to see everything these guys are worried about turn out fine. Or not fine. Different. But you get through it."

The kid thought about that. Then he held up his Goosebumps book.

"Do I ever finish the series?"

The old man laughed, a real laugh, deep and wheezing. "No. You get bored around number forty. But you become a big reader. Your whole life, you read."

"Cool," said the kid, satisfied, and picked the book back up.

---

The sun was going down. The road was orange and gold. I realized I didn't know how long we'd been driving or where we were going.

"Hey," I said, to the mirror. To the old man. "Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask."

"Am I—" I hesitated. The others were listening. Even the teenager had one ear free. "Am I doing okay? Right now? At forty-three?"

He didn't answer right away. He looked out at the hills, the long shadows, the empty highway.

"You're worried about your son," he said finally. "About whether you're doing it right. You're worried about work. You're worried about money. You're worried you haven't done anything that matters."

My throat went tight. "Yeah."

"You're doing fine," he said. "Better than fine. He's going to call you every Sunday for the rest of your life. Every Sunday. You don't know that yet." He smiled. "I do."

I had to pull over. I put the hazards on and sat on the shoulder of the road in the golden light, and I cried a little, and nobody said anything. The kid reached forward and patted my shoulder with one small, sticky hand.

After a while, I wiped my eyes and put the car back in drive.

"Okay," I said. "Who wants to pick the music?"

Four hands went up.

We argued about it for the next hundred miles.
