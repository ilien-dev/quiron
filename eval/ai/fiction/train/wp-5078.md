# The Price of the Crown

Ella knew the exact moment everything changed. It was the morning after the coronation, when she woke in the massive canopied bed in a tower that hadn't existed in her childhood village, and she realized that the Prince—now King—was not in bed beside her.

She found him three rooms over, surrounded by ledgers and advisors, already deep in the morning's political machinery. He barely looked up when she entered.

"We need to discuss taxes," he said, still reviewing documents. "The southern region is in rebellion against the increased levies. They're talking about refusing payment."

"But they're already poor," Ella said, remembering the streets she'd walked through every day before her escape into the fairy tale.

"That's precisely why we can't let them stop paying," he replied, as if this was self-evident. "If they succeed in refusing, every region will try the same thing. The budget relies on comprehensive collection."

She stared at him. This wasn't cruelty, she realized. This was something almost worse—this was logic. This was the machinery of power operating exactly as it had been designed to operate, and she'd been so focused on the crown and the escape that she'd never looked beneath the surface to see what it actually rested on.

The marriage unraveled slowly over the next six months. Not because the King stopped loving her—he seemed fond enough in his distant way—but because she couldn't unsee what she'd spent her entire life not seeing.

The servants in the castle weren't treated cruelly by most standards. They had food and shelter and were rarely beaten. But they worked fifteen-hour days for essentially nothing, and if they attempted to leave they were hunted. The system was too stable for beating to be necessary.

The lower classes weren't explicitly forbidden education—they just couldn't afford it. The wealthy controlled the schools through cost, not decree. Cleaner, more efficient.

The merchants in the cities had what appeared to be freedom—they could open businesses and keep small profits. But the taxation system was designed to ensure that no amount of work could elevate them beyond a certain threshold. The King's family owned the largest businesses anyway, conveniently exempt from many regulations they imposed on others.

Ella's father, the man she'd escaped her poverty to find, had been a merchant. Not wealthy enough to own a major enterprise, but comfortable. She'd always assumed he'd just been clever or hardworking. Now she understood: he'd been lucky. The system had a tiny window for people like him, just enough social mobility to create the illusion of possibility for everyone else.

"It's not right," she told the King one evening.

"Right is a moral abstraction," he replied, distracted by official correspondence. "We're discussing function. The country is stable. People have less chaos than they would in alternatives. Resources flow efficiently. This is as good as systems get."

"It's better for you."

"Yes," he admitted, not defensively but as fact. "The people in the positions of power benefit from the systems designed by previous people in positions of power. That's a tautology, not an argument."

She left three days later, walking back out through the castle gates past guards who didn't know whether they should stop her. She had no plan. She had nothing, actually—the clothes she'd been given since arriving, a small amount of money she'd taken from a drawer, the complete absence of any survival skills.

The refugees outside the castle recognized her immediately. The peasant girl who'd married the King, who'd been rescued from poverty into a life of unimaginable luxury. They watched her with something approaching awe as she walked through their camp, completely disoriented.

"My lady," an old woman said. "You've come to help us?"

Ella couldn't answer, because the question was based on an assumption she didn't have the emotional energy to correct.

She stayed in the camp for three weeks, and what she learned in those twenty-one days exceeded the entirety of her palace education. She learned that the "rebellion" in the southern region wasn't actually rebellion—it was starvation being met with incomprehension by people who'd never experienced it. She learned that the tax collectors had authority to seize everything if collection fell short, leaving families with literally nothing. She learned that the children in the camps died regularly from preventable illnesses, that the King's physicians had the knowledge to save them but would never be permitted to share it with people who had no money.

She also learned, in a way that felt almost worse, that there were already people working against the system. Small resistance networks, dangerous and ineffective but present. People who'd never had a crown or a castle to teach them that the system was designed intentionally rather than occurring naturally.

By the end of those three weeks, Ella understood that marrying the Prince had been a narrative trick. She'd been given just enough elevation to see both sides of the divide without having the power to bridge it. The cruelty of the system wasn't her King's fault any more than it was her own—it was structural. Systematic. Built so thoroughly into the function of power that removing it would require dismantling everything.

She couldn't dismantle a kingdom. But she could testify to what she'd seen. She could document the mathematics of poverty that her palace education had taught her made sense, and show how they played out in camps where people had nothing.

She spent the rest of her life writing and speaking and becoming gradually more dangerous to her former husband's regime. They never executed her—that would have made her a martyr. But they ensured she never had a platform large enough to threaten the system.

The fairy tale had lied, she understood finally. The escape wasn't into a better world. It was just into a larger view of the same world. And that view was something she could never unsee, no matter how much she'd given up to have it.
