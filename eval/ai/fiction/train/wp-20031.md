# The Last Game Night

I keep that photo on my desk—all of us crowded around the kitchen table, half-empty bottles of cheap wine, Parker's ridiculous homemade pizza still steaming in the background. It's from college, third year, and honestly it's hard to pinpoint exactly why that night crystallized into something worth remembering while so many others evaporated.

We'd been doing game nights for years by that point, the five of us rotating between apartments, but this particular Friday in October felt different. Nothing dramatic happened. We weren't celebrating anything specific. If anything, we were all exhausted—midterms were coming up, everyone was stressed about jobs and internships, the usual chaos of being twenty-one in a place where your biggest concerns are theoretically trivial but feel absolutely catastrophic.

Maya had brought Cards Against Humanity, which honestly wasn't even a good game, but it became the framework for everything else that night. We played for maybe two hours, but the game almost didn't matter. What mattered was Parker's terrible impressions, the way Rachel would laugh so hard she'd have to excuse herself, how James kept trying to use increasingly nonsensical cards in combinations that technically broke the rules but made us laugh anyway.

Somewhere around midnight, we abandoned the game entirely and just sat there, the bottle of wine now nearly finished, talking about absolutely nothing important. We discussed the pizza, debated which streaming service we should have been using, argued about whether our university's renovated library was an improvement or a disaster. Normal, pointless conversation. The kind you never think twice about.

But here's what I remember most viscerally: the exact quality of the light in that kitchen. The way it fell across Maya's face when she was genuinely laughing, not performing but actually lost in genuine amusement. How comfortable the cheap wooden chair was under me, how James had his feet up on another chair and nobody cared. The temperature of the kitchen, warm from cooking and body heat, the specific smell of old wine and pizza grease and someone's cheap perfume.

We couldn't have known it then, but that was one of the last times we were all together in one place. Parker moved to Seattle two years later. Rachel transferred schools. James got a job in Boston. Maya and I stayed nearby but our lives spiraled into different orbits—successful ones, we like to think, but orbits nonetheless. We FaceTime occasionally. We talk about getting everyone back together, and I think everyone means it when they say it, but we all know it won't quite be the same.

People talk about nostalgia as if it's this syrupy, artificial feeling, but it's not that. It's standing in your own kitchen, alone, making the same terrible pizza on a random Friday because you want to recreate that specific ineffable quality of having nowhere else to be and everyone you loved in the same room at the same time. It's knowing that the game never mattered, that the wine was cheap, that we were all just existing in the same space—and that was enough.

I'm not sure why that night stays with me when better nights, more dramatic nights, more consequential nights have faded. But if I had to guess, it's because of that fundamental sense of presence. We were all there. None of us were performing for social media or worried about the future. It was just five people at a kitchen table, laughing at cards and pizzas and each other, and for one night that was everything.
