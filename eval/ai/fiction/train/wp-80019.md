The first thing I learned was that blood was brighter than roses.

The optometrist had warned me about headaches. He had not warned me that the world would look freshly wounded.

I stood outside his office, lifting the glasses on and off. Without them, the traffic lights were familiar lamps whose positions mattered more than their shades. With them, the top light burned red—a fierce, impossible color—and autumn trees blazed along the avenue.

My wife, Lena, had come with me.

“Well?” she asked.

Her coat was blue. I had always thought it gray.

“You’re beautiful.”

She laughed. Around her shoulders, something laughed with her.

At first I mistook it for glare: a thin shimmer clinging to her outline. Then it turned its head.

It had no face, only a hollow in the air shaped like one. Long fingers rested upon Lena’s collarbones. When she smiled, it leaned close and drank the breath from her mouth.

I tore off the glasses.

Gone.

“What’s wrong?” Lena asked.

“Nothing. Just dizzy.”

On the subway home, I experimented.

Colors were everywhere—advertisements screaming in reds and yellows, green scarves, purple bruises beneath tired eyes. And on nearly every passenger rode one of those things.

A boy’s was small and skittish, whispering into his ear whenever he glanced at his schoolbag. An old man’s crouched upon his chest like a stone idol. A woman in a business suit had three: one covering her eyes, one gripping her tongue, and one curled asleep inside the shadow beneath her seat.

They differed, but they shared a color.

Black, I would have called it before. Yet this was not black. Black was now the polished shoe opposite me, the tunnel outside, Lena’s pupils. These creatures were the color of a door left open at the bottom of the sea.

I looked at my reflection in the window.

Something stood behind me.

It was taller than the others. Its arms wound around my body and disappeared between my ribs. Where its face should have been, hundreds of tiny mouths opened and closed.

I ripped off the glasses so hard the frame snapped at the bridge.

For three days, I refused to wear them.

Then Lena began coughing.

She insisted it was a cold. By Friday, there was blood in the sink.

At the hospital, doctors used careful voices and words like “mass” and “aggressive.” I watched Lena nod as if they were discussing someone else. I held her hand and thought of the thing drinking her breath.

The broken glasses sat in my pocket, repaired with tape.

I put them on.

Colors flooded the consultation room. The doctor’s tie was green. Lena’s blood on the tissue was red.

And the creature upon her had grown enormous.

Its fingers threaded deep into her chest. Its swollen body pulsed in time with her heart. The hollow of its face turned toward me.

One word formed inside it, not spoken but understood.

Hunger.

My own creature tightened its arms.

Its mouths whispered together.

*You cannot save her.*

Perhaps that was true. Perhaps these things were illness, grief, fear—parasites or prophets or merely names given shape. But when I reached for the creature on Lena, my hands did not pass through.

Its flesh was cold and slick.

It shrieked.

The doctor stumbled backward. Lena stared as I wrestled empty air, but I felt claws tearing my sleeves, teeth closing on my palms. My creature whispered louder, every mouth repeating that I was helpless, ridiculous, too late.

So I seized it too.

Pain exploded between my ribs as I pulled.

Something came loose with the sound of a breath held for thirty years.

I dragged both creatures into the light.

They thrashed in my fists, suddenly small as spiders. I crushed them beneath my shoe. Their bodies burst without blood, spilling that impossible color across the floor until sunlight touched it and turned it gold.

Lena’s next scan showed nothing.

The doctors called it a miracle.

Now I wear the glasses everywhere. I see blue skies, green grass, red blood, and the things that feed where no one looks.

Most people carry monsters.

Most monsters can be touched.

And whenever one turns its hollow face toward me, it recognizes the color on my hands.
