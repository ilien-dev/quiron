# Roommates

The lease is in 25-year-old me's name, which the 15-year-old finds hilarious and the 5-year-old doesn't understand at all.

We've been living together for six days now, three versions of the same person crammed into a one-bedroom apartment that was cramped enough for one adult, and I still don't have a working theory for how any of this happened. I woke up Tuesday morning to find a kindergartner sitting on my chest asking if I was the babysitter, and a sullen teenager going through my medicine cabinet with the specific disdain only a fifteen-year-old can generate, and no one — not the universe, not a note, not so much as a pamphlet — has bothered to explain why.

Little Me cries about everything and nothing. She cried because the cereal I bought doesn't have a cartoon mascot. She cried because it got dark outside at a time she didn't personally authorize. She also, and this is the part that actually keeps me up at night, cried for twenty straight minutes on day two because she said she "missed Dad's voice," and I had to sit on the bathroom floor with her while she sobbed about a man neither of us has spoken to in eleven years, and I didn't have the heart to tell her how that story ends, because she's going to have to live through it again regardless of what I say.

Teen Me is worse, honestly, in the way only past versions of yourself can be worse — not cruel exactly, just relentlessly, accurately unimpressed. She looked at my apartment, my job, my grocery list, and said, and I quote, "This is what we end up doing?" like I'd personally betrayed her by turning into someone who owns a French press and pays for a gym membership she doesn't use. She found my journal from college — I don't even know how, I thought I'd thrown it out — and read three entries out loud at dinner in a mocking voice before I physically wrestled it away from her, and the worst part is I couldn't even argue with her assessment of my own poetry. It was bad. I know it was bad. I didn't need to hear it read back to me by a fifteen-year-old with my own face.

But here's the thing nobody warns you about when three versions of yourself end up sharing a bathroom: they know things about you that you've spent years successfully not thinking about.

Little Me still remembers the exact words Mom used to say before bed, the ones I forgot I'd forgotten until she said them, unprompted, over breakfast, in that same singsong cadence, and I had to leave the room. Teen Me still has, word for word, the speech she — we — gave at a friend's funeral sophomore year, the one I can't remember writing and definitely can't remember delivering, because I've spent a decade very deliberately not remembering that whole year. She recited it to me last night, unprompted, staring straight at me the whole time, like she wanted to see if I'd flinch. I flinched.

What I'm starting to understand, six days into this deeply upsetting living arrangement, is that they're not here to annoy me, even though they are extremely good at it. Little Me is carrying grief I apparently buried so deep I forgot it existed. Teen Me is carrying anger I never let myself finish feeling. And 25-year-old me, the one paying the rent, the one who's supposed to be the adult in this apartment — I've just been carrying both of them silently for years, unpaid, uncredited, and calling it being fine.

Last night Little Me fell asleep in my bed because she was scared of the dark, and Teen Me came in around midnight, wordless, and lay down on the other side without asking, and for the first time all week nobody was crying or sneering or reading old poetry out loud. We just lay there, three versions of the same tired person, in the same body's old bed, and I thought, not for the first time this week, that maybe the assignment here isn't figuring out how to get rid of them.

Maybe it's figuring out how to actually let them stay.

I still don't know how long they're here for, or what happens when it ends, whether they just vanish back into wherever they came from or whether something about this arrangement is supposed to change before that's allowed to happen. Nobody's given me a manual. I checked.

But this morning Little Me asked, very seriously, over a bowl of the mascot-less cereal she's decided to tolerate, whether we were going to be okay. And Teen Me, who has not said one kind thing to me in six days, looked up from her phone just long enough to say, quietly, "We will be. Eventually. I've seen how it goes."

I didn't ask her to elaborate. I'm not sure I'm ready. But I let myself believe her, just for the length of breakfast, and that felt like more progress than I'd made in years on my own.
