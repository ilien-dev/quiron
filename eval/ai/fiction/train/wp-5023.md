# Thirty-One Confirmed

The file on Elena Voss ran to three hundred pages, and Kade had read every one of them twice, which was more preparation than he'd given any target in nineteen years of this work, because Elena Voss was not, by any measure the Guild used to rank its assassins, a normal contract.

Thirty-one attempts on her life. Thirty-one failures, spread across four continents and eleven years, executed by some of the most decorated killers the Guild had ever fielded — a woman who drowned her in a locked hotel bathtub and turned around to find her sitting calmly on the bed, toweling off her hair. A man who poisoned every meal she ate for six weeks straight and watched her develop, if anything, a mild seasonal allergy. Someone had detonated an entire apartment building around her. She'd walked out of the rubble asking, according to the surviving report, whether anyone had seen where she'd left her book.

Kade was, by every metric that mattered, the best the Guild had. Forty-three confirmed kills, zero failures, a reputation built on patience so total it bordered on the geological. He did not rush. He did not improvise. He selected a method, studied it until it could not fail, and then, only then, acted.

He watched Elena Voss for six months before he made a single move.

She lived, as far as he could determine, an unremarkable life — a small apartment above a bakery in a city he wouldn't name even in his own private notes, a job cataloguing archives at a museum nobody visited, a habit of walking the same three blocks every evening regardless of weather. Nothing about her suggested a woman who had survived thirty-one professional assassination attempts. Nothing about her suggested she was aware of any of them at all.

That, more than anything in the file, was what unsettled him. Survivors of near-death encounters carried something afterward — a vigilance, a flinch, some visible residue of having looked into the specific abyss of someone trying very hard to kill them. Elena Voss carried nothing. She fed the same stray cat every evening at the same hour. She hummed while she walked.

He chose a method the Guild's archives had no record of ever using against her: nothing dramatic, nothing that announced itself. A compound absorbed slowly through the skin over repeated small contact, undetectable by any screening she was likely to undergo, designed to accumulate quietly over three weeks and then simply, unremarkably, stop her heart in her sleep. He delivered the first dose via the handrail of her apartment stairwell on a Tuesday, and watched from a rooftop two blocks away with something that, in another man, might have been called excitement.

She should have been dead in nineteen days.

On day twenty-two, she knocked on his door.

He had a knife in his hand before he'd consciously decided to move, and she stood in the hallway of a safehouse that existed on no record anywhere, in a city he'd chosen specifically because nothing connected him to it, holding a paper bag from the bakery beneath her apartment, and said, mildly, "I brought pastries. I wasn't sure what you liked, so I got four kinds."

"How did you find this address."

"I didn't, really. I just walked until it felt right, and then I knocked." She said this the way another person might describe finding their car in a parking garage. "You're very good, by the way. The compound was clever. Nobody's tried skin absorption before — normally it's food, or air, or once, memorably, a scorpion someone smuggled into my shower. I only noticed yours because my elbow started itching on a day it had no reason to."

"You should be dead."

"I should be, by most people's accounting, dead about thirty-two times over now, assuming yours counts, which I suppose it does." She held out the bag. "I don't know why I don't die. I want to be upfront about that, since you clearly did excellent research and deserve better than a mystery for an answer. I've stopped trying to figure it out. I mostly just try to live decently in the meantime, in case there's a reason for it I'm supposed to be earning."

Kade lowered the knife slowly, some nineteen-year-old professional instinct screaming at him that this was exactly the kind of disarming performance a genuinely dangerous person would give, and some newer, stranger instinct — formed over six months of watching a woman hum on her evening walks — telling him it wasn't a performance at all.

"Why come here. Why not just live your life and let me fail quietly like the other thirty-one."

Elena considered the question with what looked like real care, setting the pastry bag down on the small table by his door.

"Because you're the first one who watched me for six months before you tried anything," she said. "That felt like it deserved a conversation, at minimum. And because, selfishly, I get lonely. Thirty-one people have tried to end my life and not one of them has ever stayed long enough to just talk to me afterward." She smiled, small and unguarded, entirely unlike anyone who had any business surviving a career of assassins. "So. Croissant, or the one with the almonds?"

Kade stood in his own doorway, weapon still loose in his hand, and found himself, for the first time in nineteen years of flawless, patient, solitary work, with absolutely no idea what came next.
