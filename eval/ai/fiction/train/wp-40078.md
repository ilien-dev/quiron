# Every Drop Counts

The press conference was held at night, which in hindsight should have tipped everyone off.

Gail J. McGovern stood behind the podium at the American Red Cross headquarters in Washington, D.C., in a navy blue suit and a string of pearls. She looked exactly like she always did: composed, professional, a little tired. The kind of executive who testified before Congress and ran blood drives and appeared in disaster zones in a windbreaker with the logo on it.

"Thank you all for coming," she said. "I'll keep this brief."

Then she smiled, and there were fangs.

---

The room went dead silent. Somewhere in the back, a camera clattered to the floor.

"Yes," she said calmly. "They're real. So are we. There are approximately two hundred and forty thousand vampires living in the United States, and I have had the honor of leading our community for the past forty years."

A reporter from CNN raised her hand, very slowly, as if unsure what else to do.

"Ms. McGovern," she said. "Are you telling us that the American Red Cross is—"

"The largest and most successful vampire organization in human history," said McGovern. "Yes."

---

She took questions for two hours.

The Red Cross, she explained, had been founded in 1881 by Clara Barton, who had not been a vampire, and who would have been very upset about all of this. The takeover had happened quietly in the 1950s, when a handful of vampires working in the blood services division realized they had stumbled onto the single greatest idea in the history of their species.

"For thousands of years," McGovern said, "we hunted. We lived in the shadows. We killed people, and we hated it, most of us, and you hunted us back. It was miserable for everyone." She folded her hands on the podium. "And then one day, someone realized: humans will *volunteer*. If you ask nicely. If you give them a cookie and a sticker. They'll line up around the block."

"So the blood drives—"

"The vast majority of donations go exactly where we say they go," she said firmly. "Hospitals. Trauma centers. Surgeries. We have saved millions of human lives. We're very proud of that." She paused. "We simply take a modest administrative fee."

"How modest?"

"About eight percent."

The room murmured.

"For comparison," McGovern added, "that's lower than most charities' overhead."

---

The next morning, the country was in chaos.

Churches held emergency services. Garlic sold out at every grocery store from Maine to California. Senator Hawley called for an immediate investigation. Several people on Twitter claimed they'd always known, and a few of them had, in fairness, been saying it for years.

But there was a strange thing that happened, in the days that followed.

People kept donating.

Reporters staked out blood drives, expecting empty rooms. Instead, they found lines. A retired mailman in Ohio told NBC he'd been giving blood for thirty years and had never once been bitten. A nurse in Houston said the Red Cross had shipped emergency blood to her hospital after Hurricane Harvey faster than anyone else, and she didn't care who was drinking what.

"Honestly," said a college student in Oregon, holding a juice box, "if the choice is vampires hunting people in alleys, or vampires running a really efficient nonprofit, I'll take the nonprofit."

---

A week later, McGovern gave one more interview. The reporter asked her if she was worried about backlash.

"Of course," she said. "People are frightened. They have every right to be. We kept a very large secret for a very long time."

"Why come forward now?"

She looked out the window. It was late, and the lights of the city were spread out below the building like spilled coins.

"Because blood donations have been declining for twenty years," she said. "Young people aren't giving. The supply is dangerously low. I thought, if people knew what was really at stake, if they understood that there are people who literally cannot survive without their generosity..." She shrugged. "It seemed worth the risk."

The reporter stared at her. "You came out as a vampire as a marketing strategy?"

McGovern smiled, fangs and all.

"Donations are up forty percent," she said. "We had to add Saturday hours."
