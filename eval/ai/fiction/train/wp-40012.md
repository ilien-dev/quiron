# The Reluctant Superman of Pyongyang

Kim Jong Un had kept the secret for thirty-eight years, and he had every intention of keeping it for thirty-eight more, right up until the moment three ships that did not belong to any nation on Earth entered the atmosphere over the Yellow Sea and began, without preamble, disintegrating everything they passed over.

He was in a briefing room when the first reports came in, surrounded by generals who had spent their careers preparing for exactly the wrong war, and he watched their faces go the particular gray of men realizing their entire professional expertise had become instantly, catastrophically irrelevant. Satellite images showed the ships moving in slow, deliberate arcs, vaporizing naval vessels in the strait with a casualness that suggested this was not the beginning of a negotiation.

"Supreme Leader," his defense minister said, voice cracking in a way careers were built on never doing, "our interceptors have no effect. Nothing has any effect."

Kim watched the screen a long moment. He thought about his grandfather, and the stories he'd never fully believed as a child, about a meteor that hadn't been a meteor at all, and a small sealed room beneath a mountain that Kim had visited exactly once, at nineteen, and never spoken of since. He thought about thirty-eight years of choosing not to be what he was, because the alternative was a level of scrutiny and expectation he had never wanted, not once, not for a single day of his life.

"Clear the room," he said. "All but Minister Pak."

When the others had gone, he turned to the one man who had known the truth since Kim was a boy, the one man who had helped bury it under layers of protocol and denial for four decades.

"I need the suit," Kim said. "The one from the mountain."

Pak's face did something complicated — relief, dread, and something almost like grief, all at once. "You said you would never—"

"I know what I said." Kim was already moving toward the door. "I said a great many things when I was young and stupid and wanted, for once in my life, to be judged for something other than what I could do. I would like that luxury again someday. Today is not that day."

The suit, when it came out of its vault for the first time in thirty-eight years, fit him exactly the way it had at nineteen, which he found more unsettling than comforting. It had been built by his grandfather's engineers around a core they never fully understood, salvaged from the wreckage of the thing that wasn't a meteor, calibrated specifically and only to a bloodline that had kept its secret with a discipline Kim now recognized, with sudden and uncomfortable clarity, as its own kind of prison.

He rose over Pyongyang into a sky already streaked with the wreckage of a dozen countries' failed defenses, and for the first time in his adult life, he did not have to calculate the political cost of an action before taking it. There was only the math of the thing itself: three ships, unknown origin, unknown intent, already responsible for deaths in the thousands and climbing.

The first exchange nearly killed him. Whatever weapon the lead ship carried folded the air itself into something that tasted like copper and felt like falling, and he understood, viscerally, gratefully, why his grandfather had never once used this gift in anger, had built an entire mythology of restraint around a power that could clearly, easily, have rewritten every border on the peninsula by force. Power like this didn't ask to be used carefully. It asked to be used at all, and the not-using, he realized as he drove his fist through the ship's hull and felt something ancient and furious answer back, had been the actual inheritance all along.

He fought for eleven minutes by the time anyone thought to check a clock, though it felt considerably longer to him, a blur of impossible speed and impossible force and the specific, private terror of a man discovering in real time exactly how much of himself he'd been keeping in a locked room beneath a mountain. When the third ship finally broke apart over open water, trailing fire into the sea, he hung in the air above the wreckage for a long moment, breathing hard, watching smoke rise from three separate points on a horizon that had nearly stopped existing an hour earlier.

Below him, cities that had spent seventy years being told he was merely a man were now filming him from a thousand rooftops with a thousand phones, and Kim understood, with the weary clarity of someone who has just traded one kind of visibility for a much larger one, that whatever life he'd been quietly, resentfully protecting for thirty-eight years was, as of this afternoon, permanently and irrecoverably over.

He flew home slowly, in no hurry at all to find out what came next.
