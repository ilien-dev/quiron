# 1147 Alder Lane

I cracked the fortune cookie open expecting the usual. *You will find happiness in unexpected places.* *A journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step.* Something vague and cheerful and forgettable.

Instead, the slip of paper said:

*1147 Alder Lane.*

That was it. No lucky numbers on the back. No "learn Chinese" phrase. Just an address, printed in the same faded red ink as every other fortune I'd ever read.

"What'd you get?" asked my roommate, Priya, through a mouthful of lo mein.

I showed her.

She frowned. "That's weird. Is that a real street?"

I looked it up on my phone. It was a real street. It was fifteen minutes away, on the east side of town, in a neighborhood of old houses and big trees. The listing showed a small blue two-story with a porch.

"Okay," said Priya. "Don't go there."

"I wasn't going to go there."

"You're going to go there."

I was going to go there.

---

I went on Saturday, around noon, because it seemed like the least murdery time of day.

The house looked exactly like the picture. Blue paint, peeling a little around the windows. A porch swing. A garden out front that had gone slightly wild, tomato plants sprawling over their cages. There was a car in the driveway, an old Buick with a dented fender.

I sat in my car across the street for ten minutes, feeling like an idiot.

Then the front door opened, and an old woman came out onto the porch with a watering can.

She was small, maybe eighty, in a sun hat and gardening gloves. She started watering the hanging plants along the porch rail. She moved slowly, and on the third plant, she had to stop and hold the railing for a minute, catching her breath.

I got out of the car before I'd really decided to.

---

"Excuse me," I said from the bottom of the porch steps. "Hi. This is going to sound strange."

She squinted down at me. "Most things do, at my age. Go on."

I held up the little slip of paper. "I got this in a fortune cookie. It's your address."

She came down the steps, slowly, one at a time, and took it from me. She held it very close to her face. Then she laughed, a surprised, bright sound.

"Well, I'll be," she said. "Golden Dragon, on Fourth Street?"

"Yeah. How did you know?"

"My husband," she said. "Walter. He used to do the printing for them. Forty years. All their menus, their fortunes, everything." She turned the slip over in her fingers. "He used to slip our address into a batch, every now and then. As a joke. He said someday someone would come knocking, and we'd have a new friend."

"Did anyone ever come?"

"Not once." She handed the paper back. "He passed three years ago. I didn't know any were still out there."

I didn't know what to say.

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she looked at the watering can in her hand, and the hanging plants, and the steps.

"Well," she said. "Since you're here. Could you reach those top baskets? My shoulder's not what it was."

---

I watered the hanging baskets. Then she asked if I could help her tie up the tomatoes, which were falling over. Then she made iced tea, and we sat on the porch swing and drank it.

Her name was June. She'd been a schoolteacher. She'd lived in the blue house for fifty-two years. Her kids lived in Oregon and Florida, and they called on Sundays, and she told them she was fine, which was mostly true.

She showed me a photo of Walter. He was standing in front of a printing press, grinning, ink on his hands.

"He'd be tickled," she said. "Absolutely tickled. He always said it would work."

---

I went back the next Saturday. And the one after that.

Priya started coming too. She's better with tomatoes than I am. June taught us how to make her pie crust, which apparently involves vodka. We fixed the gutter. We found a guy to look at the Buick.

---

A few months later, I went back to the Golden Dragon.

I asked the owner, an older man named Mr. Chen, about the fortunes. He smiled and disappeared into the back. When he returned, he had a small cardboard box.

"Walter's last batch," he said. "We still have a few. We put one in, now and then."

I bought the whole box.

Now, sometimes, I leave one on a table at a coffee shop, or tucked into a library book, or on a bus seat.

June's address is still on every one. She says she's ready for company.
