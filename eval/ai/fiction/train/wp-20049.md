The first horn sounded at dawn, and the city came to watch me burn.

They filled Victory Square from the palace steps to the river gates, fathers hoisting children onto their shoulders, vendors selling honey cakes stamped with my face. The baker had given me horns. The real ones, according to the priests, would emerge once the fire purified my disguise.

I stood chained beneath Saint Orlan’s golden statue while the crowd chanted my name.

“Veyra! Veyra! Veyra!”

A villain’s name was the only part of her allowed to survive.

Across the square, Captain Darian Vale climbed the execution platform in polished white armor. The rising sun caught every edge, wrapping him in radiance. The crowd changed its chant.

“Hero! Hero! Hero!”

Darian smiled.

Seven years earlier, he had smiled like that while kneeling in the mud beside me, both of us sixteen and starving, our village burning behind us.

“Do you trust me?” he had asked.

I had.

The Chancellor raised his hands. Silence rolled outward.

“Veyra of the Ash,” he proclaimed, “you stand condemned for treason, regicide, sorcery, and the slaughter at Bellweather.”

A woman near the platform spat at me.

Bellweather. They always saved Bellweather for last.

“Before sentence is carried out,” the Chancellor continued, “our laws permit a final confession. Speak truth now, and perhaps the gods will show mercy.”

The chain at my throat allowed me one step forward.

“The king ordered Bellweather destroyed,” I said.

The square erupted in laughter.

The Chancellor looked almost embarrassed for me. “His late Majesty was beloved.”

“His late Majesty feared rebellion. Bellweather refused the winter tax, so he sent Captain Vale to make an example of it.”

More laughter. Someone threw an apple. It struck my cheek and burst against the post.

Darian did not look at me.

I continued. “Captain Vale barred the gates and set fire to the granaries. When the wind changed, the flames spread to the houses.”

“Lies!” someone shouted.

“Three hundred people died. Darian rode home and said I had summoned fire from the earth.”

That was the genius of a name. Before Bellweather, I had been Veyra the king’s mage, Veyra who healed fever, Veyra who coaxed rain from empty skies. After Bellweather, I became Veyra of the Ash.

Nothing I said could cross that distance.

The Chancellor turned to Darian. “Captain?”

At last, Darian met my eyes.

“I forgive her,” he said.

The crowd sighed as if witnessing a miracle.

Rage tightened my hands against the chains.

The Chancellor nodded solemnly. “Even now, the Hero of Bellweather offers compassion.”

“I offer evidence,” I said.

That quieted them.

Darian’s smile faltered.

Seven years earlier, he had dragged me from our ruined village to the capital. He enlisted. I entered the royal academy. We made a promise in a rented attic room: we would climb high enough to ensure no king could ever burn another village like ours.

Darian climbed faster.

At Bellweather, I found him outside the gates with soot on his face and the king’s sealed orders in his hand.

“Help me,” he begged. “If the truth comes out, he’ll hang us both.”

“Why me?”

“Because you’re the only person I trust.”

I took the order.

I had kept it ever since.

Now I looked toward the statue towering over us. Saint Orlan stood with sword raised, his golden cloak billowing behind him. During the night, while the guards slept, I had used the last whisper of my magic to loosen one stone beneath his feet.

“The evidence,” I said, “is inside the saint.”

Every face tilted upward.

The Chancellor frowned. “Search it.”

Two guards climbed the monument. One reached into the crack and withdrew an oilskin packet.

For the first time that morning, Darian moved without grace.

“Forgery,” he said.

The guard handed the packet to the Chancellor. The seal was broken, but the king’s signature remained, along with the orders to close Bellweather’s gates, burn its food stores, and leave no witnesses.

A murmur passed through the square.

I watched uncertainty enter the crowd like rain soaking dust.

Then Darian laughed.

It was a warm laugh, modest and wounded.

“You see what she is capable of,” he said. “She murdered our king. Is forging his hand beyond her?”

The uncertainty vanished.

Of course.

The crowd knew who he was.

The Chancellor folded the order. “Captain Vale saved this city from the Ash Witch’s army. He carried the prince from the burning palace. He has risked his life for us a hundred times.”

“He murdered the king,” I said.

Darian’s face became very still.

I had not known until the night of my arrest. The king had threatened to reopen the Bellweather inquiry. Darian poisoned him, fired the palace, and carried the prince to safety before anyone knew there was danger.

A perfect rescue, if one arrived early enough to start it.

“I saw him enter the royal chambers,” I said. “Ask Prince Edric. He saw him too.”

All eyes turned toward the young prince seated beneath the palace canopy.

Edric was twelve. His feet did not reach the ground. Darian had saved him from the fire and reminded him of it every day since.

The boy’s lips parted.

Darian placed one hand over his heart.

Edric looked at me, then at the crowd, then at the hero in white armor.

“I saw the witch,” he whispered.

The square exploded in cheers.

The Chancellor did not bother asking whether I had truly been there.

Darian approached as the executioner soaked the wood around my feet. Up close, I could see sweat beneath his collar.

“You almost had me,” he murmured.

“No,” I said. “I wanted them to see.”

“See what?”

“How little truth matters.”

He glanced at the roaring crowd. “Then you chose an expensive lesson.”

The executioner lowered his torch.

I pulled my wrists apart.

The chains fell.

A villain was searched for knives, poisons, charms, and keys. But the guards had not recognized the thin white scar winding around my palms. They had called it an old burn.

I pressed both hands to the platform.

Magic surged through the wood, down the posts, under the stones of Victory Square. Not fire. Memory.

The square vanished.

Everyone stood at Bellweather.

They smelled smoke. Heard fists beating against locked gates. Saw Darian take a torch from a soldier and touch it to the granary door.

Then the palace: Darian tipping poison into the king’s wine. Darian waiting in the corridor while flames climbed the curtains. Darian lifting the prince only after the fire had grown bright enough for witnesses.

When the vision released them, the city was silent.

Darian stared at me. “What have you done?”

I stepped from the unlit pyre.

“Nothing,” I said. “Heroes have nothing to fear from the truth.”

The first stone struck him before I reached the palace steps.

By noon, the vendors were scraping his face from the honey cakes.

By evening, they were selling mine.
