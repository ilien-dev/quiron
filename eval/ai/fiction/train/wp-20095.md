At 6:30 a.m., the machine beside my bed began screaming.

I had been expecting it.

Without opening my eyes, I struck blindly toward the sound. My first blow missed. My second knocked over a glass of water. The third silenced the machine.

Nine minutes later, it screamed again.

So began Monday.

I rose from the wreckage of sleep and crossed the bedroom, navigating hazards laid by my past self: a discarded sock, a charging cable, the corner of a laundry basket. In the bathroom mirror, a man with my face stared back at me as if I had ruined his life.

“We can still call in sick,” he whispered.

I turned on the shower.

The pipes groaned behind the wall. For several seconds, nothing happened. Then freezing water struck me with the precision of an assassin. I performed a brief, involuntary dance before the temperature became tolerable.

Clean, dressed, and only slightly betrayed by existence, I entered the kitchen.

The cat was waiting.

She sat beside her empty bowl, tail wrapped around her paws, presenting the stillness of a monarch before an execution.

“You ate at four,” I said.

Her eyes narrowed.

“I have witnesses.”

She looked toward the darkened window, where dawn had yet to arrive. The message was clear: witnesses could disappear.

I fed her.

My own breakfast required more delicate negotiations. Two slices of bread entered the toaster. Coffee grounds entered the machine. I pressed the button and heard a click, followed by silence.

No coffee came.

I checked the water. I checked the grounds. I pressed the button again. Nothing.

There are moments that reveal a person’s character. Battle. Disaster. A loved one in peril. And then there is discovering, on a Monday morning, that the coffee machine is unplugged.

I plugged it in.

While civilization rebuilt itself, I examined my phone. Overnight, seventeen emails had arrived. Three claimed to be urgent. None were urgent. One was from a company I had never heard of, congratulating me on “taking the first step.”

I deleted it before learning where I was going.

The toaster launched breakfast into the air. One slice landed neatly on the counter. The other vanished.

I searched beneath the table, behind the toaster, even inside the sink. Nothing. Somewhere in the kitchen, a slice of toast had escaped justice.

I ate its companion while standing up.

At 7:42, I put on my coat and began the final inspection.

Wallet: left pocket.

Keys: right pocket.

Phone: hand.

Laptop: bag.

Will to live: pending.

The cat watched from the hallway, already planning what to knock over in my absence.

Outside, rain fell in the thin, spiteful way that seemed designed not to justify an umbrella. The neighbor across the hall emerged at the same time as I did. We exchanged the solemn nod of soldiers assigned to different fronts.

The elevator took forty-three seconds to arrive. Inside, a small mirrored panel gave me one last opportunity to notice the toothpaste on my collar. I did not notice it.

The bus was approaching as I reached the corner.

I ran.

My bag bounced against my hip. My shoes struck wet pavement. The driver saw me, and in that moment we became two people on opposite sides of an ancient moral question.

The doors closed.

Then they opened.

I climbed aboard, breathless, victorious, and tapped my transit card.

Insufficient balance.

Behind me, twelve commuters waited in silence.

The driver sighed and waved me through.

At 8:06 on Monday morning, beneath a gray sky, carrying one piece of toast and wearing toothpaste like a medal, I took my usual seat.

Nothing extraordinary had happened.

I was exhausted.
