# What the Birds See

Every morning at 6:40, Marcus Webb shuffles out onto his back porch in a bathrobe that has seen better decades, sets down a tray of sunflower seeds and stale bread, and mutters something to himself that the sparrows have come, over three years, to recognize as a kind of prayer.

"Nobody needs you," he says, some mornings. "Nobody's going to miss you if you stop showing up." Other mornings it's simpler, just a long tired sigh and the scrape of the tray against the railing, and the birds have learned that the sigh mornings are the ones where he stays out longest, standing at the edge of his porch with his coffee going cold, watching them eat like it's the only appointment on his calendar he still keeps.

The sparrows do not understand English, not really, not the way Marcus means it. But they understand pattern, and rhythm, and the particular tone of a voice that carries something heavy in it, and they have built, over three years of six-forty mornings, an entire private mythology around the man who feeds them.

To the sparrows, Marcus Webb is not a loser. He is not a nobody, not a failure, not any of the words he mutters into his coffee on the bad mornings. To the sparrows, Marcus Webb is the Reliable One — the single most dependable event in a life built entirely around unreliable things: weather, cats, hawks, the whims of a world that owes small brown birds nothing at all. There are eleven species that visit his porch across a given season, and every one of them has, in whatever passes for a bird's private accounting, marked him as a fixed point. A sunrise you can set your hunger by.

The old cardinal who has held territory in the maple for six years — older than any bird has a right to be, scarred along one wing from an encounter with a neighbor's cat that should have killed him — remembers a winter three years back when the seed came late for four straight days, when Marcus didn't come out at all, when the porch light stayed off and the tray sat empty and frosted over. The cardinal doesn't have words for what he felt during those four days, but he has the memory of it, sharp and specific, the particular hollow feeling of a food source gone silent, and beneath that, something closer to worry than a bird should reasonably be able to feel for a man three hundred times his size.

On the fifth day, Marcus came back out, thinner, unshaven, moving like something in him had been cracked and only partially reset, and he'd stood on the porch for a long time before he managed to fill the tray, and the cardinal had come down first, before any of the others, landed on the railing eighteen inches from Marcus's trembling hand, and stayed there. Just stayed. Marcus had gone very still, and something had happened in his face that the cardinal registered without understanding, and after that day, Marcus never missed another morning, not once, not even the week his sister called and he cried on the porch for twenty straight minutes with the tray forgotten at his feet.

The birds don't know why the man thinks so little of himself. They have no framework for divorce, for a career that quietly ended without ceremony, for the specific loneliness of a fifty-one-year-old man in a house too big for one person, cycling through the same six rooms with nobody to interrupt the silence. What they know is simpler and, in its way, more complete: that every single morning, rain or frost or the deep cold that keeps even the hardiest of them fluffed and miserable, a large slow creature with kind, tired eyes comes out onto a wooden platform and ensures that they will not go hungry that day. That in three years, across hundreds of mornings, he has never once forgotten them, even on the mornings he clearly, audibly, believes nobody would notice if he forgot himself.

This morning, Marcus stands at the railing longer than usual, coffee cooling untouched in his hand, and says, very quietly, to no one he expects to be listening, "I don't know what the point of any of this is anymore."

The cardinal lands on the railing eighteen inches away, exactly where he landed three years ago, and does not fly off when Marcus turns to look at him. Behind the cardinal, a dozen sparrows drop out of the maple in a soft flurry of wings, settling onto the tray, unbothered, utterly certain of him, the way you are only certain of the handful of things in this world that have never once let you down.

Marcus watches them eat for a long time, and something in his chest that has been clenched for months loosens, just slightly, just enough, and he does not have the vocabulary to know that eleven species of small tired birds consider him, without qualification, one of the most important beings in their entire world.

But some mornings, standing there, he almost feels it anyway.
