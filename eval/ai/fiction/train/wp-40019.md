# Ride with the Passenger

The highway stretched empty in both directions, the kind of emptiness you only find after midnight on secondary routes. Sarah had been walking for two hours when the vintage Cadillac pulled up beside her, engine purring with an impossible smoothness.

The driver was conventionally attractive in a way that made her immediately suspicious. Too clean, too composed, the kind of person who looked like they'd stepped directly from a tailored photograph. He wore an expensive suit despite it being midnight on a rural road.

"Going far?" His voice had an accent she couldn't place, something European maybe, or something older than that.

"Far enough," Sarah said, which was their first real conversation, the one that should have alerted her to the fact that something was fundamentally wrong with this encounter. But she was tired and cold and her phone was dead, so she got in.

The car smelled like tobacco and something else she couldn't identify, something that made her think of old libraries and ancient stone. The interior was immaculate, leather seats that felt warm even though the engine shouldn't have had time to warm them. The radio played a classical piece she didn't recognize.

"I'm traveling this route heading east," he said. "We can take you as far as you need."

We. She should have caught that immediately. But when she looked back at the mirror, there was nobody else in the car. She looked at the passenger seat, looked at the back seat. Empty.

"Your friends?"

"Oh, they're here," he said smoothly. "Just not in a way you can perceive yet. They will be eventually. Everyone does, given time."

Something in her registered danger then, a primal alarm that made her hand move toward the door handle. It was locked. Not locked by her, and she'd heard no sound.

"Don't be afraid," he said, which was exactly what you'd say if you were someone who genuinely preferred people to be afraid. "I'm just a traveler like you. I pick people up sometimes, hitchhikers mostly. Give them rides."

"Where are we going?"

"East," he said. "I'm always going east. The sun comes up that direction, and I follow it."

She should have been terrified, but something about his tone was almost conversational, like they were having a normal discussion about traffic patterns. The landscape outside had stopped moving, she realized. The kilometer markers were frozen, the same dark tree line visible in every direction.

"Alright," she said, hearing her voice sound distant even to herself. "So who exactly are you?"

He smiled. It was a smile that had probably destroyed lives across centuries, the kind of smile that predators practice in mirrors. "I'm who you believe I am. Your mind is doing that part. Some people see a hitchhiker. Some see a businessman. One woman once thought I was her uncle. That was a long conversation. But eventually, everyone figures out what they're really looking at."

The radio changed stations on its own, now playing something that sounded like Gregorian chanting from some time period before human music was formalized.

"What do you want from me?"

"Want?" He laughed, and the sound echoed across an impossible distance despite the car being tiny. "I'm just giving you a ride. That's what people do. They get picked up, we talk, and something changes. That's the deal with hitchhiking. You get somewhere, I get a conversation. It's simple commerce."

The landscape outside had changed when she wasn't looking. Now it looked like somewhere mythological, mountains and valleys that didn't follow normal physics. The sky had a color she didn't have a name for.

"Some people have refused," he continued conversationally. "They get out. Walk away. Find another road somehow. I respect that. Free will and all that. But most people stay, because deep down they want to know. They want to meet the passengers they can't quite see. They want the meter to run out so they can see what they've earned."

Sarah's hand moved of its own accord and she somehow understood that she was about to make a choice—not in the next second, but in the conversation itself, in the way she responded to whatever came next.

"What happens if I don't want to meet them?"

"Then I pull over at the next town and let you out," he said. "No harm done. You walk into a diner, have some coffee, wonder if you dreamed the whole thing. Happens to some people."

The alternative hung unspoken between them, dangerous and magnetic.

"And if I do want to meet them?"

He smiled again, and the car accelerated into impossible speeds across an impossible landscape, toward an impossible sunrise that moved further away the closer they got to it.

"Then we keep driving east," he said. "And you meet some very interesting passengers."
