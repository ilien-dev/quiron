The highway had been empty since 3:17 a.m., when a milk truck swerved around them and vanished west with its horn screaming.

Now Lucien and Mara stood astride the faded yellow line, facing east.

The desert was colorless before dawn. Saltbush crouched beside the asphalt. Telephone poles marched toward a horizon beginning to pale, and the mountains beyond it looked like the teeth of something sleeping.

Lucien checked his watch.

“Don’t,” Mara said.

“I wasn’t going to say anything.”

“You always say something after checking your watch.”

“The sun is punctual. I respect that.”

“It’s six minutes late.”

“It is not.”

“You see? Something.”

Lucien slipped the watch into his waistcoat. He had dressed formally: black wool, silver cuff links, shoes polished to a mirror shine. Mara wore jeans, a red leather jacket, and one yellow sneaker.

Lucien looked down. “I’ve been meaning to ask.”

“Lost the other one in Prague.”

“You haven’t been to Prague since 1891.”

“Yes.”

“You’ve owned one shoe for a hundred and forty years?”

“It was a very good shoe.”

A gust chased sand across the road. Mara hugged herself.

“Cold?” Lucien asked.

“No.”

“You’re shivering.”

“I’m terrified, idiot.”

“Oh.”

They had spent three centuries pretending fear was something that happened to livestock. Lucien considered taking her hand. He considered not taking it. In the end, Mara took his.

Her fingers were dry and hard as roots.

“What do you think happens?” she asked.

“Combustion.”

“After.”

“Ah.”

“Do you think there’s a hell?”

“For us specifically?”

“For anyone.”

Lucien watched the horizon. “I hope not.”

“That’s unexpectedly optimistic.”

“I’ve met enough priests to know eternity encourages bad management.”

Mara laughed, then covered her mouth as if laughter were indecent at an execution.

“Remember Father Benoît?” she asked.

“The one who tried to drown you in holy water?”

“The very same.”

“You bit off his ear.”

“He kept shouting into it.”

“He tasted of garlic for a week.”

“He was committed to the theme.”

The laughter came again, softer. When it faded, the silence returned larger than before.

A green road sign stood fifty yards behind them: MERCY, 12 MILES.

They had chosen the place because of the sign.

“Why tonight?” Lucien asked.

“We agreed not to interrogate each other.”

“We agreed not to stop each other.”

“Same difference.”

“No. It isn’t.”

Mara released his hand.

“The girl in Tucson,” she said.

Lucien waited.

“She wasn’t supposed to wake up. They don’t, usually. Not while I’m—” Mara touched two fingers to her throat. “But she did. She looked at me. Do you know what she said?”

“No.”

“She asked if I was an angel.”

A thin pink seam opened above the mountains.

Lucien’s skin tightened.

“What did you tell her?” he asked.

“I said yes.”

“That was kind.”

“It was a lie.”

“Those are not mutually exclusive.”

“She died believing death had come for her with blood on its chin.”

“Mara—”

“No. Don’t make it poetic. That’s how we lasted this long. Moonlight. Velvet. Loneliness. We turned appetite into a tragedy because monsters sound prettier when they’re sad.”

“Are we monsters?”

She stared at him.

“Right,” he said. “Stupid question.”

The eastern sky brightened by degrees. Night did not retreat so much as become transparent.

Mara nodded toward his waistcoat. “Your turn.”

“My turn?”

“Why tonight?”

Lucien brushed dust from his sleeve. “I’m tired.”

“You’ve been tired since Napoleon.”

“He was exhausting.”

“Lucien.”

He glanced at the MERCY sign.

“Last month,” he said, “I went back to the house where I was born.”

“I thought it was demolished.”

“It was. There’s a supermarket now. Frozen foods where the nursery was. Lottery tickets where my mother died.”

“I’m sorry.”

“I bought a peach.”

Mara blinked. “A peach.”

“They had a pyramid of them beneath electric lights. I remembered summer. Juice on my hands. Bees in the orchard. My brother throwing pits at me.” He smiled. “I sat in the parking lot until midnight, holding it. It smelled exactly right.”

“Did you eat it?”

“I tried.”

“Oh.”

“It was ashes, of course. Everything is ashes now. Wine. Bread. Peaches. Music, nearly. Even blood.” He looked at her. “I don’t want to keep consuming a world I can no longer taste.”

The pink seam had become a molten band. Birds stirred in the brush, tentative and foolish.

Mara took his hand again.

“We could leave,” she said.

Lucien turned to her.

“Not forever,” she added quickly. “Just tonight.”

“We could.”

“Find a cave. A motel. Steal blackout curtains from one of those awful casinos.”

“We could.”

“Tomorrow night, we go to Mercy.”

“Twelve miles.”

“We could walk.”

“We are standing in the road waiting to die, and you’re worried about stealing a car?”

“I have standards.”

“You ate the inventor of the leaf blower.”

“A public service.”

The mountains acquired edges. Gold touched their highest peaks.

Mara’s grip tightened until the bones in Lucien’s hand creaked.

“Tell me to stay,” she whispered.

He looked at their joined hands, then at the coming light.

“If I do,” he said, “you’ll hate me.”

“I already hate you.”

“True.”

“And if you don’t, I’ll be frightened.”

“So will I.”

She began to cry without tears, her body remembering the shape of weeping though it had forgotten the substance.

The first ray spilled over the mountains.

Smoke curled from Lucien’s cheek.

Pain arrived white and absolute. Mara gasped. Her jacket began to smolder at the shoulders. They clung to each other as the desert erupted into color—red stone, blue shadow, silver brush, a whole impossible world returned in an instant.

Then came an engine.

A rusted pickup crested the hill behind them, brakes shrieking. It stopped sideways across both lanes. The driver’s door flew open.

A small, furious woman in pink pajamas jumped out.

“What the hell are you doing?” she shouted.

Lucien and Mara stared.

The woman yanked a tarp from the truck bed and charged. Before either vampire moved, she threw it over them, plunging the world into hot blue darkness.

They collapsed beneath it, smoking.

“You suicidal morons!” the woman said, beating out flames with her slippers. “My cellar’s ten minutes away.”

Mara coughed. “We’re not going to your cellar.”

“Fine. Burn.”

There was a pause.

Lucien’s ruined lips twitched. “Does your cellar happen to contain blackout curtains?”

“And a freezer full of pig’s blood.”

Mara found his hand under the tarp.

“Lucien?”

“Yes?”

“I still hate you.”

“I know.”

Outside, the woman opened the tailgate.

Together, they crawled toward the dark.
