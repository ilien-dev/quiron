# The Annual Visit

The first time, I was twenty-nine and bleeding out on the shoulder of Route 9, and I thought the tall figure in the black coat was a paramedic.

"Are you ready?" he asked.

I laughed, which hurt. "No," I said. It was a joke. It was the kind of thing you say.

He considered this. Then he nodded, as if I'd told him I wasn't finished with my coffee, and he walked away down the highway until the dark took him.

I woke up in the hospital three days later. The doctors called it a miracle. I called it a misunderstanding.

---

He came back a year later, to the day. I was at a bar with friends, celebrating what they thought was my "second birthday." He sat down on the stool next to me. Nobody else seemed to notice him.

"Are you ready?" he asked.

I had a drink in my hand and a girl across the room I'd been working up the nerve to talk to. "No," I said.

He nodded and left.

I talked to the girl. Her name was Maria. I married her two years later.

---

It became a thing. Every year, on the anniversary of the crash, Death would show up. Sometimes at my house. Once at my office, standing politely by the water cooler. Once in a hospital waiting room, while Maria was in labor with our first.

"Are you ready?"

"God, no. Not today. Look where we are."

He looked. For the first time, I thought I saw something like amusement in the dark under his hood.

He left.

---

The trouble with a thing like this is that it gets harder, not easier.

At forty, I had kids who needed me. No.

At fifty, I had a mortgage and a daughter in college and a son who'd just started talking to me again after a bad few years. No.

At sixty, Maria got sick.

Death came that year and found me in the hospital chapel, which I think he found a little funny. He sat in the pew beside me.

"Are you ready?"

"Are you here for her?" I asked. My voice cracked.

"No," he said. "Only you. I only ever come for you."

"Then no," I said. "She needs me."

She did, for a while. And then she didn't need anything anymore. She went in the spring. I didn't see who came for her. I've wondered since if it was him, and whether he asked her, and whether she said yes.

---

That year, when he came, I was drunk in the kitchen with the lights off.

"Are you ready?"

I almost said yes. I want to be honest about that. The word was right there.

But my granddaughter had drawn me a picture that week. It was stuck to the fridge with a magnet shaped like a strawberry. It was a stick figure of me with enormous ears. I had promised her I'd come to her school play in June. She was going to be a tree.

"No," I said.

He left.

---

I'm eighty-seven now.

My knees are gone. My hearing is mostly gone. I've outlived most of my friends and one of my children, which is a thing no one should have to do. My granddaughter, the tree, has kids of her own. They call me Great-Grandpa Wes and think I'm very old, which I am.

People ask me my secret. I tell them I just keep saying no.

They laugh. They think it's a joke.

---

He came this morning. I was sitting on the porch watching the sun come up over the neighbor's fence, and I heard the steps creak, and there he was. He looked the same as always. He's the only thing in my life that has.

He sat down in Maria's old chair. He'd never done that before.

We sat for a while.

"You're in pretty deep," he said finally. It was the first thing he'd ever said to me besides the question.

"I know."

"Most people don't get this many."

"I know."

"Why do you keep saying no?"

I thought about it. I really did. I thought about Maria, and the tree, and my son at the kitchen table at twenty-three finally saying he was sorry. I thought about how every single year there had been one more thing. Some years it was big. Most years it was small. A play. A picture. A sunrise over a neighbor's fence.

"Habit, I think," I said. "And I guess I keep wanting to see what happens next."

He nodded slowly, like that was the answer he'd been waiting sixty years for.

"And now?"

The sun was fully up. Somewhere inside, my great-granddaughter was asleep on the couch after a sleepover, snoring like a small engine. She'd want pancakes when she woke up. I make the good pancakes. Everyone says so.

I looked at him.

"Ask me after breakfast," I said.

And for the first time in fifty-eight years, Death laughed.
