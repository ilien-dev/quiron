# The Wedding Toast

My grandfather had been asked, very specifically, not to give a toast.

"Keep it short, Pop," my mother had said at the rehearsal dinner. "Or better yet, don't. Just raise a glass. Smile. Sit down."

"Of course," he'd said, in the voice of a man who had already written four pages.

So when the DJ tapped the microphone at my cousin Lily's wedding and asked if anyone else wanted to say a few words, and my grandfather rose from table six like a submarine surfacing, the entire family went quiet in the way that families do when they know exactly what's coming.

He took the mic. He cleared his throat. He adjusted his tie, which had tiny golf clubs on it.

"When I met Lily's grandmother," he began, "it was 1961, and I was working at a hardware store in Dayton, Ohio."

My mother put her face in her hands.

"She came in to buy a hammer. I asked her what she needed it for. She said, 'None of your business.' And I thought, that's the woman I'm going to marry."

A few people laughed. The groom, Daniel, laughed politely, the way you laugh at a new family's jokes before you've learned which ones are dangerous.

"Now, I didn't marry her right away. First, I had to impress her. And back then, you impressed a girl by taking her somewhere nice. So I saved up for three weeks and took her to the finest restaurant in Dayton, which was a steakhouse called The Golden Bull."

"Pop," my uncle said, not quite quietly.

"I'm getting there, Richard."

He was not getting there.

He told us about the drive to the restaurant, in a borrowed Studebaker with no reverse gear. He told us about the maître d', a man named Claude who wore a monocle "for no medical reason." He told us about the menu, and what he ordered, and what she ordered, and how she'd sent her potato back twice.

Around this point, Lily caught my eye from the head table. She was smiling, but it was a hostage smile.

"And then," my grandfather said, "in the middle of dinner, the lights went out."

Everyone perked up a little. This was new. None of us had heard this part.

"Pitch black. Couldn't see my own hand. Somebody screamed. Plates broke. Claude the maître d' yelled something in French. And I reached across the table, and I found her hand in the dark, and I held it."

The room had gotten quiet now, a different kind of quiet. Even the waiters had stopped moving.

"And she squeezed back. And I thought, whatever happens next, whatever this is, I'm not letting go of this hand." He looked over at Lily and Daniel. His voice softened. "That's what marriage is, kids. The lights go out. Things break. You don't always know what's happening. But you find the other person's hand, and you hold on."

Somebody sniffed. My mother lowered her hands from her face. Daniel's eyes were shining. Lily was actually crying now, the good kind.

"That's beautiful, Pop," my mother said.

"Thank you, Carol."

He raised his glass. Everyone raised theirs.

"But what happened?" asked Daniel. "When the lights went out?"

My grandfather paused, glass held high, and looked at him with the calm, bright eyes of a man who had waited sixty-three years for someone to finally ask.

"Well," he said, "that's when the ninja attacked!"
