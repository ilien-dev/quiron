# What They Believed

I wanted to be able to sing.

That's all. That's the whole reason. I know how it sounds.

---

You learn the rules early in a world like this. Everybody does. Kill someone and something passes to you, a single thing, the thing they were proudest of. It's why murder is so carefully policed. It's why the prisons are full of people who suddenly speak perfect French.

What they don't teach you in school, what you only learn by doing it, is that the gift isn't what they were best at. It's what they *thought* they were best at.

My first was a man named Gerald. I chose him because I'd heard him sing at a bar on karaoke night, and he'd been good. Really good. A warm baritone that made the whole room go quiet. I followed him home. I won't describe the rest.

When it was done, I stood in his kitchen and opened my mouth, and nothing special came out. My voice was the same flat, cracking thing it had always been.

But I knew, suddenly and completely, how to make a perfect omelet.

Gerald, it turned out, had been prouder of his omelets.

---

That should have been the end of it. I told myself it was. I got very good at omelets. I tried to take that as the lesson.

But it gnawed at me. Because I'd *heard* him. I'd seen the room go quiet. He'd had the thing I wanted, the thing I'd wanted my whole life, and he'd thought it was less important than eggs.

---

The second was a choir director. Surely, I thought, a woman who'd built her life around music would know her voice was her gift.

She thought her best trait was patience.

I have a great deal of patience now. It doesn't help as much as you'd think.

The third was a street busker. He thought his best trait was that he was "good at reading people." Now I can look at a stranger's face and know exactly what they're afraid of. I didn't want that. I don't like what it tells me.

The fourth was an opera singer, a real one, famous. I was sure. I was certain. She'd trained for thirty years. She'd sung at the Met.

She believed, in her heart, that her best trait was that she was a good mother.

I sat on the floor of her dressing room afterward and I understood, for the first time, what I'd taken. Not a skill. A child's whole world. I could feel the shape of it in me now, a love I had no right to, pointed at a boy I'd never met.

---

I stopped after her.

I'd like to say it was guilt. It was partly guilt. But mostly it was the realization, slow and awful, that the rule wasn't going to bend for me. People who are truly great at something almost never think that's the best thing about them. The singers think they're good friends. The painters think they're good cooks. Everybody's proudest of the thing they worked hardest at being, not the thing that came easily.

The only person who'd believe their voice was the best part of them was someone who didn't have much else.

---

I went to the bar where I'd first heard Gerald. It was karaoke night again. I sat in the back and ordered a club soda and watched.

Around eleven, a girl got up. Maybe nineteen. Thin, nervous, a sweatshirt with the sleeves pulled over her hands. She sang an old song, and she sang it badly, off-key, voice shaking.

And when she finished, the room clapped politely, and she lit up like a lamp. Like it was the best moment of her life. Like it was the only thing she had.

I could read her face. Busker's gift. I knew exactly what she believed about herself.

She believed she could sing.

---

I left before she finished her second song.

I walked for a long time. I ended up at a diner that was open all night, and I went into the kitchen, and I asked the cook if he needed help. He looked at me like I was crazy. Then I made him an omelet.

He hired me on the spot.

I work there now. Nights, mostly. I'm very patient with the customers. I can tell what they need before they ask.

Some nights, when it's slow, I sing while I cook. My voice is still flat and cracking and bad.

I sing anyway. It's the only thing in me that's still mine.
