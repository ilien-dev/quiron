# The Bakery on Carver Street

The soldiers came into town on a Thursday, in the rain.

I remember because Thursday was bread day. My father ran the only bakery on Carver Street, and on Thursdays we made the dark rye that half the town lined up for. I was sixteen, and I was pulling the first loaves out of the oven when I heard the trucks.

We all went to the window. Nobody said anything. The trucks rolled past, one after another, gray and mud-streaked, and the men in the backs of them looked cold and tired and very young.

"Back from the window," my father said quietly. "Keep working."

So we kept working.

---

They took over the town hall the first day. By the end of the week, they'd put up notices on every lamppost in a language most of us couldn't read, with a translation underneath in bad grammar. Curfew at eight. No gatherings of more than five. All radios to be turned in at the town hall.

Mr. Halvorsen, who owned the hardware store, refused to turn in his radio. They took him away on a Tuesday. He came back on Friday with his arm in a sling and didn't talk to anyone for a long time.

After that, everyone turned in their radios.

---

What I didn't expect was how ordinary it would become.

That's the part nobody warns you about. You'd think an occupation would be all fear, all the time. And there was fear. There was always fear. But underneath it, life went on, stubbornly, the way grass comes up through cracks in a sidewalk.

We still made bread. People still bought it. My little sister still went to school, where the teachers now had to teach from new books. Mrs. Pell still hung her washing out every Monday, soldiers or no soldiers, and glared at any of them who walked too close to her sheets.

And the soldiers, it turned out, also needed bread.

---

The first one came in about three weeks after they arrived. He was maybe twenty. He stood in the doorway with his rifle slung over his shoulder and looked at the shelves like he wasn't sure he was allowed.

My father went very still behind the counter.

The soldier pointed at the rye. He held up two fingers. He put coins on the counter, the right amount, in our money.

My father wrapped two loaves in paper and handed them over. The soldier nodded, said something that might have been thank you, and left.

"Papa," I said. "Why did you—"

"Because he paid," my father said. "And because I'm a baker, Anika. That's what I do."

He didn't look at me when he said it. I knew he hated it. I knew he'd hated every second.

---

The young soldier kept coming back. Every Thursday, for the rye. After a while, he started saying a few words in our language. *Good morning. Thank you. Very good bread.* He had a round, freckled face and his uniform was too big for him.

One day he pulled a small photograph out of his pocket and showed it to my father. A woman in an apron, standing in front of a shop with loaves in the window.

"Mother," he said, tapping it. "Baker. Also."

My father looked at the photograph for a long moment. Then he handed it back and went into the kitchen without a word, and I heard him out there, for a long time, just standing still.

---

What I knew, and the soldier didn't, was what went out the back door on Thursday nights.

Bread, mostly. Loaves for the families whose fathers had been taken. Loaves for the people hiding in Mr. Halvorsen's cellar. And sometimes, rolled up tight and baked into the bottom of a loaf of rye, a small slip of paper with information on it: truck schedules, numbers of men, which roads they were using.

My father never told me who took the bread. I never asked. I just helped him knead it.

---

In March, the young soldier came in on a Wednesday instead of a Thursday.

He didn't have his rifle. He looked pale. He stood at the counter and didn't point at anything.

"Tomorrow," he said, in his careful, broken words. "Do not bake. Stay home. Please."

My father stared at him.

"Please," the soldier said again. He looked at me. Then he turned and left.

---

We didn't bake the next day. We stayed in the cellar with the doors locked, my father and my sister and me, and around noon we heard the gunfire start, down by the town hall. It went on for hours.

When it was over, the town was ours again. The resistance had come down out of the hills in the night. Half the soldiers had surrendered. Some of them hadn't.

I never saw the young soldier again. I don't know if he surrendered, or ran, or was one of the ones who didn't.

---

My father reopened the bakery the next Thursday. People lined up down the block. It was the longest line I'd ever seen.

He baked the rye. He sold every loaf. And that night, after we'd closed, he wrapped one loaf in paper and set it on the counter by the door, and left it there overnight.

He never explained it. He did the same thing every Thursday until the day he retired.
