# Margin Notes

Every morning at 7:15, Delia Park wakes up, and every morning she knows exactly why.

It isn't the alarm. The alarm is set for 7:30. She wakes at 7:15 because somebody, somewhere, needed her to have fifteen minutes of quiet before the day started. She can feel it the way you feel a draft from an open window. There's a reason for the quiet. There is always a reason.

She lies there and looks at the ceiling, which has a water stain shaped roughly like Ohio. She's never been to Ohio. She suspects the stain is there because the writer liked the image and then forgot about it, because nobody has mentioned Ohio since.

"Okay," she says to the room. "What are we doing today?"

Nothing answers. Nothing ever answers. That's one of the rules, as far as she can tell.

She gets up and makes coffee. The coffee maker is old and makes a gurgling noise, which she has come to understand is characterization. It says something about her. It says she doesn't replace things until they break. It says she's a little stuck. She's thought about buying a new one just to see what would happen, but she's afraid the whole kitchen would go blank, the way the corners of her apartment go soft and gray if she looks at them too long.

Nobody ever describes the corners.

At 8:02 she leaves for work. She takes the same route she always takes, past the bakery with the blue awning and the man who sells newspapers even though nobody reads newspapers anymore. He waves. She waves back. She once tried to ask him his name, and he opened his mouth and nothing came out, and then a bus went by very loudly, and when it passed he was smiling at her as if the question had never happened.

She didn't ask again. It felt cruel.

Work is at a small insurance office. She processes claims. She has figured out that her job is boring on purpose. It's there so that when something happens, it'll matter more. She's been waiting for that something for what feels like a long time, though she honestly can't tell how long. Days blur. There are whole weeks she remembers only as a sentence: *The next few weeks passed without incident.*

She hates that sentence. She'd like those weeks back.

Around noon her coworker Sam leans over the cubicle wall. Sam is funny and a little too interested in her. She knows he's a love interest because every time he talks, the light in the office does something warm and specific, and she notices his hands.

"Lunch?" he says.

"Can I ask you something weird first?"

"Always."

"Do you ever feel like you're being written?"

Sam blinks. For a second, just a second, she sees it in his face, a small flicker of panic, like a man who has heard a noise in the attic. Then it's gone.

"Like a diary?" he says.

"Sure," she says. "Like a diary."

They go to lunch. She orders a turkey sandwich. She could order anything, technically. But she has a feeling the sandwich doesn't matter, and she's learned to save her rebellions for things that do.

Here's what she's figured out, over all these mornings. She can't leave the story. She's tried. She got on a train once and rode it to the end of the line, and the end of the line was a platform, and past the platform was nothing, not darkness exactly, just a lack of description. She sat on a bench there until she fell asleep, and she woke up at 7:15 in her own bed.

But she can choose how she feels about it. Nobody writes that part down all the way. There's always a little room in the margins.

On the walk home, she stops at the bakery with the blue awning. She's never gone in. She buys a lemon tart, and the woman behind the counter hands it over with a smile that goes all the way to her eyes. It's the most detailed smile Delia has seen in months.

She eats the tart on the sidewalk. It's very good.

Maybe that's the story, she thinks. Maybe nothing big is coming. Maybe somebody out there is just trying to write a woman who notices things.

She looks up at the sky, at the soft gray place where the sky stops being specific.

"Thanks for the tart," she says.

A breeze comes down the street, warm for October. She decides to take it as a yes.
