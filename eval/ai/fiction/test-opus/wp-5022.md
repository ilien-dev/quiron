# Harvest Moon

I woke up in a bed that smelled like hay, and a voice in my head said, very calmly:

**Welcome. To return home, you must complete the game.**

I sat up. Wooden walls. A single window with checkered curtains. A small TV on a dresser, and outside, the sound of a rooster.

"No," I said out loud. "No, no, no."

Because I knew exactly where I was. The last game I'd played, at two in the morning, on my couch, eating cold pizza, was *Stardew Valley*.

---

At first I was relieved. It could've been so much worse. It could have been *Dark Souls*. It could have been *Resident Evil*. My roommate had been playing *Doom* earlier, and I'd almost picked up the controller.

Stardew Valley was fine. Stardew Valley was a farming game. The worst thing that happened in Stardew Valley was a slime in the mines.

"Okay," I said. "Voice. What does 'complete the game' mean?"

**Complete the game.**

"Right. But Stardew doesn't really end. There's no final boss."

**Complete the game.**

I got out of bed. There was a parsnip seed packet on the table and a rusty hoe leaning against the wall. Outside, the farm stretched away in every direction, completely covered in rocks, weeds, stumps, and trees.

I had a very bad feeling.

---

It took me three days to figure out what "complete" meant. It meant everything.

It meant the Community Center, all of it, every bundle. It meant catching every fish. It meant shipping every item. It meant finding every artifact and every mineral, reaching the bottom of the mines and the Skull Cavern, befriending every single villager to max hearts, and cooking every recipe.

It meant, God help me, perfection.

I've seen people online talk about getting 100% perfection in Stardew Valley. It takes them hundreds of hours. And those are people with a game clock, where a day lasts fourteen minutes and you can go to bed and skip the night.

My days lasted a day.

---

Here's what they don't show you in the game.

Clearing a field with a basic hoe is physical labor. Actual, back-destroying labor. The first week, I cleared about nine square feet and cried in the bathtub.

Watering crops by hand takes hours. The watering can doesn't refill by magic. You walk to the pond. You walk back. You do it again.

Fishing is not a minigame with a little green bar. Fishing is sitting on a dock for eight hours, getting sunburned, catching nothing, while a man named Willy tells you about the ocean.

And the villagers. In the game, you give someone a gift and their heart meter goes up. In real life, when you give Pierre a daffodil for the fortieth straight Tuesday, he starts to look at you with deep concern.

---

I'm on year three now.

My farm is actually pretty great. I've got sprinklers, finally. I have a barn with cows who know my name. I built a greenhouse. I'm a genuinely skilled farmer, which is a sentence I never expected to say about myself.

I've finished the Community Center. I've got ten hearts with everyone except Shane, who is making it very difficult, and Linus, who I think is afraid of me because I keep digging through his trash can.

I have caught every fish except the Legend. I've been trying to catch the Legend for two springs.

---

This morning, I walked down to the mountain lake before dawn, in the rain, and cast my line.

**Progress: 97%,** said the voice.

"I know," I said.

I sat down on the wet grass. The lake was gray and still. Somewhere behind me, Robin's saw was going. Sebastian was probably still asleep. Abigail would be heading out to the mines later, and I'd promised to go with her.

"Hey, voice," I said. "What happens when I hit a hundred?"

**You return home.**

I thought about home. My apartment. The couch. The cold pizza. My job doing data entry for an insurance company in a cubicle with no window.

I looked at my hands. They were brown now, and callused, and strong.

"And if I don't catch the fish?"

The voice was quiet for a long time. Then it said:

**There is no time limit.**

The rain got a little lighter. Something moved in the lake, deep down, something big.

I reeled my line in, very slowly, and went home to feed the cows.
