# The Song in the Stone

The lamp was down to its last inch of oil when Mara heard it.

At first she thought it was the water. The deep caverns were full of sounds: drips, trickles, the long low groan of rock settling over centuries. She'd been down here four days, and she'd stopped trusting her ears somewhere around the second.

But this wasn't water. Water didn't have a melody.

She stopped walking. The sound came again, faint, drifting up from somewhere ahead and below, where the tunnel narrowed and bent.

*Hush now, little stone, the mountain's asleep...*

Her breath caught. She knew those words. She knew that tune. It went up on *stone* and down on *asleep*, a little dip at the end like a stair you weren't expecting.

Her mother used to sing it. Her mother had made it up.

---

Mara had come down into the caverns looking for her brother.

Tomas had been a miner, like their father, like their grandfather. Six months ago there'd been a collapse on the lower levels. Eleven men had gone in. Nine had come out. The company had sealed the shaft and paid the families and put up a small brass plaque, and that was supposed to be the end of it.

But Mara had never believed he was dead. She'd dreamed about him every night since, the same dream: Tomas sitting in a dark place, not afraid, just waiting. So she'd packed a lamp and a rope and bread and gone in through the old abandoned entrance on the north side of the mountain, the one the children dared each other to touch.

Everyone in the village thought she'd lost her mind. She'd thought so too, sometimes.

---

*Hush now, little stone, the mountain's asleep,*
*the river runs quiet, the roots run deep...*

She followed the song.

The tunnel narrowed until she had to turn sideways. Then it opened, suddenly, into a chamber so large her lamplight didn't reach the ceiling. The floor was smooth and dry. The walls glittered faintly, veins of something pale running through the dark rock.

The singing was louder now. It filled the chamber. It seemed to come from the walls themselves.

"Hello?" Mara called. Her voice cracked. "Who's there?"

The song stopped.

In the silence, she heard breathing. Slow, and deep, and far too large to be human.

---

She should have run. Later, she would wonder why she didn't.

Instead, she lifted the lamp higher, and the light caught on something across the chamber. A shape against the far wall, curled and still, the color of the rock around it. For a moment she thought it *was* rock, a formation, a ridge of stone.

Then it opened one eye. It was the size of a cart wheel, and gold, and very old.

"You know the song," it said. The voice was low, gravelly, and it made the dust on the floor tremble.

"My mother wrote it," Mara whispered.

"No," the creature said, and there was something almost gentle in it. "Your mother heard it. When she was a child, she wandered too far into these tunnels, and she fell asleep near here, and I sang to her until the searchers found her. She must have remembered."

Mara's knees gave out. She sat down hard on the cold stone floor.

"Mama never told me that."

"Perhaps she thought it was a dream. Most of them do."

"Most of who?"

The great gold eye shifted, and looked past her, toward a darker corner of the chamber. Mara turned the lamp.

There, curled on a bed of dry moss, fast asleep, was Tomas.

He was thin. His beard had grown out wild. But his chest rose and fell, steady and even, and his face was calm.

"He came through in the collapse," the creature said. "He was hurt. His leg. He could not walk out, and I could not carry him through tunnels made for your kind. So I sang to him, so that he would not be afraid, and I waited. I thought someone would come."

Mara crawled over to her brother and took his hand. It was warm.

"Why didn't you get help? Go to the village?"

The creature made a sound, low and rumbling, that might have been a laugh.

"Child. Look at me. Would they have listened?"

---

It took two days to get Tomas out. The creature showed her a passage, wider and easier, that led up to the surface near the river. She never saw it move. It simply told her the way and then lay still again while she half-carried, half-dragged her brother toward the light.

At the entrance, she looked back once.

From somewhere deep in the mountain, faint and slow, she heard it singing again.

*Hush now, little stone...*

She sang the rest under her breath the whole way home.
