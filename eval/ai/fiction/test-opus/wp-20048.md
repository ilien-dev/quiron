# The Tuesday Before

The machine only went backward, and it only went once.

Harold Brenner had spent eleven years and most of his pension building it in the garage, and when it finally worked, it did one thing: it sent a person back to a single moment of their choosing, with no way to return. You didn't change history. You didn't meet yourself. The physics wouldn't allow it. Once you arrived, you simply lived forward from there, in a world that had always had you in it.

"So it's a one-way ticket," said his daughter, Anna, when he showed her.

"It's a one-way ticket."

"And you can't go back and stop anything? Stock market, lottery, any of that?"

"You can try," Harold said. "But you arrive as a stranger. Nobody knows you. The version of you that lived there already is still there. You'd just be an old man with a head full of things nobody would believe."

Anna looked at the machine for a long time. It wasn't impressive. It looked like a phone booth someone had built out of an old refrigerator and a lot of copper wire.

"Then why build it?" she asked.

Harold didn't answer that day.

---

He answered six months later, on a Sunday, when he sat her down at the kitchen table and told her he was going.

"Dad."

"I've thought about it."

"Where? When?"

"April 14th, 1979. A Tuesday."

She knew the date. Everyone in the family knew the date. It was the day her grandmother, Harold's mother, had taken the bus to the hospital alone and been told she had six months. She had died that October. She had never told anyone about that appointment until weeks later. She'd sat in the waiting room by herself.

"You can't save her," Anna said quietly. "You said so."

"I know. I'm not going to save her."

"Then what?"

Harold turned his coffee mug around on the table, a quarter turn at a time.

"I'm going to sit next to her in the waiting room," he said. "That's all. She won't know who I am. I'll just be some old man. But she won't be by herself."

Anna started to argue and then didn't. She put her hand over his.

"And after?"

"After, I'll live. I'll be seventy-one in 1979. I've got some savings converted to old bills. I'll get a little apartment. I'll watch the Pirates win the World Series again." He smiled. "I'll probably see myself around town, a young idiot with a mustache. I'll leave him alone."

"You'll miss everything here."

"I'll miss you," he said. "That's different."

---

The machine hummed. Anna stood in the doorway of the garage, arms crossed, crying without seeming to notice she was doing it.

"Write me a letter," she said. "Put it somewhere. Somewhere I'll find it."

"I already did," Harold said. "Check under the floorboard in the attic. The loose one. I'll put it there in 1981."

He stepped inside and closed the door.

There was no flash. There was no sound. The hum stopped, and when Anna opened the booth, it was empty, and smelled faintly of ozone and her father's aftershave.

---

The hospital waiting room in 1979 was mostly orange. Harold had forgotten that. Orange chairs, brown carpet, a television bolted high on the wall playing a game show with the sound off.

She was there, exactly where he knew she'd be. Third chair from the window. Blue coat. Her purse held in both hands on her lap like it might try to leave.

She was younger than him now. That was the strangest part.

Harold sat down one seat away. He didn't say anything for a while. Then he said, "Long wait today."

She looked over. Polite, guarded. "It always is."

"You here for yourself?"

"Just some tests." She paused. "You?"

"Just keeping somebody company," he said.

She glanced around the room, maybe looking for whoever that was. When she didn't find anyone, something in her face softened, like she'd decided he was a lonely old man and she could afford to be kind to him.

They talked about the weather. About the Pirates. About her son, who she said was a good boy but didn't call enough. Harold laughed so hard at that she looked at him funny.

When the nurse called her name, she stood up and smoothed her coat.

"Would you," Harold said, and stopped. "Would you mind if I was still here when you came out?"

She studied him. Then she nodded.

He was there when she came out. She'd been crying. He didn't ask. He just walked her to the bus stop and waited with her until it came.

---

Forty-seven years later, Anna pried up the loose floorboard in the attic.

The envelope was yellowed and soft. Inside was one sheet, in her father's handwriting.

*She was fine at the bus stop. She held my arm. I think it helped. The Pirates won. I'm happy. Don't wait for me. I'm already here.*
