# Just Lovely

I was standing in the cereal aisle of the Hy-Vee, trying to decide between two kinds of granola that were basically the same, when Linda Hoffstetter touched my elbow.

Linda from church. Linda who ran the bake sale. Linda whose husband had the boat. I knew her the way you know a lot of people in a town this size, which is to say I knew her face and her car and roughly how she felt about the new stoplight on Main.

"Oh, Tom," she said, beaming. "I saw your gravestone today. Just lovely!"

Then she patted my arm, put a box of Raisin Bran in her cart, and walked away toward the dairy section.

I stood there holding the granola.

---

My first thought was that I'd misheard her. My second thought was that she'd confused me with somebody else, some other Tom. There were at least three. Tom Reilly had just had his knee replaced. Maybe she meant his gravestone. Maybe she was confused about which surgeries were fatal.

I put both kinds of granola in my cart and followed her.

She was comparing yogurts when I caught up. "Linda," I said. "Sorry. What did you say back there?"

"Hm?" She looked up, bright as ever. "Oh! Your stone. Out at Fairview. I was visiting Harold and I walked past it. It's gorgeous, Tom. That gray granite. And the little carved oak tree. Very tasteful. Did Karen pick it out?"

Karen is my wife. Karen is very much alive and was, at that moment, at home, probably wondering why I was taking so long to buy granola.

"I don't have a gravestone," I said.

Linda laughed and swatted my arm. "Well, somebody went to a lot of trouble for you, then." She dropped a strawberry yogurt into her cart. "Tell Karen I said hi."

---

I didn't go home. I drove straight out to Fairview.

It's a small cemetery, on a hill past the grain elevator. I've been there for a few funerals. I knew roughly where Harold Hoffstetter was buried, near the big maple, and I walked the rows around him in the late afternoon light, feeling ridiculous.

And then I stopped feeling ridiculous.

Gray granite. A little carved oak tree in the corner. My name, spelled correctly, middle initial and everything.

THOMAS J. BECKER

The birth date was right. The death date was blank. Underneath, in smaller letters, it said: *He always came home.*

I stood there a long time.

---

When I got home, Karen was on the couch with a book. She looked up.

"You were gone forever. Did they run out of granola?"

I sat down across from her. "Karen. Did you buy me a gravestone?"

She went very still. Then she put the book down, carefully, pages open, like she didn't want to lose her place.

"Who told you?"

"Linda Hoffstetter. In the cereal aisle."

"Oh, for God's sake, Linda." She rubbed her face. "It was supposed to be for our anniversary."

"A gravestone was supposed to be for our anniversary?"

"No. Well, yes. Both of them." She sighed. "Mine's right next to yours, Tom. Did you not look to the left?"

I hadn't looked to the left.

"I got a deal on the plot," she said. "And after your dad passed and we had to do everything in a rush, I just thought, I don't ever want our kids to go through that. So I did it. Two stones. Side by side. Oak tree on yours, because of the one you planted in the backyard the year Emma was born. Mine has a bird."

"And 'He always came home'?"

She shrugged, a little embarrassed. "You did. Every night. Thirty-one years."

I didn't say anything for a minute. Then I got up and sat next to her on the couch.

"What does yours say?" I asked.

She smiled. "Go look."

---

I went back the next morning. Hers was just to the left of mine. Same gray granite. A little sparrow carved in the corner.

Underneath her name, it said: *She waited up.*

On the way home I stopped at the Hy-Vee. Linda was there again, somehow, by the bananas.

"Linda," I said. "You were right. They're lovely."

"Oh, I know," she said, and walked away.
