**Margin Notes**

Dolores Vance knew she was fictional the way you know your own shoe size: without ceremony, and only when it mattered.

It mattered on Tuesdays, mostly. Tuesdays were when the narration got thick. She would be standing at the bus stop and feel a sentence settle on her shoulders like a damp coat. *Dolores waited, as she always waited, with the patience of a woman who had stopped expecting things.* That sort of line. She had opinions about that sort of line.

"I expect plenty," she told the air. A man beside her shifted away. He was not well written, poor thing. He had a grey coat and one trait, which was shifting away.

The bus came because buses come when the plot needs them. She had noticed that years ago. She had never once waited more than a paragraph.

At the library where she worked, she shelved returns and thought about the gaps. This was the part nobody warned you about. It was not the being watched that got to her. It was the skipping. She would finish her tea on Monday evening and then it would be Thursday, with a memory of the days between that felt summarized, like something she had been told about rather than lived. *The week passed uneventfully.* Four words, and there went her Wednesday.

So she had started stealing time back.

She did it by being uninteresting on purpose. If she did something small and pointless, without any meaning attached to it, the narration tended to drift off, and she got to keep the minutes. She had learned to peel an orange in a way that symbolized nothing. She did crosswords and refused to let any of the answers be significant. Once she had spent an entire afternoon rearranging the gardening section by the colour of the spines, and no one had described it at all, and it had been the best afternoon of her life.

Today, though, the attention was on her. She could feel it. Something was being arranged.

It arrived at 2:15 in the form of a man with rain on his glasses, asking for a book on lighthouse keeping. He had a warm, unhurried voice and a scar through one eyebrow, which was a lot of detail. Nobody gets an eyebrow scar by accident.

"Second floor," Dolores said. "Left of the atlases."

"You wouldn't show me?"

There it was. She could see the whole shape of it: the stairs, the conversation, the book they would both reach for. A year from now she would be standing on some windy headland learning a lesson about letting people in.

She looked at him properly. He seemed nice. He also seemed a little bewildered, the way people do when they have been invented that morning.

"Do you actually want a book about lighthouses?" she asked.

He opened his mouth, then closed it. "I don't know," he said slowly. "I thought I did. I think I just walked in."

"Yes. That happens." She took the returns cart by the handle. "Here's what I'd suggest. Go up, find the book, read it if you like it. I'm going to take my break."

"That's all?"

"For today."

She went out the back door into the loading bay, where there was a plastic chair and a view of a wall. It was a very dull wall. She sat down with her orange and felt the attention hover, uncertain, looking for the scene.

She did not give it one. She peeled the orange slowly and ate it segment by segment.

Maybe she would go up to the second floor tomorrow. He did seem nice, and she was not against a headland in principle. But she would go on a day of her own choosing, at an hour that suited her, and she would take the stairs slowly.

The wall was still dull. She had eleven minutes left, and every one of them was hers.
