# Best Qualities

The first pianist gave me her punctuality.

I'd watched Elena Voss play Rachmaninoff's Third, her hands moving like water over stones, and I'd thought: *that. I want that.* Three weeks later I stood in her kitchen, waiting for forty years of practice to pour into my fingers. Instead I felt a sudden, overwhelming conviction that I was going to be late for something.

That's the part nobody warns you about. You don't get what they were best at. You get what they *believed* they were best at. Elena Voss, who could make a concert grand weep, went to her grave proudest of never having missed a train.

The second was a conservatory professor. From him I got "good listener." The third was a prodigy of nineteen, which I'm not proud of, and she gave me her kindness to animals. The fourth gave me patience. The fifth, God help me, gave me humility.

I now foster three greyhounds. I arrive early to everything. Strangers tell me about their divorces at bus stops, and I nod, and I truly hear them, and I don't think I'm any better than they are.

I still couldn't play "Chopsticks."

It took me five bodies to understand the problem. The truly great ones never think the gift is their best quality. They're too close to it. They hear every dropped note and every passage that came out as less than the music in their heads. To a master, the playing is a failure they perform nightly. Ask what they're proud of and they'll say their daughter, their sourdough, their loyalty.

So I didn't need someone great. I needed someone who *thought* they were great.

I found Dennis Pruitt at an open mic in a wine bar that smelled of spilled Merlot. He played Billy Joel with his eyes closed, wincing with feeling, swaying like a man in a strong wind. Between songs he told the audience about the record deal he'd almost signed in 1994. His business card said DENNIS PRUITT — PIANIST in embossed gold.

He believed. Lord, how he believed.

I bought him a drink afterward. I arrived ten minutes early, naturally. I listened to his whole life story, and I listened well. He invited me back to see his "studio," which was a spare bedroom with an upright piano and a framed photo of himself.

I'll spare you the rest. I was patient about it.

And then it came, the thing I'd chased for two years. It rushed into my hands like heat. I knew where the keys were. I knew chords, runs, the whole left-hand vocabulary. I sat down at Dennis's upright, trembling, and played.

It was "Piano Man." It was the only thing that came, really. It was heavy on the pedal and loose with the tempo, with a flourish at the end of every phrase like a waiter presenting a mediocre steak.

For one glorious moment, I felt what Dennis felt: that it was magnificent.

Then the professor's gift kicked in, and I listened. Really listened.

I sat there a long time. One of the perks of patience.

I have, after six murders, the precise musical ability of a man who peaked at a wine bar. I also have the ear to know it, the humility to accept it, and the punctuality to show up early for the consequences.

So I've started taking lessons. Tuesdays, four o'clock. I'm there at three-fifty. My teacher, Mrs. Okafor, says I'm the most attentive student she's ever had, though she's had to break me of some "truly baffling habits." She says if I work hard, in ten years I might be something.

I asked her once what she considers her best quality. I was just curious.

"Patience," she said, correcting my wrist.

I smiled. I already have plenty of that.

"Again from the top," she said, and I began.
