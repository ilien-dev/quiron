**Not Yet**

The first time, I was forty-one, face down on my kitchen floor with a heart that had quit mid-beat.

He was shorter than I expected. He crouched beside me, robe pooling on the linoleum, and asked, "Are you ready to go?"

"No," I said, because I thought I was hallucinating, and because I've always been a smartass.

He nodded, made a small mark in a ledger, and left. My heart started up again like a lawnmower on the third pull.

I told nobody. Who would I tell?

He came back the next year, same date, March ninth. I was in the grocery store, holding a cantaloupe. Everyone else froze mid-step. He asked again.

"No?" I said.

Another mark. Gone. The cantaloupe was fine.

For the first decade it felt like a cheat code. I ate what I wanted. I took up motorcycles. I figured I had a loophole and I intended to live in it.

By ninety, I understood the loophole didn't cover anything but the dying. My knees went. My eyes went. My wife, Dolores, went, properly, on a Tuesday in October, and he came for her without asking me anything at all. I screamed at him in the hospital corridor. He looked at me with something like embarrassment.

"She said yes," he told me. "People usually do."

That March, I almost said it too. I had the word in my mouth. But my granddaughter was pregnant, and I wanted to see the baby.

"No," I said.

There is always a baby, you see. That's the trap. There is always a graduation, a wedding, a final season of something, a tomato plant that hasn't come in yet. Nobody tells you that life doesn't run out of reasons. It only runs out of body.

I'm a hundred and thirty-four now. I have outlived my children, which I don't recommend, and two of my grandchildren, which is worse. The newspapers stopped calling years ago, when I stopped being a marvel and started being unsettling. I live in a care home where the staff rotate out before they can do the math on me. I can't walk. I can barely hear. Food tastes like wet paper.

And every March ninth, the clock stops and he sits down in the visitor's chair.

We've gotten familiar. He brings the ledger, but he hardly looks at it anymore. Sometime around year sixty he started staying a few minutes. Around year eighty he started asking about my week.

"You know," he said last year, "you're the only one who ever said it."

"Said what?"

"No. Everyone thinks it. They beg, they bargain, they offer me things. Chess, for some reason. But when I ask the actual question, they're so frightened they just nod." He turned a page that didn't need turning. "It was always a question. I don't think anyone built it to be answered that way, but it was always a question."

"So I could do this forever."

"You could."

"Would you let me?"

He was quiet for a while. "I've been doing this a very long time," he said. "You're the only appointment I have that I don't dread."

That one sat with me all year. I'd always thought I was the one who couldn't stop. That I was too scared, too stubborn, too deep into the con to fold. And that's true. Every year the word *yes* gets heavier, because every year it has to justify all the years I spent refusing it.

But I'd never once considered that somebody was on the other side of the door, hoping I'd keep it shut.

It's March eighth tonight. The nurse has turned me toward the window. There's a great-great-granddaughter I've never met, due in June, and I notice myself reaching for her the way I've reached for all the others, as the next rung on the ladder.

I'm so tired. I've been tired for forty years.

But tomorrow the clock will stop, and he'll settle into that chair and ask me about my week first, the way he does. He'll put off the question as long as he decently can. And when he finally asks it, I'll look at that old friend of mine and think about who would visit him if I went.

"No," I'll say. "Not yet. Sit a while."
