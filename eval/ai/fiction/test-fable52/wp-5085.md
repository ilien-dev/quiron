**The Bread Line**

The soldiers took the bakery first, which told me they had someone local advising them. You don't take the bakery first unless you know that Marta Oyelaran's ovens feed half of Dunmere before six in the morning, and that whoever hands out the bread decides what the town thinks of the day.

I came back in the third week of the occupation, on a transit permit that listed me as an agricultural assessor. The checkpoint sat where the old welcome sign used to be. A boy of maybe nineteen checked my papers with the grave attention of someone who had been told the job mattered. He had a sunburned neck and a rifle he held like a rake.

"Purpose?"

"Soil surveys. The eastern plots."

He waved me through. I had learned to ride a bicycle on that road. I'd broken my wrist forty yards past where he stood, and my father had carried me home telling me the whole way that the bone would knit stronger than before. He was wrong about that. It still aches in the rain.

Dunmere looked almost the same, which was the worst part. I had prepared myself for rubble. Instead the hardware store had its awning out, and Mr. Brandt was sweeping his step as he had every morning of my life, except that now there was a flag I didn't know hanging from the post office and a curfew notice pasted over the church fete posters. The enemy hadn't destroyed my town. They'd moved into it, like a hand into a glove.

My orders were simple. Count vehicles. Note where the officers slept. Find out who was cooperating.

That last one sat in my stomach like a stone.

I took a room above the pharmacy and spent two days walking with a clipboard and a soil auger, counting trucks behind the grain elevator. Eleven. Officers billeted at the Halloran house, the big one with the wraparound porch, where I'd once been sick on the lawn after my first beer. I wrote it all down in a cipher that looked like nitrogen readings.

On the third morning I joined the bread line.

It ran down Cask Street, quiet and orderly, two soldiers lounging at the door. Inside, Marta was behind the counter in her floury apron, exactly as she had been when she used to slip me the burnt-edged rolls for free. Beside her stood an enemy captain, checking ration cards against a ledger. She was chatting with him. She laughed at something he said.

So there was my answer. I had my name for the list.

When I reached the counter she looked at me for half a second too long. Then her face went blank and professional.

"Card?"

I handed over the forged one. The captain glanced at it, at me, and nodded. Marta reached under the counter rather than to the shelf behind her and gave me a loaf wrapped in brown paper.

"Day-old," she said. "It's all assessors get."

I ate it in my room, furious, tearing it apart with my hands. Halfway through, my fingers found the paper. It was a slip rolled tight as a cigarette, baked into the heel of the loaf, written in handwriting I knew from a hundred birthday cake orders.

*Fuel depot is NOT at the elevator. Trucks are decoys. Real one under the school gym. Captain talks when he's flattered. Stop walking around with that clipboard like a fool, Danny, they've noticed. Come Thursday. Buy rye.*

I sat there a long time.

Then I took out my report and crossed off the name I'd been about to write. I thought about the line on Cask Street, about Mr. Brandt sweeping his step where he could see the whole square, about every quiet face I had taken for surrender.

I'd come home believing I was the only one still fighting. But Dunmere had been at war for three weeks before I arrived. It fought the way it did everything else: early in the morning, without fuss, and with flour on its hands.

On Thursday I bought rye.
