# Office Hours

The first thing you lose is your sense of urgency.

I noticed it on day two, standing in the cereal aisle of the Kroger on Delmar, where I'd been standing since day one. Alive, I would have checked my phone forty times. Dead, I watched a box of Raisin Bran for eleven hours and found it, if not interesting, then sufficient.

The bite itself was undramatic. My neighbor Carol got me on the forearm while I was helping her bring in her recycling bins. She'd seemed off, but Carol always seemed off. I remember thinking, *well, that's rude*, and then having a fever, and then not having anything.

People assume it's all rage. It isn't. It's more like being very tired at a party where you don't know anyone. You drift. You bump into things. Occasionally a living person runs past and something in your brainstem stands up like a dog hearing a can opener, and off you go, shambling, and you never catch them, and then you forget why you were walking and find yourself in a Kroger.

There are about thirty of us in here. I don't know their names, because I don't know mine anymore. It went sometime in the first week, along with my PIN and the plot of every movie I've ever seen. I've given the others names based on what's left of them. There's Lanyard, who still wears his conference badge. There's Bride. There's a man in a single roller skate whom I think of as Roller Skate, though it feels unfair to define him by his worst day.

We don't talk. We moan, but it isn't communication. It's more like what a refrigerator does. Still, there's a companionship in it. When one of us turns, we all turn. When one of us walks into the automatic doors, which stopped being automatic when the power failed, we all walk into the doors. I spent thirty-four years alive trying to feel like part of something. It turns out all it took was the complete absence of a self.

The hunger is the part I'd warn you about.

It isn't like being hungry. Hungry was a problem with a solution; you ate a sandwich and became a person again. This is more like a word on the tip of your tongue. Constant, low, maddening. You smell a living person and your whole body says *that one, that's the word*, and you're sure that if you could just get your teeth into it, you'd remember everything.

You wouldn't. I've seen the ones who catch somebody. They look up afterward with the same blank faces, mouths red, still trying to remember.

I haven't caught anybody. I'd like to say that's a moral choice. Mostly I'm slow, and my left ankle faces the wrong way.

But here's what I can't explain.

There's a family living in the stockroom. A woman, an old man, a girl of about nine. They've been back there for weeks, behind the swinging doors with the little plastic windows, and every one of us knows it. We can smell them the way you can smell rain. Some nights the whole herd of us piles against those doors, moaning, pushing, and the doors hold because the old man wedged a pallet jack through the handles. He seems handy.

The girl watches us through the plastic window. She's not as afraid as she should be. She's given us names too. I've heard her through the door. Lanyard is "Mr. Badge." Bride is "the Princess." I'm "Cereal Guy," which is fair.

Last week she slid a drawing under the door. All of us, in crayon, standing in a row, holding hands. She gave us smiles. She gave Roller Skate his other skate.

The others walked over it. I stood on it by accident and then didn't move for two days, and I've decided to believe that was on purpose.

Because something is left. That's what I'm trying to tell you. It's not much. Imagine a house where the family's moved out and the furniture's gone, but in one back room someone forgot to turn off a lamp. I don't know what's lit in mine. I only know that when the herd pushes against the stockroom doors, I end up at the front, and I'm slow, and wide, and my ankle faces the wrong way, and I'm very hard to get around.

Last night the pallet jack slipped an inch. I heard the woman crying. I leaned on the doors with everything I've got left. I leaned the wrong way. Toward us.

The doors held.

This morning the girl came to the window. She looked at me for a long time. Then she waved.

It took me most of an hour, and I did it badly.

But I waved back.
