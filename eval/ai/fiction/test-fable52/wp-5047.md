**The Low Road**

The rope ran out at two hundred feet, and I kept going anyway.

That's the part the rescue team will have trouble with, if there is a rescue team. Dale was the one who knew I'd come down here, and Dale thinks I turned back at the squeeze. I should have. The passage past it wasn't on any survey, and I am forty-three years old with a bad knee and a daughter who expects me at her recital on Saturday.

But the air was moving. Any caver will tell you what that means: somewhere ahead, the cave breathes. Somewhere ahead, there's more.

So I followed the breath down a throat of wet limestone, my headlamp carving a small bright room out of the dark and carrying it with me. I'd been at it an hour when I heard the singing.

I stopped. Water does things underground. It chuckles and mutters, and a mind starved for input will stitch it into voices. I knew that. I held my breath and waited for the sound to dissolve back into dripping.

It didn't. It resolved.

*Hush now, the low road, the low road is long...*

My mouth went dry. I knew the next line before it came.

*...but I'll walk beside you till morning has come.*

You won't find that song anywhere. I've looked. My mother sang it to me in a farmhouse outside Harlan, and when I asked where it came from, she said her mother sang it, and that was all the provenance it had. It had a strange hitch in the third line, a held note where she always took a breath, and I sang it that way to my own daughter, breath and all, because that was how the song went.

The voice in the cave took the breath.

I should tell you my mother has been dead for eleven years. I should also tell you I don't believe in ghosts, and I went toward the voice anyway, because belief is a daylight thing.

The passage opened into a chamber so large my lamp couldn't find the far wall. The floor sloped down to black water, perfectly still. The singing came from across it, or under it, or from the stone itself. It was hard to say. It wasn't quite my mother's voice. It was close the way a face in a dream is close: right in every detail and wrong in sum.

*Hush now, the low road...*

"Hello?" I said, and hated how small it came out.

The song stopped.

Then, from the dark, my own voice said, "Hello?"

It wasn't an echo. It came too late for one, and it was too careful. Something was trying the word out. I stood very still, and I understood, the way you understand a step that isn't there, that the lullaby hadn't been sung *to* me. It had been sung *back*.

How many times had I hummed it down here? Thirty years of caving, in these hills and others, humming the low road in crawlways to keep my breathing slow. And before me, my mother's father, a coal man, forty years under this same ridge. His mother before him. A family carrying one song into the earth, generation after generation, and something down here listening the whole time. Something patient, learning the tune and the words and the breath in the third line.

It had been practicing.

"Hello," it said again, better this time. Then, in my mother's voice, with her warmth, it said my name.

The water was no longer still. A slow ring spread from the center, as if something large had shifted its weight.

I ran. I don't remember the squeeze, or the rope, or the bad knee. I remember daylight, and Dale's face, and that I couldn't speak for an hour.

I made the recital. My daughter was wonderful.

But last night I passed her room, and she was humming it, the low road, breath and all, and I stood in the hallway and thought about how sound carries down.

How one day she'll have children.

How it's still listening. And how well it has learned to sing.
