# The Deposition

The conference room on the fourteenth floor smelled of burnt coffee and carpet glue. Margaret Oyelaran had been taking depositions for twenty-two years, and she had learned to read a witness within the first five minutes. This one, a retired school bus driver named Walter Pruitt, had been reading her right back.

"Mr. Pruitt," she said, "let's return to the evening of March ninth."

"Sure."

"You were the night custodian at Halvorsen Storage. You were the only employee on site."

"Me and the cat. Biscuit. He's not on payroll."

The court reporter's fingers moved without expression. Across the table, Walter's own lawyer, a young man named Deshawn who had clearly been handed this case as a punishment, stared at his legal pad as if it might open and swallow him.

"At approximately eleven forty," Margaret continued, "the contents of Unit 316 were destroyed. Forty-one antique clocks belonging to my client, valued at just over two hundred thousand dollars. You've stated you were present when it happened."

"I was."

"And you've declined, in three separate written statements, to explain how it happened."

Walter folded his hands. They were big hands, spotted and steady. "I didn't decline. I said nobody would believe me. That's different."

"You're under oath now, Mr. Pruitt. Belief is my problem, not yours. Walk me through it."

He looked at Deshawn, who gave the small shrug of a man with nothing left to protect.

"All right," Walter said. "I do my rounds at eleven. Third floor's last. I get up there and Biscuit's sitting outside 316, which he never does, because he hates the third floor. Something about the vents. He's got his ears flat."

"The cat's demeanor is noted."

"Then I hear the clocks. Forty-one of them, and every one's ticking. Now, your client told me when he rented the unit that none of them ran. Said he bought them broke, was going to fix them up. But I'm standing in that hallway and it sounds like a hailstorm on a tin roof."

Margaret wrote something down. She did not, in fact, know that the clocks had been broken. Her client had not mentioned it.

"The lock was off the door," Walter went on. "Not cut. Just sitting on the floor, closed, like somebody had passed it through the hasp. I rolled the door up maybe a foot and looked under."

"And what did you see?"

"A fella sitting cross-legged in the middle of the floor with a little flashlight in his teeth. He had the back off one of the clocks. He was taking something out of it. Little glass tubes, about like so." He held his fingers two inches apart. "He had a whole row of them lined up on a towel. I'd guess he'd been through thirty clocks already."

Deshawn had stopped looking at his legal pad.

"Mr. Pruitt," Margaret said carefully, "are you telling me there was something concealed inside my client's property?"

"I'm telling you what I saw. He looked up at me. Real calm. Took the flashlight out of his mouth and said, 'You should go downstairs, sir. They followed me here.' Polite as you please."

"Who followed him?"

"That's what I asked. He didn't answer. He just looked up at the ceiling. So I looked up at the ceiling."

The court reporter's hands had slowed. The room was very quiet.

"There's a vent up there," Walter said. "Big one, two feet square. The grate was already gone. And there was a face in it, upside down, all wrapped in black, just the eyes showing. Then a second one behind it."

Margaret set down her pen. "Mr. Pruitt, I'm going to remind you once more that you are under oath."

"Yes, ma'am," Walter said. "And that's when the ninja attacked!"
