# One Way

The machine only goes forward. That's the first thing they tell you at the clinic, before the paperwork, before the price. Forward, and never back. Dr. Okafor said it the way a ferryman might mention the river only runs downhill.

"How far?" I asked.

"As far as you want. But think of it as emigrating. You can't write home."

Mara was asleep against my shoulder, six years old and lighter than she'd been at five. The disease in her blood had a long name and a short prognosis. Eight months, the specialists said. Research was promising, they said. Fifteen years, maybe twenty, before a treatment.

So I bought twenty years.

I sold the house to do it. I said goodbye to my mother, who held Mara's face in both hands and memorized it. "Go," she told me. "Don't you dare stay for me." My brother drove us to the clinic and stood in the parking lot with his hands in his pockets, already looking like a photograph.

The jump itself was nothing. A hum, a pressure behind the eyes, like a yawn that wouldn't finish. Mara didn't even wake.

When the door opened, a different technician stood there, older than my brother, wearing a uniform in a cut I didn't recognize.

"Welcome," she said. "It's 2046. Let's get you oriented."

Orientation took three days. They teach you the money, the gadgets, the words that have changed meaning. They give you a counselor, because everyone who arrives is grieving, even the ones who haven't found out yet what for.

My mother had died in 2035. She'd left a box at the clinic's archive, which is a service they offer, the only kind of mail that works here. Inside were eleven years of birthday cards for Mara, one per year, each a little shakier than the last. *You are nine today, wherever you are. You are fourteen. I hope you like horses still.*

I read them in the hallway so Mara wouldn't see me.

The treatment did not exist.

"It's close," the hematologist told me, and I heard the specialists of twenty years ago in his voice. "Trials are underway. Ten years. Perhaps less."

Mara had eight months, the same eight months she'd carried across two decades like a coin in her pocket.

My brother came to see us. He was sixty-one. He had grandchildren and a bad hip and he cried when Mara, who had seen him last week, asked why he was wearing an old man costume.

"You'll jump again," he said to me that night. It wasn't a question.

"I have to."

"I know." He turned his glass in his hands. "I'll leave a box."

This time I bought ten years, on credit against a future I'd have to work off. This time there was no one in the parking lot. My brother couldn't stand it twice, and I didn't blame him.

Hum. Pressure. Door.

"Welcome. It's 2056."

The treatment was an infusion, four sessions, covered by a public health system that hadn't existed when I left. The nurse who administered it was younger than the cure. To her it was routine, like setting a bone.

By spring Mara was running.

There was a box from my brother. He'd made it to 2053. In it were photographs of his family, a letter, and the keys to a small house he'd left us, in a town I'd never heard of, built on land that had been ocean-view and was now simply ocean-adjacent.

We live there now. Mara is seven. She's learning the new words faster than I am, and she corrects me at dinner.

Sometimes she asks whether we'll jump again, to see what comes next.

"No," I tell her. "We go the slow way now. One day at a time, like everyone else."

She thinks that sounds boring. I think it sounds like the most extraordinary machine ever built.
