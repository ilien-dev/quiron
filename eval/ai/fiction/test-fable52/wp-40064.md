**Just Lovely**

I was comparing two identical cans of crushed tomatoes when Marguerite Dell from the library board came around the end of aisle six.

I knew her the way you know anyone in a town of four thousand. She ran the used book sale. She had once sold me a water-damaged atlas for a dollar and told me I was robbing her.

"Oh, it's you," she said, pleased, and touched my sleeve. "I saw your gravestone today. Just lovely!"

Then she walked off toward the dairy case, humming.

I stood there holding the tomatoes. I'm forty-three. I have a cholesterol number my doctor describes as "a conversation," but nothing that would justify masonry.

I caught up with her at the yogurt.

"Marguerite. My what?"

"Your gravestone, dear. At Hollis Brothers, in the yard out back. I was there about Walter's." Her husband, dead since spring. "They had yours on the bench. Gray granite, a little sprig of something carved at the top. Rosemary, I think. Very tasteful."

"I didn't order a gravestone."

"Well, somebody has good taste." She selected a peach yogurt, inspected the date, and put it back. "Don't spoil it for yourself by fretting."

I left my basket on the floor and drove to Hollis Brothers.

The yard was behind a low cinder-block building on Route 9, rows of blank stones standing around like a waiting room. A young man in a dust mask was running a hose over a slab. He shut the water off when he saw my face.

"I'm told you have my gravestone," I said.

He didn't laugh. He asked my name, and when I gave it he nodded slowly and led me to a workbench under a tin roof.

It was gray granite. There was a sprig of rosemary. Below it was my name, all three parts of it, including the middle one I don't use, and my birth date. After the dash, the stone was smooth.

"Who ordered this?"

He went inside and came back with a manila folder, soft with age. "Paid in full, 1994. Instructions were to cut it this year and call the number on file." He frowned. "Number's been disconnected. We were going to send a letter."

The order form was filled out in a hand I knew before I read the signature. My mother made her sevens with a little crossbar, like a European. She had been dead for nineteen years.

A note was stapled to the form, on the back of a recipe card.

*He will never do this for himself. He will leave it for some poor stranger to sort out, the way he leaves everything. So it's done. Rosemary, because he put it in the scrambled eggs when he was nine and was so proud. Do not let him talk you into the shiny black. And tell him it is not an omen. It is a chore I took off his list.*

I read it twice. The young man looked politely at the hose.

In 1994 I was eleven. She had been healthy then, as far as anyone knew. She had driven out here some ordinary afternoon, probably with me at school, picked out granite, and written a check. Then she'd gone home and made dinner and never said a word.

"We can store it," the young man said. "No charge. Long as you need."

"A good long while, I hope."

"That's usually the preference."

I ran my thumb over the rosemary. The cuts were clean and still a little gritty.

On the way home I stopped back at the store. My basket was right where I'd left it. I bought the tomatoes, both cans, and a carton of eggs. Then I went looking for the herbs.
