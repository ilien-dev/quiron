The door appeared in the laundromat between the change machine and the dryer that ate socks.

It was narrow, painted blue, and fitted with a brass knocker shaped like a hand holding its own wrist. A card above it read:

**BEHIND THIS DOOR IS THE ANSWER TO THE QUESTION YOU HAVE NEVER DARED TO ASK.**

Underneath, in smaller letters:

**One customer at a time. No refunds.**

Mara noticed it while folding her dead brother’s shirts.

She had put off washing them for eleven months. Eventually, his smell had faded anyway, leaving only the smell of an apartment she could no longer afford.

She looked away from the door.

Then she looked back.

“Oh, excellent,” said the man sitting atop the washing machines. “That second look. That’s practically a signature.”

He wore a fox-colored suit and no shoes.

“Are you the owner?”

“Of several things.”

“Is this some kind of promotion?”

“Absolutely.”

“What’s the catch?”

He beamed. “People usually ask that after.”

***

The man called himself Venn. Mara knew enough mythology to distrust anyone who introduced himself while juggling objects that weren’t there.

“What happens if I open it?”

“You receive an answer.”

“And you get?”

“Your next question.”

“That’s all?”

“That,” said Venn, “is generally the next question.”

Mara folded a shirt so carefully that the sleeves aligned.

Her brother had called three times the night he died. She had been angry with him. He had borrowed money again. He had lied again. She had watched his name brighten her phone and turned it facedown.

The police said the car had gone through the barrier quickly.

She had never listened to the voicemail.

Venn stopped juggling.

“Ah,” he said, softly enough to sound almost decent.

Mara stood.

“Can it tell me whether he suffered?”

“Yes.”

“Whether answering would have changed anything?”

“Yes.”

“Whether—”

“Only one answer.”

She put her hand on the knob.

Behind her, a dryer finished its cycle with three cheerful beeps.

***

The room beyond contained a kitchen table.

Her mother’s kitchen table, specifically, with the cigarette burn beneath the salt shaker and the leg repaired with silver tape.

On it sat a telephone.

Mara understood immediately, with the awful certainty of dreams, that she could pick it up and hear her brother’s last call.

Instead, she asked the empty room, “Did he know I loved him?”

The telephone rang once.

“Yes,” said her brother’s voice.

Nothing else.

She waited.

Then she sat down, pressed her hands against her mouth, and cried until the kitchen disappeared.

***

She came out carrying an empty laundry basket.

Venn offered her a handkerchief. She refused it.

“Finished?” he asked.

She nodded.

He held out his palm.

A tiny silver hook lay in it.

“What’s that?”

The hook vanished.

Venn closed his fingers.

“Thank you.”

***

For three days, nothing happened.

On the fourth, Mara found an envelope under her apartment door.

Inside was a photograph of herself, aged perhaps seventy, standing beside a little girl with her brother’s eyes.

On the back, someone had written:

**DON’T GO TO THE WEDDING.**

Mara stared at it.

She wondered whose wedding.

Then the wondering stopped.

Not faded. Stopped.

She made coffee.

That frightened her more than the photograph.

By lunchtime, she had discovered the shape of the theft. She could ask questions aloud. She could search for practical information. But whenever she approached something unknown simply because she wanted to know, her mind slid away.

A siren passed. She closed the window.

Her neighbor mentioned a secret. Mara changed the subject.

She found a lump beneath her arm and decided she was probably imagining it.

That evening, she returned to the laundromat.

***

Venn was teaching a washing machine to purr.

“Give it back,” Mara said.

“Your next question? You gave it freely.”

“You took my curiosity.”

“Your next question was ‘What’s that?’ A wonderfully broad inquiry. The hook caught everything attached.”

“You knew what I meant.”

“I’m a trickster god. Understanding what people mean would put me out of business.”

Mara slapped the photograph against his chest.

“What happens at the wedding?”

“See? You can still ask.”

“But I don’t care about the answer.”

“Yes,” he said. “Most find that restful.”

She thought of the lump. Of walking past a child crying behind a locked door.

“What’s your price?”

Venn smiled.

“Bring me another customer.”

***

Mara returned the following evening with a cardboard box.

Inside lay a small black cat.

Venn’s smile collapsed.

“No.”

“He’s very curious.”

“Animals don’t count.”

“Where does it say that?”

“The terms are understood.”

“By whom?”

He folded his arms.

Mara put the box down beside the blue door.

The cat climbed out, stretched, and inspected the brass knocker.

“You can’t make him open it,” Venn said.

“I know.”

The cat sniffed the threshold.

Venn watched.

The cat lifted one paw.

Venn leaned forward.

Then it turned around, climbed into the box, and fell asleep.

Mara sat on a washing machine.

An hour passed.

“What are you doing?” Venn demanded.

“Waiting.”

“For what?”

She shrugged.

The god glanced at the box.

Another hour passed.

“Does it know something?”

Mara examined her fingernails.

“Why won’t it investigate the door?”

“Maybe there’s something better in the box.”

Venn looked inside.

The silver hook flashed between his lips.

***

Mara felt the world rush back: the photograph, the wedding, the lump, the extraordinary question of whether a god could swear himself unconscious.

Venn spat out the hook.

“You put that in there!”

“You left it in my head.”

“That isn’t fair!”

Mara picked up the sleeping cat.

“Isn’t it?”

Behind her, the blue door clicked shut.

She had a doctor to call.
