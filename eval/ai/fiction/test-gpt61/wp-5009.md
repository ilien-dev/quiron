Lord Veyr had rehearsed the speech for eleven years.

He had practiced before mirrors, prisoners, and once, during a difficult winter, a snowman dressed as his father. He had revised the opening seventeen times. The pause after “inevitable” was timed to a distant roll of thunder, which the weather division had assured him would be ready.

Now the hero knelt before his throne, wrists bound in silver, the Sword of Morning lying just beyond reach.

“At last,” Veyr said, “you understand.”

The hero spat blood onto the marble.

Excellent. Exactly the response Veyr had anticipated.

“You believed yourself an agent of destiny. Yet every victory, every narrow escape, every friend you gathered along your journey—”

A crossbow snapped.

The hero fell sideways.

For several seconds, the only sound was a little brass casing rolling down the throne-room steps.

Veyr looked at the body.

Then at the crossbow.

Then at the guard holding it.

“What,” he said.

The guard lowered his weapon.

“He was reaching for his sword, my lord.”

“Yes.”

“So I shot him.”

“Yes,” Veyr repeated, though this second yes had considerably less certainty in it.

The guard shifted his weight.

“Was I meant to let him?”

Veyr descended the steps. He nudged the hero with one pointed boot.

Nothing.

He nudged harder.

Still nothing.

“That,” Veyr said quietly, “was the last prince of the Dawn Kingdom.”

The guard swallowed.

“Should I have used the cheaper bolts?”

Behind the throne, the thunder finally arrived.

Veyr closed his eyes.

***

The court physician confirmed the death. So did the necromancer, though his confirmation included an optimistic quotation for further services.

Veyr dismissed them both.

The guard remained in the throne room because no one had told him he could leave.

“You don’t understand,” Veyr said. “He had three companions infiltrating the fortress. They were supposed to rescue him.”

“Want me to sound the alarm?”

“No. Yes. Obviously yes.”

The guard pulled a cord.

Somewhere below, bells began ringing.

Veyr stared at him.

“There was an alarm cord?”

“By every door, my lord. Fire regulations.”

Within twenty minutes, the companions had been captured. The sorceress had entered through a drainage channel fitted with a perfectly ordinary iron grate. The rogue had been identified because the guards knew one another. The dwarf had gotten lost.

Veyr sat heavily on the steps.

“This isn’t how it happens.”

The guard said nothing.

“There is a confrontation. Revelations. I tell him who betrayed his father.”

“Who did?”

“His father’s adviser.”

“Ah.”

“And then he tells me revenge won’t bring my family back.”

The guard considered this.

“Would it?”

“No.”

“Sorry, my lord.”

Veyr covered his face.

***

At dawn, the conquered kingdoms surrendered.

By noon, Veyr had received forty-seven petitions concerning grain tariffs.

By three, he understood why his predecessors had spent so much time hunting.

He summoned the guard.

“You’ve ruined everything.”

The man stood stiffly before the desk.

“Yes, my lord.”

“I was going to lose.”

The guard blinked.

“Eventually,” Veyr said. “Spectacularly. There would have been fire. A collapsing bridge. Possibly forgiveness.”

He pushed a petition across the desk.

“What am I supposed to do about irrigation rights?”

The guard read it slowly.

“My brother farms downstream. Could ask him.”

Veyr looked up.

“You have a brother?”

“Two.”

“And a name, presumably.”

The guard opened his mouth.

Veyr raised a hand.

“No. Don’t tell me yet.”

For the first time all day, something like hope stirred in his chest.

He stood, swept his cloak behind him, and pointed dramatically at the man who had killed destiny.

“You,” he declared, “are my new archnemesis.”

The guard looked at the overflowing desk.

“Does that come with a raise?”
