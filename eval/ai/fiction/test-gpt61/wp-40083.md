The first thing I asked Jimmy’s dad was where my dog had gone.

“Behind the bowling alley,” he said. “A woman named Denise is feeding him chicken. He’s pretending he doesn’t have a home.”

I found Buster there twenty minutes later, looking embarrassed.

After that, I believed Jimmy.

We were nine that summer. His dad repaired washing machines and wore the same green jacket even when it was hot. He knew where baseballs landed, whether peaches were sweet, and exactly how long you could hold a sparkler before regretting it.

He never showed off.

“Ask him something hard,” Jimmy told me.

So I asked how many stars there were.

His dad gave me a number so long I forgot the beginning before he reached the end.

“Does that count the ones that haven’t happened yet?” Jimmy asked.

“No,” his dad said, and started again.

***

By October, I’d learned there were rules.

He wouldn’t tell you test answers. He wouldn’t tell you what anybody thought about you. And he wouldn’t answer questions beginning with *When will I die?*

“Knowing a thing doesn’t always help,” he explained.

“But you know it anyway?”

He was replacing a tiny screw in a toaster. His hands stopped.

“Yes.”

I thought about that for nearly a minute, which was a long time then.

“Even stuff you don’t want to?”

“Yes.”

That Christmas, I brought him earmuffs.

He cried, which made me think I’d done something wrong, until he put them on.

***

When we were thirteen, Jimmy’s mother left.

His dad packed her suitcase before she asked for it. He put an umbrella beside the door. Outside, the sky was blue.

Jimmy wouldn’t speak to him for three days.

“You could’ve stopped her,” he finally shouted.

His dad sat at the kitchen table, still wearing his work boots.

“I could’ve made her stay.”

“That’s the same thing.”

“No,” his dad said. “It isn’t.”

I hated him a little for that. Jimmy hated him more.

At thirteen, we still believed every broken thing ought to be repairable if you knew enough about it.

***

At seventeen, Jimmy asked his dad whether he should marry Elena.

They’d been dating eleven weeks.

His dad smiled.

“What?” Jimmy demanded. “Is that funny?”

“No.”

“Then tell me.”

“I won’t.”

“You tell everybody everything!”

“Very rarely,” his dad said.

Jimmy slammed the door hard enough to break the little window in it.

His dad already had the replacement glass in the garage.

***

I moved away. Jimmy stayed.

There were Christmas cards, then photographs of children, then messages we meant to answer sooner.

When Jimmy called me at forty-three, I knew before he said it.

“Dad’s sick.”

“How sick?”

A long silence.

“He won’t tell me.”

I drove home that Friday.

The green jacket hung beside the hospital bed. His dad looked smaller, but when I sat down he said, “Buster really did love you. The chicken was just very good.”

I laughed. Then I couldn’t.

Jimmy was downstairs getting coffee neither of us wanted.

“Can I ask you something?” I said.

“You always could.”

“Was it awful? Knowing everything?”

He watched a pigeon settle on the windowsill.

“Sometimes.”

“Then how did you stand it?”

He turned toward the door.

“Jimmy used to come home from school and tell me about his day.”

“But you already knew about it.”

“Oh, yes.”

“Then why—”

“Because he wanted to tell me.”

***

Near the end, Jimmy climbed into the bed beside him, an enormous man folding himself around his father.

I stood outside the half-open door.

“Dad?”

“Yes.”

“Am I going to be okay?”

His father took a breath.

“Yes.”

“Are the kids?”

“Yes.”

“Did Mom love you?”

“Very much.”

Jimmy wiped his face on his sleeve.

“Do you know how much I love you?”

For the first time in all the years I’d known him, his father hesitated.

Jimmy lifted his head, frightened.

Then his dad touched his cheek.

“Tell me,” he said.

So Jimmy did, stumbling over it, repeating himself, getting some of it wrong.

His father listened to every word.
