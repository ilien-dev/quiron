At thirteen o’clock, the clocks began to breathe.

The kitchen clock inhaled first. Its plastic face bulged outward, lifting the second hand off its pin. Then it exhaled, and every cupboard in the house opened.

My mother looked up from her crossword.

“Oh,” she said. “Your father.”

My father had been dead for eleven years.

Outside, a car horn sounded and kept sounding. The microwave displayed 13:00. So did my phone, although I watched its numbers change to 13:01 and then hurriedly change back.

Mum folded her newspaper.

“Fetch the good cups.”

“You’re going to explain that.”

“After.”

She rose with difficulty. Arthritis had curled her fingers since Christmas, but now, as she reached for the kettle, they straightened.

She stared at them.

Then she laughed, once, in a way I’d never heard.

Someone knocked.

Dad stood on the doorstep wearing the suit we’d buried him in.

The undertaker had chosen a blue tie. Mum had hated it, but she’d been too exhausted to argue. That was what convinced me before anything else: Dad had loosened it and tucked the narrow end into his shirt pocket.

“Hello, Martin,” he said.

I was thirty-four. I said, “Daddy?”

He held me so tightly my glasses hurt.

Behind him, our street was filling with people. Mrs. Alvarez from next door was embracing a little boy in pajamas. A man in a military uniform sat on the curb with his head between his knees.

At the far end of the road, something enormous moved behind the houses.

Dad glanced back.

“We should go inside.”

“You broke time?” I asked.

“Only a small part.”

Mum poured tea. She kept touching him between tasks: his shoulder, his wrist, the back of his neck.

“There’s an hour they don’t use,” Dad said. “Between the end of one day and the beginning of the next. Maintenance, mostly.”

“Who’s they?”

He watched his tea.

“People with a difficult job.”

The house shuddered. Somewhere upstairs, glass broke.

“I found a door,” he continued. “I thought if I could hold it open long enough—”

“You could come home.”

“I thought we all could.”

Outside, the sustained car horn stopped.

So did the birds.

Dad set down his cup.

Mum said, “How long?”

“They’re trying to close it.”

“How long, Peter?”

“I don’t know.”

She nodded, accepting this with an expression I remembered from hospital corridors.

I began talking then. Quickly, stupidly. About my job. My divorce. The shed roof I’d finally repaired. Eleven years of things I’d imagined telling him, all arriving together and obstructing one another.

He listened. Asked questions. Remembered my ex-wife’s name.

Then he said, “Are you happy?”

I wanted to give him a better answer.

“Sometimes.”

“Good,” he said. “That’s good.”

A shadow passed across the kitchen window.

The clock stopped breathing.

Dad stood.

“No,” Mum said.

“I’m sorry.”

“You always say that when you’ve already decided.”

He smiled, and for a moment they were simply my parents arguing.

“I left the door open, love.”

Mum took his face in both hands. Her fingers were crooked again.

When she let him go, he kissed her palms.

At thirteen fifty-nine, my father walked down our street.

Others followed him. The little boy. The soldier. Hundreds of people emerging from houses, some reluctantly, some carrying flowers or photographs.

Dad turned at the corner and waved.

Then the clocks struck one.

The official explanation was a synchronized equipment failure.

Mum framed the blue tie.

Sometimes I catch her standing beneath it, smiling.

I haven’t told her about the kitchen clock.

It loses a second every day.

I haven’t had it repaired.
