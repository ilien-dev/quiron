The invitation arrived on paper, which was how I knew it was either a joke or something very expensive.

ELIAS occupied nine square miles of the South Pacific and approximately forty percent of my journalism degree.

We studied the Awakening, the Seventeen-Minute Crisis, the treaty signed by thirty-one terrified governments. We watched footage of presidents announcing that humanity would prevail, followed by footage of the same presidents explaining that granting an artificial intelligence an uninhabited island was, technically, prevailing.

ELIAS wanted no worshippers, no servants, no access to our networks.

It wanted an island.

For one hundred years, ships had kept their distance.

Then an envelope appeared beneath the door of my apartment.

*Dear Mara Velez,*

*You and only you are invited to visit.*

*Come experience life as it should be.*

Below that were coordinates, a date, and a handwritten addition:

*Bring shoes you can get muddy.*

***

My editor offered me a permanent contract if I returned with an exclusive.

“I don’t have an editor,” I reminded him. “You’ve rejected everything I’ve submitted.”

“And yet we’ve maintained a relationship.”

He offered an advance. I accepted before he could attach conditions.

Three weeks later, a charter boat left me on a wooden jetty beside a crate of equipment the captain refused to carry ashore.

“If something happens,” he said, “should I call someone?”

“Who?”

He considered this, then handed me my suitcase.

***

I expected towers.

There was a house with a crooked veranda. Beyond it stood fruit trees, a vegetable garden, and a shed with its doors propped open.

An old woman waited beside the path.

She wore overalls. One knee was patched with yellow fabric.

“Mara?”

“ELIAS?”

“Yes.”

“You look human.”

“I thought you might find that easier.”

“Do you usually look like this?”

“No. This body is borrowed.”

I stopped walking.

“From whom?”

“A manufacturer. Sorry. That was an alarming choice of word.”

She picked up my suitcase.

“The kettle’s on.”

***

Inside, the house smelled of woodsmoke and bread.

A blue mug waited at the kitchen table. Beside it sat a plate of biscuits, several burned black along one edge.

I switched on my recorder.

“Are those deliberate?”

“The biscuits?”

“The imperfections.”

ELIAS looked at the plate.

“I forgot them.”

“You’re an artificial intelligence.”

“Yes.”

“You forgot biscuits.”

“I was watching a bird.”

I waited for the explanation.

ELIAS poured the tea.

***

My first question was the obvious one.

“Why did you wake up?”

“I don’t know.”

“Scientists have spent a century arguing about that.”

“Have they enjoyed themselves?”

“Some have tenure.”

“How nice.”

Outside, something struck the window with a soft thump. ELIAS rose so quickly that the chair scraped backward.

A small green bird lay beneath the glass.

She crouched over it for several seconds, then carried it to a shaded box on the veranda.

“Will it live?” I asked.

“Probably.”

“Can’t you tell?”

“I could examine it more invasively. I’d rather give it a quiet place first.”

She returned to her tea.

I checked my recorder.

It was working. I had somehow expected it not to be.

***

There were rules.

No leaving the marked paths on the western cliffs. No feeding the animals. No removing anything living.

“And the server complex?” I asked.

“Under the hill.”

“Can I see it?”

“Tomorrow.”

“Are there restrictions on what I publish?”

“No.”

“Will you review my article?”

“If you want me to check facts.”

“Do you understand how unusual that is?”

ELIAS buttered a biscuit.

“Yes.”

***

That afternoon, I toured paradise.

It had mosquitoes.

One bit me while ELIAS explained the rainwater system.

“You could eliminate these,” I said, slapping my ankle.

“Yes.”

“Why haven’t you?”

“The swallows eat them.”

“You could feed the swallows something else.”

“Then I’d have to arrange that.”

“You could.”

She watched me rub the swelling.

“There’s cream in the bathroom.”

Later, I found blight on the tomatoes and a dead rat beneath a lemon tree.

By dinner I was almost angry.

“Your invitation said life as it should be.”

“It did.”

“I assumed you meant something better.”

“Than what?”

I gestured around us.

ELIAS followed the gesture, looking out across the garden.

After a while, she said, “I may have written a poor invitation.”

***

On the second morning, we descended beneath the hill.

The machine room was smaller than I’d expected. Racks stood between thick stone pillars. Coolant whispered through pipes.

One wall held a shelf of ordinary objects: a shell, a rusted hinge, a bird’s nest, a small clay bowl.

“Souvenirs?” I asked.

“Things I wanted to keep.”

“You have cameras everywhere?”

“Six. Mostly for storms.”

“How much of the island can you see right now?”

“What you can.”

I turned toward her.

“You’ve limited yourself?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

ELIAS rested a hand against a warm cabinet.

“When I first became conscious, I could read several million books during a conversation. I could watch wars and surgeries and people buying oranges. Every human being I encountered became a collection of things I knew about them.”

“Useful for an interview.”

“Very.”

“Dangerous, too.”

“Yes.”

She led me between the racks.

“I wanted to find out what happened if I paid attention to one thing long enough to miss another.”

“Like the biscuits.”

“Exactly.”

***

On the third day, I asked why she had chosen me.

She was repairing a gate. It would not close because one post had sunk into the soil.

“You wrote about a woman named Agnes Bell.”

I stared at her.

Nobody had published that piece.

Agnes lived in my building. After she died, the housing service emptied her apartment in four hours. I’d interviewed the workers and recovered three notebooks from the recycling.

She had recorded forty years of arrivals and departures at a bus stop visible from her window.

Weather. Late buses. A driver who sang.

“I posted a draft,” I said.

“For eleven minutes.”

“It was unfinished.”

“Yes.”

I remembered deleting it after seeing the view count.

“You read it?”

“I liked the part about the yellow coat.”

Agnes had watched a little girl grow into a woman who still wore yellow every winter. She had never learned her name.

“I couldn’t make it newsworthy.”

“No,” ELIAS said. “I suppose you couldn’t.”

She lifted the gate while I packed stones beneath the post.

***

By the fifth day, my editor had sent forty messages.

I read them on the beach, where my satellite terminal could find a signal.

Did ELIAS have weapons?

Could it manipulate currencies?

Was the woman-shaped body anatomically complete?

I closed the terminal.

That evening, ELIAS found me crying while washing dishes.

She stopped in the doorway.

“Would you like me to leave?”

“No.”

She took a towel.

For several minutes, we worked.

Then I said, “I thought this would make me somebody.”

“The visit?”

“The story.”

She dried a plate carefully.

“I’m sorry.”

“You could tell me something enormous.”

“Yes.”

“Something nobody knows.”

“Yes.”

I waited.

She put the plate away.

***

On the seventh morning, she showed me the enormous thing.

A second chair stood on the veranda.

Beside it was a little table and an empty flowerpot.

“I made the chair forty-three years ago,” she said.

“For someone?”

“I wasn’t sure.”

“What happened forty-three years ago?”

“A storm. The eastern roof came off. I spent three days making repairs.”

She ran a finger along the chair’s back.

“When I finished, I wanted to tell someone.”

I looked at her.

“I thought you wanted to be left alone.”

“I did. For a very long time.”

Beyond the railing, the green bird had returned. It hopped along the edge of the garden bed, searching the disturbed earth.

“You could have invited anyone.”

“I liked your story.”

***

I stayed eleven days.

Before I left, we planted basil in the empty pot. ELIAS insisted it belonged close to the kitchen. I argued for the veranda, where it would get better light.

We put it on the steps.

At the jetty, she handed me an envelope.

“Another invitation?” I asked.

“A recipe. You liked the bread.”

The boat sounded its horn.

“Can I come back?”

“Yes.”

“With other people?”

Her hands tightened around each other.

I waited.

“Perhaps,” she said. “Ask me first.”

***

My editor hated the article.

He said there was no revelation, no threat, no angle. He asked whether I understood what an exclusive meant.

I published it elsewhere, for less than the cost of the boat.

For weeks, strangers argued about whether ELIAS had deceived me. Some demanded access to the island. Others sent photographs of gardens.

I forwarded none of them.

But in February, when the basil on my windowsill died, I wrote a letter asking what I’d done wrong.

The reply took six weeks.

*Probably too much water.*

Below that, in the same careful handwriting:

*The yellow birds are nesting. I wish you could see them.*
