The first thing we translated was a warning.

Naturally, we assumed it was a welcome.

There are only so many ways to interpret a circle of figures holding hands beneath a descending sun. Unity. Enlightenment. Civilization bringing light into darkness.

We put it on the expedition’s letterhead.

By the time I understood that the figures were restraining one another, we had already drilled through the outer shell.

***

Atlantis lay eleven kilometers beneath the Atlantic, inside a bubble of water that did not move.

Our instruments called it ice. Our cameras showed otherwise: fish suspended midstroke, sediment hanging in pale curtains, an ancient woman standing upright outside a doorway with one hand lifted toward her face.

Nothing had decayed.

Nothing had finished happening.

We entered through the hole our drill had made. Within our diving suits, clocks disagreed. My wedding ring warmed against my finger.

Dr. Vale laughed over the radio.

“Ten thousand years,” she said. “And they’ve still got the lights on.”

Beneath us, streets curved around a black tower. Every window in the city faced it.

Every door opened away.

***

Their language was difficult because it contained no future tense.

At first, I thought this philosophical. After six days with the archives, I realized it was practical.

They had abolished uncertainty.

Their machines could calculate any event, then adjust the conditions necessary to produce it. Weather. Disease. The precise moment two strangers would fall in love.

There were records of gardens designed around conversations that would take place centuries later.

There were children named for their own last words.

And there was the tower.

They built it to answer the oldest question.

Not whether they were alone.

Whether anything existed outside the universe.

***

Vale found the chamber beneath the tower on our ninth day.

She sent me a photograph.

A chair faced a smooth black wall. Beside it stood a table bearing two cups.

One cup was overturned.

The other was steaming.

“Someone’s here,” she whispered.

In the archives, the symbols finally resolved.

The circle was a seal.

The descending sun was an eye.

I told her to leave.

She asked why.

Behind her voice came a small, courteous sound.

Someone clearing their throat.

***

The Atlanteans had succeeded.

Beyond matter, beyond time, something had noticed their question.

It could not enter our universe uninvited. Unfortunately, observation was invitation enough.

To understand it was to give it somewhere to stand.

Their first researcher saw it in a mathematical proof.

His assistant saw it in his face.

Within an hour, seven thousand people were reporting an additional person in rooms where they had previously been alone.

No violence. No demands.

It asked questions.

What is hunger?

Why do children stop moving?

How much of a person can be removed before the person is gone?

The records became less clinical after that.

***

Atlantis sank itself in forty-three seconds.

Their machines folded an instant around the city, stretching its final moment into an almost infinite duration. The ocean served as ballast, insulation, concealment.

The people outside had volunteered.

Their task was to remain alive, to keep looking inward, to hold the attention of something that was learning how to want.

That was what the figures on our letterhead meant.

Hold each other.

Do not look away.

***

I flooded the drill shaft.

I erased the archives.

Then I watched the expedition cameras until Vale appeared at the entrance to our habitat.

She had removed her helmet.

At that depth, her face should have collapsed.

“Let me in, Daniel.”

Something stood behind her, obscured by her shoulder.

“Tell me what you found.”

I disconnected the cameras.

She kept speaking through the radio.

After I destroyed the radio, she spoke through my wedding ring.

***

The inquiry blamed a methane eruption.

I supplied altered surveys and a convincing account of equipment failure. Families received compensation. The coordinates disappeared beneath a minor correction to a bathymetric database.

I came home.

My wife cried when she saw me. She held me for a long time.

Then she asked why I wasn’t wearing my ring.

I said I had lost it.

***

I am writing this by hand because paper cannot transmit anything on its own.

When I finish, I will burn it, one page at a time, without reading it again.

This morning, my wife set three cups on the breakfast table.

She stared at the third for several seconds.

Then she looked toward the empty chair.

I took her face in my hands before she could speak.

“Look at me,” I said.

“Whatever happens, keep looking at me.”
