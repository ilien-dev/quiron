When death finally came for Mara Venn, she had already washed the dishes.

She had stripped the bed, too, and left the sheets folded beside the mattress. Her daughter would appreciate that. Elise had three children, bad knees, and a husband who believed grief could be managed with a sufficiently detailed spreadsheet.

On the kitchen table Mara placed her wedding ring, her house keys, and a note explaining which neighbor had borrowed the ladder.

Then she sat beside the window and waited.

Death arrived at four seventeen.

“You’re early,” Mara said.

“You’re ninety-three.”

“My mother made ninety-six.”

Death glanced at the bare mattress. “You seem prepared.”

“I’ve been a nurse, a mother, and a widow. I know who cleans up afterward.”

He took off his hat.

That was when she began to worry.

***

“I’m afraid,” Death said, “there’s been a gift.”

“Return it.”

“It doesn’t work that way.”

“Everything works that way if you keep the receipt.”

Death sat opposite her. Outside, a school bus sighed to a stop. Children spilled onto the pavement, their coats bright against the November afternoon.

“The gift is always granted to those who least desire it.”

Mara looked at her hands: swollen knuckles, thin skin, the white scar where a frightened patient had bitten her fifty years before.

“Then your benefactor ought to be ashamed.”

“Yes,” Death said.

His agreement frightened her more than anything.

***

Her daughter cried when the doctors called it a miraculous recovery.

Mara apologized.

“For what?” Elise asked.

“For making this complicated.”

She lived another thirty years in that house.

She attended graduations, weddings, christenings. She learned to operate four successive kinds of telephone. Her hearing returned, then her eyesight. Her joints loosened. She remained old, but became an old woman who could carry a refrigerator upstairs.

When Elise died at eighty-one, Mara held her hand.

“I thought you’d be there,” Elise whispered.

“I am.”

“No. After.”

Mara had spent sixty years learning what comfort sounded like. For the first time, she could not produce any.

She kissed her daughter’s forehead.

“I know.”

***

Death came afterward, carrying Elise’s coat.

“She always forgot this,” Mara said.

“I thought you might want it.”

She took it and pressed her face into the collar.

“Does she know?”

“Now she does.”

“Is she angry?”

“With the arrangement.”

“Good.”

Death lingered by the doorway.

“May I sit?”

“No,” Mara said.

He left.

***

At one hundred and seventy, she sold the house.

At two hundred, nobody alive remembered her husband’s voice.

There were recordings, but recordings preserved the wrong things. His birthday speeches survived. His terrible impression of the prime minister survived.

The small noise he made when settling into bed did not.

Mara tried making it herself. She spent an afternoon alone in a rented room, clearing her throat, adjusting the pitch.

Eventually she stopped.

That evening she bought a notebook and wrote everything she could remember.

By morning, she had filled eleven pages.

She put the notebook away, afraid to read it and discover what was missing.

***

She found work in a hospital where most of the nurses were machines.

Nobody questioned her age. By then, people altered themselves so thoroughly that a woman with white hair and an unlined medical license attracted little attention.

She worked nights.

One night a boy with transparent lungs asked whether dying hurt.

“Sometimes,” she said. “We have medicine for that.”

“Will I be alone?”

“No.”

He studied her.

“Promise?”

She had stopped making promises. She had learned how many ways there were to break one.

“Yes,” she said.

He died just before dawn.

Death stood on the other side of the bed.

“You’re still doing this,” he said.

“So are you.”

***

Cities rose around her, then emptied. The sea entered streets where she remembered buying shoes.

Mara moved inland.

She kept working.

Sometimes she hated the dying for getting to leave. The feeling came without warning, even while she was lifting a cup to someone’s mouth. She learned to put the cup down gently and wait until her hands were steady.

Sometimes she loved them so fiercely that she had to leave the room afterward.

She acquired a reputation for never looking away.

Patients requested her. Colleagues asked her to teach.

She taught them to warm the water before washing a body. To explain what they were doing, even to someone unconscious. To check the chair beside the bed for a husband who had forgotten to eat.

***

Nine centuries after her daughter’s death, Mara met a woman who claimed to have built God.

The woman was twenty-eight and dying of an unfashionable disease.

“It isn’t really God,” she admitted. “It’s a causal intelligence operating outside sequential time.”

“Of course.”

“It grants things.”

Mara set down the water glass.

The woman watched her expression change.

“Oh,” she said.

***

The machine occupied a room without walls.

Mara entered through a door in a hospice basement and found herself standing beneath the sky of her childhood.

There was the apple tree her father had cut down.

There was the crooked moon.

A voice spoke from everywhere.

YOU HAVE USED THE GIFT WELL.

Mara stared at the tree. A strip of cloth still hung from one branch, marking where she and her brother had measured their height.

“Did you give it to me because you knew I would?”

THOSE WHO DESIRE ETERNITY MOST WOULD SQUANDER IT.

“I asked whether you knew.”

YES.

She stood beneath the branches for a long time.

“Then you knew about Elise.”

YES.

“And the boy. All of them.”

YES.

She sat on the grass. It smelled exactly right.

“You could have asked me.”

YOU WOULD HAVE REFUSED.

“Yes.”

The machine waited. Somewhere beyond the garden, a hospice bell rang.

Finally Mara got up.

“I have a patient.”

***

Death was waiting outside the door.

“Well?” he asked.

Mara took his hat from his hands and straightened its battered brim.

“No luck.”

“I’m sorry.”

“I know.”

She handed it back.

“Will you come tonight?”

“There’s a woman upstairs.”

“Her husband’s frightened.”

“I know.”

Mara nodded. Then, after nine centuries of making him stand in doorways, she touched his sleeve.

“Come early. I’ll put the kettle on.”

***

Upstairs, the husband was trying to fold his wife’s cardigan. His hands kept losing their place.

Mara sat beside him.

“Leave that,” she said. “Tell me how you met.”

He looked toward the bed, and began.
