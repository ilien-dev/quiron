The first thing I noticed was that they’d gotten his height wrong.

The prosecutor called the defendant a large, intimidating man. He stood five foot eight, perhaps nine in the prison shoes. Daniel had been six foot two. I remembered because I’d had to tilt the knife upward.

“Can you remain impartial?” the judge asked me.

“Yes, Your Honor.”

They seated me beside a retired librarian who smelled of peppermint.

***

The accused was named Peter Vale. He repaired commercial refrigerators, had a daughter missing her front teeth, and apparently possessed the worst luck of any man alive.

His van had been parked behind Daniel’s building. His fingerprints were on the service entrance. Three days before the murder, he’d threatened Daniel over an unpaid invoice.

“I said he’d regret it,” Peter told the detective in the recording. “I meant small claims court.”

The prosecutor paused the video there.

Nobody laughed.

I’d chosen that entrance because the camera had been broken for months. I hadn’t known about the van.

***

On the fourth day, they showed the photographs.

The librarian looked down. A young juror whispered, “Jesus.”

I studied the blood beneath Daniel’s head. At the time, I’d watched it spread until it reached the toe of my shoe. Here, enlarged on a screen, it looked almost black.

Daniel’s sister sat in the front row. She wore a silver bird around her neck.

I had given him that necklace for our anniversary. He’d said he lost it.

During lunch, I vomited into a courthouse toilet.

***

Peter’s lawyer was twenty-seven and perpetually apologizing.

“Sorry, Your Honor.”

“Sorry. One moment.”

“Sorry, could you repeat that?”

She had no witnesses. Peter had been alone after his last repair call. The building’s manager insisted nobody else used the service entrance.

I wanted to raise my hand.

I used it, I wanted to say. Daniel gave me a key when we were still pretending he would leave his wife.

Instead, I took notes.

***

The prosecution’s final witness was Daniel’s wife.

She said her husband was generous. Patient. A devoted father.

She cried without covering her face.

I remembered her calling me six months earlier.

“He says you’re stalking him.”

I remembered trying to explain the messages, the apartment, the money.

“If you contact my family again,” she’d said, “I’ll call the police.”

On the stand, she twisted her wedding ring.

For the first time, I wondered what he’d told her about everything else.

***

We voted eleven to one.

The librarian sighed.

“What more do you need?”

“Something placing him inside.”

“The fingerprints.”

“He worked there.”

“The threat.”

“Isn’t murder.”

They argued until five. Then six.

A salesman loosened his tie. “Some of us have lives.”

“So does he,” I said.

Nobody spoke for a while.

***

By the third day, two others had joined me.

By the fifth, we were six and six.

The librarian watched me across the table.

“You seem awfully certain.”

“I’m certain there’s doubt.”

“That isn’t what I said.”

I looked away first.

***

The judge declared a mistrial.

Peter folded over the defense table, shaking. His lawyer put a hand between his shoulders.

I went home and sat at my kitchen counter. For five days I’d imagined this moment: his release, my relief.

At nine, the news showed Peter leaving court. Charges remained pending. The prosecutor promised another trial.

His daughter ran toward him.

He stopped her before she could jump into his arms. Reporters crowded close, shouting questions.

I watched him guide her through them, one hand shielding her face.

Then I called the number on my jury summons.

The clerk answered.

“I was on the Vale jury,” I said.

“Yes?”

“There’s something I should have told the judge.”

She asked me to hold.

While I waited, I took Daniel’s key from the drawer and placed it on the counter.
