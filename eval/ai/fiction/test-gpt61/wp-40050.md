The first thing a president asks is whether aliens are real.

Not every president asks aloud. Carter wrote it on a yellow legal pad and slid it across the table, as though the aliens might be listening.

They were, but that’s not why we use the room under the Lincoln Memorial.

I was twenty-eight then. My assignment was to stand by the door while Director Voss delivered the Continuity Briefing. When Carter began crying, I looked away.

I’d expected fear.

“They’ve been trying to get home for how long?” he asked.

He spent the next forty minutes asking about spare parts.

Reagan assumed we were pitching a film.

He listened through the visitors, the thing beneath Yellowstone, and the emergency telephone that occasionally rang despite having no internal components.

“Hell of a second act,” he said.

Then Voss showed him the photographs from the moon.

Reagan’s smile disappeared.

“Those are our boys?”

“Yes, Mr. President.”

“But we brought them back.”

“We brought something back.”

He studied the photographs for a long time. Then he turned them facedown, carefully, one at a time.

“Do their wives know?”

Bush senior already knew most of it.

He corrected two dates and asked whether the ocean had started making that noise again.

Afterward, Voss retired.

I inherited the briefing.

***

Clinton wanted definitions.

“What exactly do we mean by ‘immortal’?”

“Unable to die, sir.”

“Unable, or hasn’t yet?”

That distinction cost us three months and a research team.

He was good with the telephone, though. When it rang during his briefing, he answered before I could stop him.

He listened, nodded, and said, “Well, I hear that.”

Then he hung up.

“What did it say, sir?”

“Seems lonely.”

I asked whether it had identified itself.

He gave me the look presidents give you when they’ve decided something is classified above your clearance.

***

Bush junior asked whether the thing under Yellowstone could be killed.

We told him what we’d tried.

He asked again.

Later, alone, he asked me whether prayer helped.

“I don’t know, sir.”

He nodded.

“Well. Doesn’t cost anything.”

For eight years, the monitoring station recorded a small reduction in movement every Sunday morning.

We never told him. There wasn’t enough evidence.

I regret that.

***

Obama requested the full documentation.

He read until dawn, making notes in the margins. At six, he reached the final folder.

Every president reaches it differently.

He read the single page twice.

“How certain are we?”

“Certain.”

He stood by the wall for a while, looking at the photograph of Earth taken from the moon.

“And we can’t warn them?”

“No, sir.”

“All right,” he said.

Then, quieter: “All right.”

***

Trump thought the final folder was a test.

“Who else knows?”

“Living former presidents. This office. Eleven people at the installation.”

“And nobody’s said anything?”

“No, sir.”

He looked almost impressed.

At the door, he stopped.

“My youngest, too?”

I’d heard that question before. Different children. Same question.

“Yes, sir.”

Biden asked me how old I was.

“Seventy-four.”

“Jesus. You’ve been carrying this a long time.”

He pushed the folder back across the table without finishing it.

“Tell me.”

So I did.

On September 3, 2081, every human being will hear three knocks from the nearest door.

We must not open it.

We must not explain why.

Every explanation we’ve tested causes people to open the door.

Biden sat with his hands folded.

Finally, he asked the question none of the others had.

“Who told us?”

I took out the last photograph.

In it, I’m standing outside a door, much older than I am now.

On the back, in my handwriting, are five words.

*I thought it was her.*
