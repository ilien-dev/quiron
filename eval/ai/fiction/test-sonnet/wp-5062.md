# The Prophecy Problem

The villain's lair was, by any objective measure, an excellent villain's lair — obsidian columns, a lava moat, a throne carved to look like it was screaming — and Kessandra had spent eleven years building toward this exact confrontation, and now she couldn't focus on any of it because the hero and the princess were standing four feet apart, radiating absolutely zero romantic tension, and it was driving her insane.

"Are you two going to fight me, or should I come back after you've figured out whatever this is," she said, gesturing between them with her staff.

The hero, a broad-shouldered young man named Garrick with a jawline the bards had already written three separate ballads about, blinked. "Figured what out?"

"This." She gestured again, more emphatically. "The tension. The moment. You rescued her from a burning tower two chapters ago. She's beautiful, you're heroic, the lighting in here is genuinely very flattering, I designed it that way on purpose, and you're just — standing there. Like coworkers."

The princess, Aveline, frowned, looking at Garrick as though seeing him for the first time and finding the experience mildly clarifying rather than moving. "I mean, he's very brave. I respect that enormously."

"Respect," Kessandra repeated flatly.

"She's extremely competent with a blade," Garrick offered, like a reference letter. "I admire that in a person."

"Admire." Kessandra pinched the bridge of her nose. Behind her, one of her lava-moat guardians — a creature she'd spent considerable resources animating specifically to look intimidating during dramatic pauses — had apparently given up on the confrontation and was now floating listlessly near the ceiling. "This is a prophecy, you understand. It's written down. Ancient text, old kingdom, illuminated manuscript, the whole apparatus. 'The chosen one and the last daughter of the sun throne shall unite, and their union shall end the age of shadow.' I've read it forty times. It doesn't say 'shall respect each other's competence.' It says unite."

"We assumed that meant, like, teaming up," Garrick said.

"It does not mean teaming up! Why would an ancient prophet care whether you team up! Nobody writes a prophecy about people cooperating professionally, that's not a story, that's a performance review!"

Aveline crossed her arms. "Well, what do you want us to do, force it? I'm not going to manufacture feelings because a dead prophet from four hundred years ago had expectations."

"I'm not asking you to manufacture anything, I'm asking why there's nothing there to manufacture from! Do you know what I gave up to be a proper villain in this narrative? I had a sister. I had a garden. I had a perfectly nice cottage with a view of the eastern hills, and I burned all of it down, metaphorically and in one case literally, because the story needed a shadow for you two to unite against. And you won't even hold hands!"

"I held her hand during the tower rescue," Garrick said, wounded.

"That doesn't count, you were carrying her down a ladder, that's occupational, that's not romance, that's workplace safety."

Aveline stepped forward, studying Kessandra with an expression that had shifted from confusion into something more genuinely curious. "Wait. You're upset about this. Like, actually upset. Why does it matter to you whether we fall in love?"

Kessandra opened her mouth to deliver a villain speech about cosmic balance and narrative necessity, and found, humiliatingly, that what came out instead was smaller and truer than she'd planned.

"Because if the prophecy doesn't resolve the way it's supposed to, none of this meant anything," she said. "The eleven years. The sister I don't speak to anymore. The garden. If you two don't fall in love and end the age of shadow together, then I just — burned my whole life down for nothing. For a plot that doesn't complete. And I would very much like it to have been for something."

Silence settled over the obsidian throne room, broken only by the half-hearted crackle of the lava moat and the guardian creature drifting gently back down from the ceiling, apparently sensing the dramatic register had changed.

Garrick and Aveline looked at each other — really looked, for what seemed like the first time in the entire story — and something passed between them that hadn't been there a minute ago, not romance exactly, but the first flicker of two people recognizing a shared, absurd predicament.

"She has a point," Aveline said slowly. "About the garden."

"I did like her cottage description," Garrick agreed. "Very evocative."

Kessandra watched, cautiously hopeful, as the two of them took one small, tentative step closer together, and thought that maybe — just maybe — eleven years of villainy were about to pay off after all, even if not in any way the old prophet could possibly have specified.
