# Escort Service

The first zombie I ever killed was trying to save my life.

I didn't understand that at the time. At the time it was just teeth and rot and a shriek that didn't sound human anymore, and I put a fire axe through its skull because that's what you did, that's what everyone had been doing for six months, and I didn't stop to ask questions of something that used to be a mail carrier named Gary.

It was only after, when I turned around, that I saw the other three of them standing in a loose semicircle behind me. Not attacking. Just standing. Watching the alley mouth I'd been about to walk into, where a whole crowd of the things — snarling, hungry, the real kind — had been about to spill out and take me apart.

Gary and his three friends had gotten there first.

I ran. Obviously I ran. You don't stop to have a conversation with the dead. But it happened again three days later, a different group, herding me away from a collapsed overpass before it groaned and came down in a wave of dust and rebar that would have flattened me. And again after that, and again, until I stopped being able to tell myself it was coincidence.

They never speak. I want to be clear about that, in case anyone reading this — if anyone is ever reading this — thinks I've lost the plot completely and started imagining conversations with corpses. They don't talk. They don't seem to think, not in any way I recognize. But something in them still orbits me, the way iron filings orbit a magnet, and whatever that something is, it doesn't want me to die.

I've tested it. God forgive me, I've tested it, because six months of near-misses will make anyone curious enough to be reckless. I walked toward a group of the fast ones once, on purpose, just to see. Four of my escorts peeled off and intercepted, tearing into their own kind with a viciousness they never show me, buying me the ten seconds I needed to get behind a chain-link fence. They didn't celebrate. They didn't even look at me afterward. They just resumed their slow, patient orbit, like nothing had happened, like it was simply Tuesday.

I've stopped trying to lose them. There's no point. I tried for two weeks straight back in month three, doubling back through department stores, wading through a river to break the scent trail, anything I could think of, and every single time, by nightfall, I'd hear that low collective groan somewhere behind me, and I'd know they'd found me again. Not hunting. Following. Herding, maybe, though toward what I still don't know.

There are seven of them now, more than there've ever been at once. I recognize some of their faces, the ones still recognizable — a woman in a torn cardigan who I think used to work at the pharmacy on Fifth, a teenager in a letterman jacket that doesn't fit him anymore because half his shoulder's gone. I don't know why they chose me out of everyone left in this dying city. I don't know if "chose" is even the right word for whatever passes through what's left of their brains.

Last night I dreamed I asked Gary why. In the dream he opened his mouth to answer and only made that same wet, ruined sound they all make, and I woke up before I found out whether it would have meant anything.

Tonight they're doing something they've never done before. Instead of trailing behind me at their usual distance, they're gathering close, all seven of them, pressing in around the boarded-up gas station where I've been sleeping, restless, agitated, groaning low in their throats like something is coming.

I don't know what they're protecting me from this time.

I just know I've learned to trust it when they get like this, and so I'm sitting in the dark with my back against the cinderblock wall, my axe across my knees, waiting to find out what seven dead strangers think is worth keeping me alive for.
