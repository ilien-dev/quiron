# Are You Ready to Die?

The words had been on Wren's collarbone since the day she was born, small dark script curling just above her heart in a language of ink no dermatologist could ever explain: ARE YOU READY TO DIE?

Not exactly the soulmate tattoo people dreamed about. Wren had grown up watching other kids show off their marks with a kind of shy pride — I THINK YOU DROPPED THIS, or WANT TO SHARE MY UMBRELLA, or, in one memorable case from her high school, DO YOU BELIEVE IN AURAS, which everyone agreed was objectively insufferable but at least sounded like the start of a conversation. Wren's mark got her sympathetic looks from strangers on buses and, once, an unsolicited pamphlet about surviving abusive relationships from a well-meaning woman at a farmer's market.

She was twenty-six and had mostly stopped expecting to meet whoever it belonged to. It was easier that way — easier to assume the words were a cruel cosmic joke, a threat with no context, than to spend her whole life flinching at every stranger who might say them.

She was working the night shift at County General when the man came in through the ambulance bay doors, and she heard the words before she saw who'd spoken them, and her whole body went cold and electric at once.

"Are you ready to die?"

It wasn't menace. That was the first thing she registered, underneath the roar in her own ears. It was clinical, urgent, the voice of a paramedic doing triage, addressed to the gurney he was wheeling in, to a teenage boy gray-faced and barely conscious from blood loss, and it took her a full three seconds to understand the question hadn't been for her at all. It had simply been said, in her presence, by the person whose voice matched the mark on her skin exactly the way she'd always imagined it would, down to the particular rasp on the D.

She looked up at him properly for the first time. Mid-thirties, dark eyes gone wide with the specific exhaustion of someone six hours into a double shift, a smear of someone else's blood across his forearm. His name badge read D. OKONKWO — PARAMEDIC.

"That's — " she started, and had no idea how to finish the sentence in the middle of a trauma intake, with a dying teenager between them and an entire ER erupting into motion around her.

"Not now," he said, not unkindly, already moving, already calling out vitals to the trauma team converging around the gurney. "Talk after."

She should have known, she thought later, standing outside the staff lounge with her hands still shaking two hours after the boy had been stabilized and sent up to surgery, that a soulmate mark reading ARE YOU READY TO DIE? would belong to someone who said it constantly, professionally, to strangers on the worst day of their lives. It made a horrible, obvious kind of sense once she saw it in context. It wasn't a threat. It was a question he asked dozens of times a week, gently, urgently, to people teetering on an edge he was trying to pull them back from.

He found her there afterward, badge still crooked, looking somehow both wrung out and lit up from the inside.

"So," he said. "I'm going to guess, from the way you froze up back there, that you've been carrying that particular sentence around for a while."

She pulled her collar aside without quite deciding to, showing him the script above her collarbone, and watched something complicated move across his face — recognition, and underneath it, a flinch she understood completely, because she'd spent twenty-six years having the same reaction to the words themselves.

"I always hated that mine sounded like a threat," he admitted. "Had a girlfriend in med school who saw it and left the same week. Didn't stick around long enough to hear me say it to a car accident victim instead of to her."

"I always hated that mine sounded like I was destined to meet someone terrifying," Wren said. "Turns out I was destined to meet someone who says the scariest sentence in the English language for a living and means something completely different by it every single time."

He laughed, surprised, the exhaustion in his face cracking open into something warmer. "Devon," he said, holding out a hand that still smelled faintly of antiseptic.

"Wren." She shook it, and something settled in her chest that had been unsettled for as long as she could remember — not romance yet, not quite, but the enormous, quiet relief of finally understanding a sentence that had followed her since birth, of watching a threat resolve, in real time, into the exact opposite of what it had always sounded like.

"For what it's worth," Devon said, still holding her hand a beat longer than the handshake strictly required, "I am. Ready, I mean. Whenever you want to actually find out what this is."

Wren looked down at the words on her own skin, then up at the man who'd spent his whole career asking dying strangers the question written there, and decided, for the first time in twenty-six years, that she was ready too.
