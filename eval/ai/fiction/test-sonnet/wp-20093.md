# Renewal

I don't remember selling my soul. I remember the swing set behind my grandmother's house, and the smell of rain on hot asphalt, and a man in a very nice coat crouching down to my height and asking if I wanted to make a deal that would make my parents stop fighting.

I said yes. I was five. I said yes to a lot of things at five — yes to vegetables I hated if it meant dessert, yes to bedtime if it meant one more story, yes to a stranger's coat pocket where he pulled out a piece of paper that smelled like a birthday candle just blown out.

I don't remember signing anything. I don't remember him leaving. What I remember is that the fighting did stop, not long after, and I decided that was just how things worked out sometimes, the way things work out for five-year-olds who don't ask hard questions about luck.

I'm twenty-one now. It's a Tuesday. I'm making coffee in my apartment with the radio on, and there's a knock at the door that has a rhythm to it I don't recognize but immediately, unaccountably, dread.

The man in the coat looks exactly the same. That's the first thing I notice, once the shock of remembering — actually remembering, all at once, sixteen years dropping into place like a key turning a lock — knocks the wind out of me. He hasn't aged a day. He's holding a folder.

"Hello again," he says, like we're old coworkers. "It's that time."

"What time."

"Contract renewal." He steps past me into my apartment without waiting to be asked, the way you'd walk into your own house, and sets the folder on my kitchen counter next to the coffee I haven't finished making. "Standard seven-year term, up for review. I'll admit, most clients remember signing. You were young. I did wonder if that would cause complications."

"I was five."

"You were," he agrees, not unkindly. "Your parents' marriage was falling apart in a way that was going to leave some genuinely nasty scars on you. You wanted it fixed. I fixed it. That was payment enough for the first term — a childhood without the divorce, without the custody fights, without your father's drinking getting as bad as it was on its way to getting. You've had sixteen good years because of that signature."

I sit down because my legs have started negotiating with the floor without consulting me first.

"And now?"

"Now we renew, or we don't." He opens the folder. Inside is a single page, handwritten in a script too neat to be anyone's actual handwriting, the ink the color of dried blood in a way that feels less dramatic and more like a printer running low on toner. "The terms are simple. Another seven years of — let's call it favorable circumstance. Doors open, opportunities that would otherwise pass you by, a general tilting of luck in your direction. In exchange, when the final term comes due — and there will be a final term, we don't do these forever — the payment comes out of what's left. What's actually yours to give."

"My soul."

"Your soul," he agrees. "Though I'd point out you already gave that up at five. This isn't a new sale. This is just deciding whether you want the goods that came with it."

I think about the last sixteen years. The stable house. The parents who, whatever their other flaws, never once screamed at each other in front of me. The father who never touched a drink after a certain Tuesday I don't remember, that I now understand wasn't chance.

I think about what I don't know yet — what's coming, what doors haven't opened, what I might lose by walking away from a deal that was never really mine to make and yet made me, brick by brick, into whoever I am now.

"What happens if I don't sign?"

He studies me for a moment, and for the first time since he walked in, something almost like respect crosses his face.

"Then the last sixteen years stand as they are, unpaid for beyond what's already spent, and you go back to being an ordinary young man with an ordinary young man's luck. No favors. No further debt." He closes the folder halfway. "Most people sign. It's easier to keep taking than to stop and pay for what you've already had."

I look at the pen he's already set beside the paper, and I think, for the first time in my life, about what it might mean to say no to something.
