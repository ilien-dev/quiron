# The Horned Island

Captain James Hook had buried three ships and forty men chasing rumors across the Aegean before he found a sailor drunk enough, or frightened enough, to tell him the truth about the island.

"Children go missing from the coastal villages," the old sailor said, gripping his cup with both hands though the tavern was warm. "Every generation, a handful. Taken from their beds at night, and no sign of struggle, no blood, nothing but an open window and a smell like crushed grass and wine left too long in the sun. The mothers pray to every saint they know. It doesn't help. It's never helped, not in a hundred years."

"Taken by who?"

The sailor lowered his voice, as though the name itself might summon its owner. "They say a young god, ageless, wild-eyed, wearing the horns of a goat on his brow — that he sails from an island no chart shows, sung there by pipes only children can hear, and that he takes them to become vessels. For what, nobody agrees. Old magic. Fey blood. Something that needs young, unspoiled souls to live inside."

Hook had heard stranger things in his years at sea, and buried men who'd laughed at stranger things than this. He bought the sailor another drink and asked for a heading.

It took him four months and the loss of two more good sailors — swallowed by a sea that seemed to actively resent the direction he was sailing — before the island rose out of the fog like something surfacing from a long sleep. It should not have existed by any chart in his possession. The trees grew wrong, twisted toward a sun that sat too low and too gold in a sky the color of a healing bruise, and the moment his boots touched the sand, he heard it: music, high and reedy, drifting from somewhere deep in the interior, carrying on it the unmistakable, delighted laughter of children.

He left half his remaining crew guarding the ship and went in with the rest, cutlass drawn, following the sound through undergrowth that seemed to part for him with unnatural cooperation, as if the island itself were curious what he'd do once he arrived.

The clearing, when he found it, held more children than he could count at a glance — dozens, dressed in scraps of a hundred different decades and countries, dancing in a loose, dazed circle around a boy who could not have been older than sixteen by his face, though something ancient moved behind his eyes. Goat horns curled from his temples, small and sharp, still growing. He played a set of reed pipes with fingers too long for a boy's hand, and every child in the clearing moved like a puppet on the same delighted string.

"Another one," the horned boy said without missing a note, without even opening his eyes. "How many captains have I watched wash up on this sand, hook or sword or musket in hand, certain they'd be the one to leave with something that isn't theirs?"

"I've buried men enough on the way here to know this isn't a courtesy call," Hook said. "Release them."

"They're not prisoners." The piping never stopped. "Ask them yourself, if you like. Ask if they want to go back to cold beds and colder fathers, back to villages that will bury half of them in fevers before they're grown. I offer them forever. I offer them a purpose older than your God and kinder than he's ever been to children."

"You offer them nothing. You hollow them for your own use and call it a gift."

For the first time, the piping faltered, and the boy's ancient eyes opened fully, fixing on Hook with an intensity that made the captain's namesake hand ache with old, phantom pain.

"You're the pirate," the boy said slowly, something like delight creeping into his voice. "The one who fears clocks. How fitting — the man terrified of growing old, come to rescue children from the one place they're offered exactly that. I wonder which of us understands fear less."

"I understand enough to know a predator when I hear one flatter himself as a god."

The boy's smile widened, sharp at the edges in a way that had nothing to do with teeth. He lifted the pipes again, and the music that came out this time was different — lower, older, a summons rather than a lure, and around the clearing the shadows between the twisted trees began to move with a weight and purpose the wind alone could not explain.

Hook set his jaw, raised his cutlass, and thought, not for the first time in his long and complicated life, that the stories men told about him were never half as strange as the truth.

"Then let's see," he said, "which of us the children choose to follow home."
