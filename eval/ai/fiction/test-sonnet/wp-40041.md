# Field Report, Site 114-B

Dr. Amara Osei had excavated seventeen sites in her career, and none of them had made her hands shake the way this one did.

The dig was routine, on paper. A collapsed structure on the outskirts of what used to be called Ohio, dated with reasonable confidence to the early twenty-first century, part of the broader Reclamation Survey cataloguing what little the Warm Centuries had left behind. Most such sites yielded nothing but slag and stubborn plastic that would outlive the sun. This one, buried under four meters of compacted silt in what had once been a basement, had yielded something that still worked.

"Run it again," she said.

Her assistant, a nervous postgrad named Teodor who had never worked a find above Category Three importance, ran the scan again. The object sat under the imaging array like something asleep: a slab of dark glass, roughly rectangular, the length of Amara's hand, with a single circular depression near its base. No visible seams. No corrosion, despite three centuries in acidic soil that should have reduced it to nothing.

"Power signature's still there," Teodor said, voice tight. "Faint. But there. Whatever's inside it hasn't died."

Amara had theories, the way every archaeologist develops theories within the first ten minutes of finding something they don't understand, because the alternative is admitting you're looking at a total blank. Her first theory was religious — some manner of household idol, given the care with which it had been placed, upright, deliberately, on what looked like it had once been furniture. Devotional objects from the pre-Collapse era were common enough; people had worshipped stranger things than glass slabs.

Her second theory, forming as Teodor adjusted the scanner and the readout bloomed with data neither of them fully understood, was worse. Better, maybe. She wasn't sure yet.

"There's a symbol," Teodor said. "Etched on the back. Very faint."

Amara leaned over the case. Under raking light, she could just make out the shape — a stylized fruit, bitten once, the wound of the bite rendered in a few economical lines. She'd seen the symbol before, in a handful of similarly desperate hoards from the same period, always associated with objects of high personal value. Whatever it meant to the people who'd made it, it had meant something.

"Try the depression," she said. "Careful. If there's stored charge in there, I want a full containment field up before you touch it."

It took four hours to rig the field, four hours during which Amara found herself unable to look away from the slab, turning theories over the way she imagined the people of this era had turned the object itself over, absently, without thinking, a thousand times a day. A tool. It had the weight distribution of a tool, something meant to be held constantly, carried everywhere, referred to instinctively.

When Teodor finally pressed the depression, nothing happened for eleven seconds, and then the glass came alive.

Light. Not violent, not alarming — a soft, even glow that resolved, after a moment of adjustment neither of them was prepared for, into an image. Rows of small colored shapes. Text in a script Amara could almost, almost read, degraded by three centuries of dormant storage into flickering fragments. A single word near the top, larger than the rest, pulsing faintly: a percentage, ticking upward with agonizing slowness, as though the object were waking from a sleep it hadn't expected to survive.

"It's booting," Teodor whispered, and Amara understood, with the particular vertigo of a scholar watching a hypothesis become fact in real time, that he was right, that this was the word for it, an old word surfacing out of her own training in ancient digital archaeology, a word she had read a hundred times in academic papers and never once expected to use while standing over an actual find.

Forty percent. Fifty.

Somewhere in that glass and dead metal, whatever this person had trusted with the small, constant business of their life — their contacts, their photographs, their arguments, their love, whatever people three hundred years ago poured into these things without thinking twice about it — was still, impossibly, trying to remember itself.

"Whatever we find in there," Amara said quietly, watching the number climb, "treat it like a person's diary. Not an artifact. A diary."

Teodor nodded, and they waited together in the ruined basement, in the dark, for a dead man's phone to finish turning back on.
