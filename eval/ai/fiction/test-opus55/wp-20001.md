# The Parts Nobody Reads

Marguerite Oyelaran knew she was fictional the way you know you're dreaming: without proof, but with total certainty.

It started with the weather. Every morning was either "crisp" or "heavy with the promise of rain." Never just Tuesday. Then there were the people. The barista at Delmont's had green eyes and one personality trait (sardonic), and when Marguerite asked her where she'd grown up, the woman simply smiled and said, "Your usual?" as if the question had slid off a surface too smooth to hold it.

Not enough budget for a backstory, Marguerite figured. Fair enough.

She took the coffee. It tasted the way coffee tastes in books: "bitter and perfect." She'd never once had a lukewarm cup. She'd never once burned her tongue. It was a little lonely, honestly, living in a world with no bad coffee.

---

The tricky part about knowing was the scenes.

She could feel them coming the way you feel a sneeze. The air would thicken. Details would sharpen: the exact grain of the oak table, the precise slant of the light through the blinds. Someone would walk in with a significant expression. Then she'd say something cleverer than she was, and her heart would do things hearts don't actually do, and then — nothing. A white gap. And she'd be somewhere else, three days later, holding a letter.

She'd tried resisting once. Her ex-husband (described, on his first appearance, as "handsome in a way that had stopped mattering") showed up at her door, and she could feel the confrontation building, the dialogue loading like a spring.

She said, "I'm not doing this."

He blinked. The light through the blinds flickered, uncertain.

"I know what you're here to say," she told him. "You're going to tell me about the house. I'm going to cry, but in a restrained way, which shows growth. Can we skip it?"

There was a long pause, the kind marked by a line break.

Then he said, "Marguerite, it's about the house," and her eyes were already stinging, and she understood that you could argue with the story, but you couldn't win.

---

So she stopped fighting the scenes and started living in the gaps.

That's where she was free. In the white space between chapters, nobody was writing. Nobody was watching. In the gaps she did things that would never make the cut: she cleaned the grout in her bathroom. She learned the names of every pigeon outside her window (eleven, all of them called Gerald, because she was tired). She read the same mediocre mystery novel four times and enjoyed it more each time, because nothing in it was about her.

She called her mother in the gaps. Her mother didn't exist on the page, had never been mentioned, so in the gaps she could be anyone. Some days she was gentle. Some days she was a retired trapeze artist with opinions about tomatoes. Marguerite loved her either way.

---

Lately the scenes were getting closer together. The light was always slanting now. People kept entering with significant expressions.

She knew what that meant. The ending was near.

She wasn't scared, exactly. Mostly she was curious whether she'd get a good last line. She hoped it would be something quiet. Not a death, not a wedding, just her at the window with the Geralds, the coffee for once a little lukewarm, nothing happening at all.

And then, she suspected, someone would close the book.

And then the whole world would be gap.

She'd finally have time to fix the hinge on the kitchen cabinet. She'd been meaning to. It was the kind of thing nobody ever writes down.
