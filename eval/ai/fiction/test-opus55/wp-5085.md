# Sugar

The first thing they took was the water tower, which made sense. It was the tallest thing in Halvern, and our name was painted across it in letters my grandfather helped hang. They didn't paint over it. They just raised their flag beside it, so for three months the town looked like it was introducing itself to a stranger.

The second thing they took was the school, and the third was the church. The fourth was nothing in particular. After that it stopped feeling like taking and started feeling like living somewhere else without ever having moved.

My mother kept the diner open. People said she was brave, and people said she was a collaborator, sometimes in the same breath and sometimes the same people. She said only that the grill didn't know there was a war on, and neither did the eggs.

The soldiers came in at six, after their shift change. Mostly they were boys, nineteen or twenty, with rifles they leaned against the booths the way my brother used to lean his hockey stick. They pointed at the menu because they couldn't read it, and they paid in their own money, which my mother took without a word and kept in a coffee can under the register.

"What's it for?" I asked her once.

"After," she said.

There was one I noticed. He was thinner than the rest, with a cowlick that stood straight up no matter how much water he flattened it with. He sat at the counter instead of a booth. Every morning he ordered coffee and stirred in four sugars, slowly and one at a time, tapping the spoon twice on the rim after each.

My brother used to do that. Four sugars, two taps. Danny had been gone eleven months by then. He'd been one of the first to go when the trucks crossed the river, and we never got him back, not even a box.

I hated the soldier for the spoon.

So one morning I filled the sugar dispenser in front of his seat with salt.

My mother saw me do it. She didn't stop me. She went back to the grill, and her shoulders got very still, the way they did when she was listening hard.

He came in at six and sat at the counter. He poured: one, tap tap. Two, tap tap. Three. Four.

He drank.

I waited for him to spit it out, throw the cup, maybe draw the pistol at his hip. I'd decided I didn't care which. Some part of me wanted him to, so the hate would have somewhere solid to stand.

He swallowed, and then he took another sip. Without looking up, he said in careful, terrible English, "It is good. Thank you."

He finished the whole cup and left his money on the counter, plus a little extra, folded small.

After he left I stood there with the salt shaker in my hand and felt like a fool, though I couldn't have said exactly why. My mother scraped the grill clean. Then she came over, unfolded the extra bill, and found writing on it in pencil, in his language. Underneath it was a crude drawing of the river and the bridge, with an X on the eastern road and a number: *4*.

It took us two days and old Mr. Pasternak, who had been a translator before he was a drunk, to work it out. *Four days. They leave by the east road. Do not be on it.*

On the fourth day the trucks rolled out before dawn. Three hours later the east road, the one that would have been jammed with families heading for the coast, disappeared under artillery from the other side. Nobody in Halvern was on it.

He never came back to the diner. I don't know if he made it home or if he was ever anywhere near the east road himself. I don't know his name.

The water tower still says HALVERN. Their flag came down, and somebody left the pole standing, and none of us have gotten around to removing it.

My mother still keeps the coffee can under the register. When I asked her again what it was for, she said she was saving it until someone came in who could tell her where to send it.

Every morning I fill the sugar dispensers, and I check them twice.
