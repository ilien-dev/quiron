# The Last Speaker

The Bureau's rules were simple: you could look, but you couldn't touch. Travelers arrived as a kind of weather, a faint pressure in the air that dogs sometimes noticed and people never did. You couldn't move a teacup. You couldn't change a thing. That was the point. The past was a library, not a workshop.

Inés Varga had waited eleven years for her slot.

She arrived on a hillside in northern California on an October afternoon in 1891. The grass was gold and brittle. Below her, a woman sat on an overturned crate outside a cabin, shelling beans into a tin bowl. Her name, according to a single census line and one missionary's letter, was Maria Tsoq. She was sixty-three. She was the last person alive who spoke Kalehi as her first language.

No recording of Kalehi existed. There were forty-one words written down by a surveyor who couldn't spell, and a grammar sketch that was mostly guesses. Inés had spent her career on those guesses.

She sat down in the grass, which did not bend under her, and listened.

For a long time there was nothing but beans hitting tin. Then a boy came up the path, maybe nine years old, carrying a sack of flour on his shoulder. Maria said something to him in English, sharp and practical: put it inside, wipe your feet. He obeyed. When he came back out, she patted the crate beside her, and he sat.

Then she began to talk in Kalehi.

Inés had imagined this moment so often that she had stopped believing it could happen. In her imagination the language had been clipped and formal, like the surveyor's list. It was nothing like that. It ran. It had a rising lilt at the ends of phrases, as if every sentence leaned forward toward the next one. There were sounds she had no symbol for, a soft catch in the throat, a click that was almost a laugh.

The boy answered haltingly, mixing in English. Maria corrected him, gently, and made him say a word again. Then again. He rolled his eyes. She laughed and cuffed the back of his head.

Inés wrote nothing down. Instruments didn't cross over, and paper was useless to a ghost. Her memory was the only recorder, and she had trained it for years for this. She focused on rhythm first, then vowels. She was not here to save the language. Nobody could do that. Maria would die in four years. The boy would grow up and go to a boarding school where speaking Kalehi earned a strap across the hands, and he would forget most of it. History had already happened. The Bureau didn't let you forget that.

But that afternoon on the hill, Maria was teaching him anyway.

She pointed at the hills and named them. She pointed at a hawk and said a word that had four syllables and sounded like wind moving through something hollow. She held up a bean and made the boy say its name, and then she said a long sentence and the boy laughed so hard he nearly fell off the crate. Inés would never know what the joke was. She found she didn't mind.

The light went orange, then red. Maria stood, knees cracking, and said something to the boy that ended in that forward-leaning lilt. He answered with the same lilt, perfectly, without thinking. She looked at him for a moment, then went inside.

Inés's recall tone sounded, low and patient, like a held breath.

Back in the present she sat in the Bureau's white recovery room with a glass of water she didn't drink. A technician asked if she wanted to dictate notes while the details were still fresh.

"In a minute," she said.

She closed her eyes and heard the hawk-word again, four syllables of wind. She didn't know what it meant yet. She would spend years finding out.

She already knew it had been said, and heard, and answered on that hill. Nothing she did could change that, and she was glad.
