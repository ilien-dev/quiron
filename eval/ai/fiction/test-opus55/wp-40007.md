# The Night Your Grandfather Proposed

"Tell us again," said Mia, already in pajamas, already sure of the answer.

Grandma Rosa set down her tea with the care of someone handling an artifact. Across the living room, Grandpa Teo lowered his newspaper an inch, the way he always did when this particular story began.

"It was the summer of 1971," Grandma said. "I was working nights at the Silver Lantern, a diner on the edge of town. Terrible coffee. Excellent pie."

"The pie was also terrible," Grandpa said from behind the paper.

"The pie was *excellent*, and you ate two slices every night for three weeks, so hush." She turned back to the children. "Your grandfather came in every evening at eleven fifteen. Always the same booth. Always the same order. Coffee, black. Cherry pie. And he never said more than four words to me."

"Five," said Grandpa.

"Five words. 'Coffee, please. And the pie.'"

Leo, who was nine and therefore a scholar, counted on his fingers. "That's five."

"Thank you, Leo. Now. On the twenty-second night, he didn't come in at eleven fifteen. Or eleven thirty. I remember being angry about it, which was ridiculous, because I didn't even know his name. At midnight, the door opened, and in he walked. Wearing a suit."

"A rented suit," Grandpa clarified.

"A rented suit that was two sizes too big. The sleeves went past his knuckles. He looked like a little boy at a funeral. And he had flowers. Carnations, which he'd clearly bought at the gas station, because they still had the price sticker on them."

Mia giggled. Grandpa's newspaper dipped another inch, revealing eyes that were trying very hard not to smile.

"He sat down in his booth, and I came over with the coffee pot, and he said, 'I'm not here for pie tonight.' Which was more words than he'd said in three weeks combined. And then he stood up, knocked over the sugar dispenser, got down on one knee in a puddle of sugar, and opened a little velvet box."

"You'd been on zero dates," Leo pointed out.

"Zero dates," Grandma agreed. "I told him he was out of his mind. He said, 'Probably. But I've watched you for three weeks, and you're kind to every drunk and every trucker and every lonely old man who comes through that door, and I'd like to spend my life with someone like that.' And then he said, 'Also, I can't afford to keep eating this pie.'"

"That part's true," said Grandpa.

"So I said..." Grandma paused, as she always did, letting the silence stretch.

"*What did you say?*" both children demanded, though they'd heard it forty times.

"I said, 'Ask me again after you've taken me to dinner somewhere that isn't here.'"

"And he did!" Mia shouted.

"Not yet," said Grandma, and her voice dropped low. "Because just as he was getting up off that sticky floor, the window behind the counter shattered. Glass everywhere. The cook screamed. The lights flickered. And a figure dropped down from the ceiling vent, all in black, a sword strapped across its back, landing silent as snow right between your grandfather and me."

Leo's mouth fell open. "That's not how the story goes."

"That's exactly how the story goes. I've just never told you the ending, because your mother made me promise to wait until you were old enough."

"Rosa," Grandpa said warningly, folding his newspaper completely now.

"Oh, they're old enough, Teo." She picked up her tea, took a long, slow sip, and smiled at her husband with forty-five years of mischief behind her eyes.

"Your grandfather stood up, rolled those ridiculous sleeves back, and said, very calmly, 'Rosa, please step behind the counter.'"

She set down the cup.

"And that's when the ninja attacked!"
