# The Wrong Gift

I wanted to play the piano. That's all. Not fame, not money. Just to sit at the bench and have my hands know what to do.

I tried lessons first. Six years of them. My teacher, a kind woman named Ottilie, finally told me, gently, that some people have hands that listen and some people have hands that only hear. Mine only heard.

So I went to the concert halls instead.

The first was Marcus Vell, who played Rachmaninoff like he was arguing with God and winning. I waited for him in the parking garage after a Tuesday recital. It was quicker than I expected. Afterward I went home, sat at my upright, and placed my fingers on the keys.

Nothing. Still clumsy and still deaf.

But the next morning, when my neighbor's dog barked for forty minutes straight, I didn't mind. I felt a calm I'd never known. *Patience.* Marcus Vell, it turned out, had been proudest of his patience with his autistic son. Not his Rachmaninoff. His patience.

That was when I understood the rules.

The second was a jazz pianist in Chicago, a man whose left hand could walk a bass line drunk. I got his laugh. Big, warm, and contagious. Strangers started telling me their problems on the train.

The third was a church organist who had played the same Lutheran hymns for fifty-one years. I thought, *surely*. Surely a woman who gave her whole life to the instrument believed the instrument was her gift.

I got her ability to forgive.

That one was hard to carry. I lay awake for weeks, forgiving everyone. My father. The boys from eighth grade. Marcus Vell's son, for nothing, for existing. Myself, almost. Almost.

By the eleventh, I had a collection. Generosity. A good memory for names. The ability to make a perfect omelet. Courage. Honesty, which made the next three very difficult to explain to my landlord. I was, by any measure, becoming a wonderful person. People liked me. A woman at the bakery asked me to dinner.

And I still couldn't play "Für Elise" without looking at my hands.

I started researching more carefully. I wanted someone vain about it. Someone who'd answer the question *what's the best thing about you?* with *my playing* before you finished asking. I found him: Dmitri Haas, the international prodigy, a man who had once walked off stage in Vienna because a cough "disrespected the Chopin."

I sat in the third row of his Boston recital, and I watched him play, and for the first time in years I felt something other than hunger. He was extraordinary. And he was miserable. You could see it in his shoulders. The music was the only thing he had, and it was eating him.

I waited for him by the stage door. He came out alone, smoking.

"You were wonderful," I said. Honesty, Victim Number Six. I couldn't have lied if I'd wanted to.

He looked at me with exhausted eyes. "I hate it," he said. "I've hated it since I was nine. But it's all I'm good at."

I had the wire in my pocket. My hands, my clumsy, deaf hands, were steady.

And then the forgiveness kicked in. And the patience, and the courage, and the honesty, and the warmth of a jazzman's laugh, and eleven other people's best selves all spoke up at once, crowding the small dark room where I used to live alone.

"Do you want to get a coffee?" I heard myself say.

He blinked. "What?"

"I'm a terrible pianist," I told him. "Truly awful. I'd like to tell you about it."

He laughed, a little.

We went to a diner. I told him about Ottilie, about my hands that only heard. He told me about Vienna, about the cough. Somewhere around the second refill, I realized I didn't want his gift anymore.

I think I finally had the one thing all of them had been trying to give me.

I just don't know whose it was.
