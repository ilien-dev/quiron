**The Annual Visit**

The first time, I was thirty-four and bleeding out on the shoulder of Route 9, my car folded around a telephone pole like a paper crane. The pain had already gone somewhere far away. Then he was crouching beside the shattered window, tall and patient, with a face like a coin worn smooth by too many hands.

"Are you ready to go?" he asked.

I laughed, which hurt, and said, "Nah. Not really."

He considered this. Then he nodded, stood, and walked off into the treeline. The paramedics told me later my heart had stopped for four minutes. I told them I'd been chatting.

---

He came back exactly one year later. I was making pancakes.

"Are you ready to go?"

I flipped a pancake. It landed badly. "Not yet. I just started a new job."

He nodded and left. The kitchen smelled faintly of cold earth for an hour afterward.

That's when I understood the deal. It wasn't a miracle. It was a question, and as long as I kept answering it the same way, the answer held.

---

Year five, I had a daughter. Death showed up in the hospital room while she slept on my chest, all eight pounds of her.

"Are you ready?"

I didn't even look up. "Look at her. What do you think?"

He looked at her for a long time. I swear something in his smooth face softened. Then he was gone.

---

Year twelve, my wife Mara asked me why I always cleared my calendar on the fourteenth of March. I told her the truth. She thought I was joking, then she thought I was sick, and then, the following March, she sat beside me on the porch and watched a tall figure walk up our driveway without leaving footprints in the snow.

"Are you ready?" he asked.

"No," I said.

Mara grabbed my hand so hard her nails left marks. After he left, she didn't speak for a long time. Finally she said, "Will he ask me?"

"I don't know."

He didn't. He came for her eighteen years later, on an ordinary Tuesday, without an appointment. I was holding her hand when it happened, and I looked up and saw him standing at the foot of the hospital bed.

"You didn't ask her," I said.

"She was ready," he said. "People usually are, when it's time. You're the only one who ever said no."

That March, when he came, I almost said yes. I sat with the word in my mouth for a full minute. Then my granddaughter called to ask how to fix the garbage disposal, and I said, "Not today," and went to find my wrench.

---

Year forty-one, my daughter turned gray. That was the year it started to feel wrong.

Year fifty-six, I buried her.

You're not supposed to outlive your children. There's a reason. It breaks something in the order of things, and you can feel the break every morning when you wake up. My hands shake now. My eyes are cataract-clouded. I've had both hips replaced and my knees sound like gravel. Doctors write papers about me. Reporters stopped coming around a decade ago because nobody believes it anymore.

And every March fourteenth, he walks up the path.

---

This year, I'm a hundred and twelve. I'm sitting on the same porch, in a different chair, because the old one finally gave out. He arrives at noon, as always.

"Are you ready to go?"

Here's what nobody tells you about saying no for seventy-eight years: it becomes who you are. It's a habit carved so deep it's the only groove left. I'm terrified of the word *yes*. Not of what comes after, but of the fact that saying it means admitting all the nos were just fear wearing a clever hat.

"Can I ask you something?" I said.

He waited.

"Why did you leave? That first time. I was joking."

He sat down beside me. I'd never seen him sit before. The porch boards didn't creak.

"Because you said no," he said simply. "Nobody had ever said no. I wanted to see what you'd do with it."

"And?"

He looked out at the yard, at the swing set my great-grandson had outgrown, at the apple tree Mara had planted, at the window of the room where my daughter was born.

"You did well," he said.

I felt something loosen in my chest. Something old.

"Next year," I said. "Ask me again next year."

He smiled. It was the first time.

"I'll bring coffee," he said.
