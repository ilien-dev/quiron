# Just Lovely

Marjorie Pell, who cut my hair twice a year and talked about her tomatoes the whole time, found me in the cereal aisle on a Tuesday.

"Oh, it's you!" She touched my elbow. Her cart held three cans of cat food and a single lemon. "I saw your gravestone today. Just lovely!"

She smiled the way you smile at someone's new car, then pushed her cart toward the dairy section, humming.

I stood holding a box of bran flakes I didn't want. I thought about following her. Instead I put the box back, left my basket in the aisle, and drove to the only cemetery in town.

Oakridge sits on a slope behind the Methodist church. I'd been there for my grandfather's funeral and never again. The afternoon was gray and wet, the grass spongy underfoot. I didn't know where to look, so I walked the rows the way you'd walk a parking lot looking for your car: every stone a small, wrong answer.

I found it near the back, under a crooked maple.

It was granite, pale and speckled, the kind of stone I'd have picked if anyone had ever asked me. My full name was cut into the face, including the middle name I hate. Below it was my birth date, correct to the day.

Below that: *2071.*

I did the math standing there in wet shoes. I'd be eighty-three.

The epitaph was smaller, in a soft serif font:

*She finally called her sister.*

I sat down on the grass. My jeans soaked through and I didn't care.

Leah and I hadn't spoken in six years. It wasn't one fight. It was a slow accumulation: a missed wedding toast, a comment about our mother's will, a voicemail I never returned, and then another. Somewhere along the way silence stopped being a decision and became the weather. I'd told myself I'd call when I knew what to say. I never knew what to say.

The groundskeeper came up the hill on a little cart, saw me, and slowed down.

"That one's been there a while," he said, nodding at the stone. "Long as I've worked here."

"How long is that?"

"Twenty-two years." He squinted at it. "Always thought it was odd. Nobody ever comes to it." He looked at me, then at the name, then at me again, and something in his face decided not to ask. "Moss grows on everything else up here. Never on that one."

He drove off.

I touched the stone. It was cold and real, and there was no moss on it at all.

I didn't know who had put it there. Maybe someone with a strange sense of humor. Maybe something with a stranger one. Maybe Marjorie Pell knew more about the town than she let on between stories about her heirloom Brandywines. But I noticed what the stone didn't say. It didn't say *beloved wife* or *devoted mother* or *she tried her best.* Out of everything I might have done in eighty-three years, it picked one thing, the thing I'd been carrying around like a stone in my coat.

It didn't say when, either. Only *finally.*

I took out my phone. Her number was still there, under *Leah* with no last name, as if I'd only ever known one. My thumb hovered over it for a long time. A crow landed on the maple, judged me, and left.

It rang four times. I almost hung up.

"Hello?" Her voice was older, and exactly the same.

"Hi," I said. "It's me. I'm sorry it took so long."

There was a pause long enough that I thought the call had dropped. Then I heard her breathe out, and it sounded like she'd been holding that breath for six years too.

"Where are you?" she asked. "It sounds windy."

I looked at the stone, at all that clean, mossless granite, at the dash between the dates where everything else would go.

"Somewhere lovely," I said. "Just lovely. I'll tell you about it."
