**The Second Verse**

The rope burned through my gloves as I lowered myself into the third chamber, my headlamp cutting a narrow white path through the dark. Forty meters down, the Cuevas del Silencio stopped feeling like rock and started feeling like a throat.

I'd told the rescue team I was mapping the eastern passages. That was half true. The other half was that I'd been coming down here every spring for twenty-two years, ever since my little brother Teo wandered off from a school trip and the mountain swallowed him whole.

They searched for nine days. They found one red sneaker near the river sump. Then they stopped.

I touched down on the chamber floor and unclipped. Water dripped somewhere to my left, slow and patient as a clock. I checked my compass, marked the wall with chalk, and was reaching for my notebook when I heard it.

*Lirra, lirra, the moon is a coin,*
*Spend it on dreaming, little one, join...*

My pencil snapped in my fingers.

No one else knew that song. My grandmother made it up in a kitchen in Oaxaca, half in Spanish, half in nonsense, and passed it to my mother, who sang it to us every night in the bedroom Teo and I shared. *Lirra* wasn't even a word. It was just a sound she liked.

*...the stars in your pocket, the dark at the door,*
*Lirra, lirra, you'll need nothing more.*

The voice was high and thin and unbearably small. A child's voice. Seven years old, maybe. Teo had been seven.

"Hello?" My own voice came out cracked. "Who's there?"

The song stopped. Then, after a long moment, it started again from the beginning, as if it hadn't heard me. As if it couldn't.

I followed it.

I'm a professional. I know what caves do to people: the way silence presses until your brain fills it with whispers, the way echoes can carry a sound half a kilometer through limestone and drop it in your ear like it came from beside you. I told myself all of that as I squeezed through a crack barely wide enough for my shoulders, as I waded thigh-deep through water so cold it felt like teeth.

None of it slowed me down.

The passage opened into a dome no map had ever recorded. Calcite curtains hung from the ceiling like frozen laundry, and in the center, where a thin seam of daylight once must have reached, there was a shelf of smooth stone.

The song was coming from there. Or from everywhere. The chamber hummed with it, the notes folding over themselves, worn soft by repetition. I understood, with a feeling like falling, that I wasn't hearing a voice. I was hearing what was left of one. Twenty-two years of a frightened little boy singing himself to sleep, soaked into stone the way water soaks into it, until the cave had learned the melody by heart.

On the shelf, curled on his side, was a small shape in a faded blue jacket. One red sneaker.

I sat down beside him. My headlamp trembled across the rock.

The song reached its end and paused, the way it must have paused for years, waiting. And I realized why it kept starting over.

Teo only knew the first verse. Mom wrote the second one later, the winter after we lost him, and sang it to me alone in our half-empty room. She never sang it again after that.

I took a breath that rattled.

*Lirra, lirra, the coin's in the sky,*
*Somebody's coming, so don't you cry.*
*They'll carry the lantern, they'll know you by name,*
*Lirra, lirra, they'll take you home again.*

My voice rang through the dome and came back to me, and for one moment the two of us were singing together.

Then the chamber went quiet. Truly quiet, for the first time.

I clipped my radio to my collar and called the surface.

"Found him," I said. "I'm bringing my brother home."
