Jimmy down the street said his dad knew everything.

Not *a lot*. Everything.

“He knows what’s at the bottom of the ocean,” Jimmy told us, squatting beside the storm drain. “He knows who shot President Kennedy. He knows why dogs spin before they lie down.”

“Everybody knows that,” Maya said. “They’re checking for snakes.”

Jimmy shook his head sadly. “That’s not why.”

We demanded proof.

So Jimmy led us to his house, a narrow blue place with wind chimes that never moved, even when the trees did. His dad was in the kitchen, peeling an apple into one long red ribbon.

“Dad,” Jimmy said, “what’s in Maya’s pocket?”

“A brass key to a diary she claims she doesn’t keep.”

Maya went pale.

“What did I have for breakfast?” I asked.

“You skipped breakfast. You told your mother you weren’t hungry, but really you were worried about today’s math test.”

I hadn’t told anyone about the test.

We tried harder questions.

What was buried beneath the old playground? A dog named Pickles, three stolen bicycles, and the left shoe of a man still living in Ohio.

How many stars were there? He gave us a number so long Jimmy got bored halfway through.

What was the worst thing Principal Dorsey had ever done? Jimmy’s dad stopped peeling.

“You’re too young for that answer,” he said.

That convinced us.

For weeks, his kitchen became our oracle. We asked who liked whom, where lost things had gone, whether aliens existed. They did, but were “neither close nor interesting.” He answered calmly, though he always looked tired afterward.

Then Maya asked, “When will I die?”

The apple skin snapped.

Jimmy’s dad set down the knife.

“I don’t answer future questions.”

“You don’t know?”

“I know.”

“So tell us.”

“No.”

Jimmy stared at the table. He had clearly heard this before.

We left without another question, but the mystery ate at me. Knowing everything sounded like the greatest superpower imaginable. Why refuse the only answers that mattered?

That night, I climbed through Jimmy’s bedroom window.

“You want to ask when you die,” he said.

“No. I want to ask if we can stop it.”

We crept downstairs.

His dad sat in the dark kitchen. No apple, no knife. Just two cups of cocoa and one glass of milk waiting on the table.

“You knew we’d come,” I whispered.

“Yes.”

“You know what we’re going to ask.”

“Yes.”

Jimmy stayed on the stairs.

“Can the future be changed?”

His dad slid the cocoa toward me. “No.”

The word seemed too small to hold so much terror.

“Then why not warn people?”

“Because knowing doesn’t change what happens.” He looked at Jimmy. “It changes everything before it happens.”

I didn’t understand.

He sighed. “Your mother will die someday. You know that much, vaguely, and still laugh with her. If you knew the date, every laugh would become subtraction.”

Jimmy came down then, crying silently.

His dad opened his arms, and Jimmy ran into them.

“How long?” Jimmy asked.

His dad held him tighter.

Outside, brakes screamed.

A car struck the oak tree in front of the house. The impact rattled every window. Jimmy’s father did not flinch.

I ran to the porch. The driver stumbled out, shaken but alive. The passenger seat had folded inward beneath a spear of broken branch.

An empty passenger seat.

When I turned back, Jimmy’s dad was watching me.

“You were supposed to be in that car,” I said.

“Yes.”

“But you said the future can’t change.”

“It can’t.”

He glanced at the clock. Midnight.

Across the street, my mother’s car pulled into our driveway. She got out, saw the wreck, and stared at the crushed passenger seat where Jimmy’s dad usually rode home from their Tuesday card game.

Then she saw him standing safely behind me.

She dropped her keys.

Jimmy’s dad closed his eyes.

For the first time, I understood how someone could know everything and still be surprised.

Jimmy hugged him around the waist.

“You lied,” Jimmy said.

His father began to laugh—a wild, broken laugh full of terror and joy.

“Yes,” he whispered.

And somewhere, for reasons only dogs knew, every dog on the street began to spin.
