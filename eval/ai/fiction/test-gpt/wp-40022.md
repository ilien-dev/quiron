The door appeared in the alley behind Mrs. Bell’s bakery on a Tuesday morning, fitted neatly between two brick walls that had never contained a doorway before.

It was a handsome door. Blue as a summer sky, with a brass knob polished to a small, accusing sun. At eye level hung a white card.

DO NOT OPEN THIS DOOR.

Beneath, in smaller letters:

SERIOUSLY. THERE IS NOTHING BEHIND IT.

By noon, seventeen people had tried the knob. It was locked.

By evening, someone had brought a crowbar.

That was how the people of Little Aubrey learned that the door could not be burned, broken, unhinged, tunneled beneath, climbed over, or bribed. The mayor stationed two constables in the alley, ostensibly to prevent vandalism. Both were discovered at midnight with their ears pressed to the wood.

“Did you hear anything?” everyone asked.

The constables exchanged a troubled glance.

“Knocking,” one admitted.

“From which side?”

Neither would say.

Within a week, the alley became a pilgrimage site. Scholars measured the door. Priests blessed it. Children dared one another to lick the knob. Mrs. Bell sold blue-iced biscuits shaped like keys.

Only Mara Vale seemed immune.

“That,” she said, carrying flour through the crowd, “is a curiosity trap.”

Mara repaired watches in a narrow shop across the street. She was practical, suspicious, and thirty-seven years old—an age by which she believed every mystery had revealed itself as either machinery, money, or loneliness.

The townspeople laughed.

“A trap for what?” asked the mayor.

“For you,” Mara said.

Then the door began whispering names.

Not loudly. Never while crowds watched. It waited until one person stood near it alone, then breathed a name through the keyhole.

The butcher heard the name of his first love. Mrs. Bell heard her dead son’s. The mayor claimed he had heard nothing, although he returned each dawn with fresh oil for the hinges.

People stopped laughing at Mara.

They began asking her to open the door.

“You’re clever,” they said. “You won’t be fooled.”

“Exactly why I won’t open it.”

Then, one rainy evening, as Mara hurried past with her umbrella low, the door whispered:

“Elias Vale.”

She stopped.

Rain rattled in the gutters. Somewhere, thunder worried at the hills.

Elias had been her younger brother. Twenty years earlier, when he was nine and she was seventeen, he had vanished beside the river. His shoes were found in the reeds, neatly placed together. No body ever surfaced.

Mara stepped toward the door.

“That isn’t his voice,” she said.

“No,” replied the keyhole. “But I know where he went.”

Mara struck the door with her umbrella and walked away.

The next morning, a brass key lay on her workbench.

It was warm.

She threw it into the river.

The following morning, it lay inside the half-repaired pocket watch of Magistrate Hobb.

She buried it in the churchyard. She melted it in a crucible. She paid a traveling merchant to carry it north. Each morning it returned, hidden somewhere cleverer: beneath her tongue, inside an egg, frozen in the center of her washbasin.

On the seventh day, it was clutched in the hand of a tiny wooden figure she had carved as a child. A figure of Elias.

Mara took the key to the alley.

The blue door waited.

“You’re not him,” she said.

The card fluttered though there was no wind.

DO NOT OPEN THIS DOOR.

SERIOUSLY.

Mara inserted the key.

The lock gave a delighted click.

Beyond the door lay no monster, treasure, heaven, or hell. There was only another alley, moonlit and empty. At its far end stood a red door.

Mara glanced back. Little Aubrey had vanished. Behind her stretched a third alley, ending in a green door.

“Very sophisticated,” she called. “A maze.”

“Not a maze,” said someone above.

A man sat on the roof, dangling long legs over the gutter. He wore a coat sewn with playing cards, and his smile had too many corners.

Mara recognized Lyr, god of thieves, riddles, foxes, crossroads, and regrettable decisions.

“A maze hides the exit,” Lyr said. “Every door here is an exit.”

“Then send me home.”

“Blue door.”

She opened it. Beyond lay a ballroom where masked dancers waltzed beneath the sea.

She slammed it shut.

Lyr laughed. “I didn’t say which blue door.”

Doors appeared along every wall: blue, red, green, black, gold. Thousands climbed into the sky, hung flat upon the ground, floated unsupported in the air. From behind them came weeping, music, barking dogs, battlefield horns, lullabies.

And one small boy’s laughter.

Mara went still.

A white door stood behind her.

“Elias?”

The laughter came again.

She reached for the knob, then stopped.

“That’s the trap,” she said.

Lyr’s smile thinned.

“Obviously.”

“No. The first door wasn’t the trap. This is. Infinite choices, each promising an answer. I could search forever.”

“You could.”

“And every door opens somewhere?”

“Certainly.”

“Then one opens where Elias went.”

“Certainly.”

Mara looked at the white door until her eyes burned.

Then she sat down.

Lyr frowned. “What are you doing?”

“Waiting.”

“For what?”

“You.”

Gods, Mara knew, shared one weakness with children and cats: they could not endure being ignored.

Lyr began whistling. Mara examined her fingernails. He conjured a feast. She declined. He made the white door tremble as Elias called her name.

She wept, but did not move.

At last Lyr dropped from the roof.

“Most people open doors.”

“So I gathered.”

“This is boring.”

“So leave.”

“I can’t. You’re in my trap.”

“And you’re here with me.”

For the first time, the trickster god looked alarmed.

Mara smiled.

“Tell me where my brother went, and I’ll open one.”

Lyr spat, cursed, transformed her hair into snakes, and offered her immortality. Mara waited.

Finally, he whispered, “He fell in the river. A fisherman found him downstream. He had forgotten his name. He grew up by the coast. He repairs boats. He is happy.”

“Alive?”

“Old, fat, seven children.”

Mara closed her eyes.

“Which door?”

Lyr pointed sullenly to a small brown door beneath her chair.

She opened it onto a sunlit harbor. An old man looked up from a broken mast, and the tool fell from his hand.

Behind Mara, Lyr lunged for freedom.

She shut the door in his face.

Years later, another mysterious door appeared in another town. Its card read:

PLEASE OPEN. A GOD IS TRAPPED INSIDE.

No one believed it.

Not for the first week, anyway.
