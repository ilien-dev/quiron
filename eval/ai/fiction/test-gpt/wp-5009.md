Lord Malovar had rehearsed the speech for seventeen years.

He began as thunder rolled over the cathedral ruins, precisely as planned.

“You see, Elian, when your father cast me into the Abyss—”

A crossbow snapped.

Elian, Last Prince of Aurum, jerked backward. The legendary sword slipped from his fingers. He touched the bolt in his throat, made a wet, questioning sound, and collapsed at Malovar’s feet.

For several seconds, even the thunder seemed embarrassed.

Malovar stared at the body. Then at the henchman holding the crossbow.

“What,” he said softly, “did you do?”

The henchman lowered the weapon. He wore the same iron mask and black gambeson as the other forty guards. He had no name anyone knew, not even in the payroll ledger.

“He was armed, my lord.”

“I was speaking.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“He was supposed to learn that I am his uncle.”

The henchman glanced at Elian. “He can’t hear you now.”

“I am aware of that.”

At the edge of the ruined cathedral, Elian’s companions remained frozen: the flame-haired princess, the elderly wizard, the reformed assassin, and the orphan boy whose mysterious birthmark had not yet become relevant.

Malovar pinched the bridge of his nose.

“Do you understand,” he asked, “how much work went into this moment? The forged letters? The murdered mentors? The symbolic ravens?”

“No, my lord.”

“Seventeen years.”

The henchman nodded sympathetically. “Long time.”

The princess finally screamed and charged.

The henchman shot her too.

She fell beside Elian.

The wizard raised his staff. “Foul servant! You know not what powers—”

A second henchman, inspired by the first, stabbed him from behind.

That was when the story began to come apart.

The cathedral floor trembled. Somewhere beneath the earth, the Dragon of Ending stirred, confused. It had been promised a climactic duel at dawn. The legendary sword flickered, searching for a living hand of royal blood. Finding none, it turned into an ordinary sword.

The reformed assassin looked at Malovar.

“Do I still switch sides?”

Malovar gazed at the accumulating corpses. “To whom?”

“Oh.” She considered this, then quietly returned to his side.

The orphan boy raised his hand. “I might be the prince’s secret brother.”

Every crossbow in the cathedral aimed at him.

“I might not be,” he added.

Malovar descended the altar steps, cloak dragging through rainwater. He stopped before the nameless henchman.

“Heroes do not simply die during revelations.”

“Everyone dies if you shoot them correctly, my lord.”

“That is not the point.”

“Was he protected by magic?”

“By destiny!”

The henchman looked around. “Destiny should have worn armor.”

Behind them, Elian coughed.

Everyone turned.

With immense effort, the prince rolled onto one elbow. Golden light spilled from his wound. The heavens opened. A choir of unseen ancestors began a single triumphant note.

The henchman reloaded.

Malovar slapped the crossbow down.

“No! Absolutely not. The prophecy is restoring him.”

“Then I should hurry.”

“You will stand there and let destiny proceed.”

Elian rose, trembling, haloed in sacred fire. His hand stretched toward the fallen sword.

The henchman kicked it through a hole in the floor.

The sacred fire went out.

Elian stared after it.

“Oh,” he whispered.

The henchman drew a knife.

Afterward, the Dragon of Ending went back to sleep. The orphan removed his birthmark with spit. The assassin requested steady employment. Malovar conquered the kingdom by Thursday.

Yet songs were not written about him.

They were written about the henchman.

Minstrels called him Plotbreaker, Fate’s Bane, the Man Without a Name. They gave him a tragic childhood, a forbidden love, and a secret claim to the throne.

When Malovar heard the first ballad, he summoned the henchman to the highest tower.

“You,” he said, as lightning framed them both, “have stolen my story.”

The henchman drew his crossbow.

Malovar sighed.

“Could you at least let me finish the monologue?”

“No, my lord.”
