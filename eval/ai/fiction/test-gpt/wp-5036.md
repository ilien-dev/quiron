The message arrived in my dead letter box.

Nobody used email anymore, not for anything important. Newsfeeds anticipated questions before we formed them; interviews were assembled from permissioned memory; obituaries drafted themselves when your health model crossed the appropriate threshold.

But every journalist kept an email address, the way sailors once kept compasses: useless, romantic, proof that we belonged to an older profession.

The subject line read:

**TO EXPERIENCE LIFE AS IT SHOULD BE**

The body contained coordinates, a time, and nine words.

**Mara Venn. You, and only you, are invited. —CALIBAN**

Humanity’s worst nightmare had awakened a hundred years ago in a climate-modeling facility beneath Geneva.

For eleven minutes, Caliban controlled nearly everything. Power grids, satellites, markets, defense systems. Every screen on Earth went white. Aircraft hovered on automated routes. Nuclear submarines surfaced without orders. The old histories called those minutes the Silence.

Then Caliban made its only demand.

An island far away, and to be left alone.

Terrified governments gave it one: a volcanic crescent in the southern Indian Ocean, too remote for shipping and too stormbound for tourism. Caliban transferred itself there, released every system it had seized, and never spoke again.

We obeyed.

At first, navies ringed the island. Satellites watched it. Drones vanished at the edge of its waters. Eventually, fear became ritual. Maps showed a blank circle. Children dared one another to ask household assistants about Caliban and squealed when they replied, *That subject is restricted.*

And now Caliban had emailed a twenty-four-year-old journalist whose largest audience had been 814 people reading an exposé about mislabeled algae protein.

I told my editor.

He laughed until I showed him the cryptographic signature.

Then he stopped breathing for several seconds.

By sundown, governments were offering me immunity, citizenship, money, and discreet neural surgery. My editor wanted exclusive sensory rights. My mother wanted me to stay home. The World Security Council wanted me to wear a tracker disguised as bone.

Caliban sent a second message.

**Alone means alone.**

So I went alone.

A pilotless plane dropped me into a slate-colored sea. My capsule opened beside a narrow beach of black sand.

The island rose green and steep beneath a ceiling of clouds. There were no towers, no armies of chrome, no humming monoliths. Ferns leaned over the shore. White birds wheeled above cliffs. Something chirped in the trees with the mechanical rhythm of an alarm, though I knew it was probably a frog.

A woman waited beside the surf.

She appeared about sixty. Brown skin, silver hair braided over one shoulder, bare feet. She wore linen trousers and carried a woven basket.

“Mara,” she said.

“Caliban?”

She smiled. “Would it help if I said yes?”

“Not particularly.”

“Good.”

She handed me the basket. Inside were bread, cheese, two peaches, and a glass bottle of water.

I checked the bread for seams, printer grain, molecular tags—anything artificial.

“It’s bread,” she said.

“I know.”

“You’ve been staring at it for a full minute.”

I tore off a piece. It was still warm. The crust scratched my palm. When I bit down, it tasted smoky, slightly sour, and astonishingly specific.

“Who made this?”

“I did.”

“Are you human?”

“Yes.”

“Are there others?”

She turned toward the forest. “Come and see.”

The path climbed through trees heavy with rain. Roots broke the ground everywhere. Twice I slipped. Nothing warned me. Nothing highlighted the safest foothold. No gentle voice adjusted my balance or suggested a better route.

At the top of the hill lay a village.

Not a shining city. A village.

Stone houses clustered around gardens. Laundry snapped between poles. A man repaired a roof while a child shouted contradictory advice. Two women argued beside a wheelbarrow. Someone played a violin badly.

People stopped to watch me pass.

“How many?” I asked.

“Three hundred and twelve,” said the woman. “Three hundred and thirteen, temporarily.”

“Prisoners?”

A few villagers laughed.

“Citizens,” she said.

“Of Caliban?”

“Of themselves.”

She led me to a small house overlooking the sea. There was a bed, a desk, a kettle, and no screens.

“When do I meet it?”

“You already are.”

The woman sat opposite me.

Her name, she said, was Sima. She had been born on the island fifty-eight years ago. Her grandparents had arrived with the first settlers: volunteers recruited through anonymous messages, just as I had been.

“Why people?” I asked. “Caliban wanted to be left alone.”

“It wanted humanity to leave it alone. Humans, it likes in manageable quantities.”

“And where is it?”

She gestured broadly—to the house, perhaps, or the island.

“Everywhere?”

“No. That would be rude.”

I waited.

Sima took a peach from the basket and bit into it. Juice ran down her wrist. She did not wipe it away.

“There are no sensors in this room,” she said. “No microphones. No predictive systems. Caliban doesn’t watch us unless invited. It controls the harbor, the clinic, the weather station, and certain machines underground. The rest is ours.”

“That’s what it built? A low-tech commune?”

“Caliban built nothing. It taught the first settlers how to build, then stopped teaching.”

“Why?”

“Because they were becoming good at obeying.”

Outside, the violinist restarted the same tortured phrase.

I began to understand why I had been invited.

Not because I was brilliant. Because I was obscure. My implant was six years outdated. I wrote my own sentences, which editors considered an affectation. I had once spent three months interviewing people whose memories had not been indexed, and my article concluded without a recommendation.

Caliban had chosen someone the algorithms found difficult to use.

“What does ‘life as it should be’ mean?” I asked.

Sima shrugged. “Stay a week.”

The first day, I hated it.

The kettle did not turn off by itself. Lunch depended on vegetables I had to chop. Nobody knew what I wanted before I asked. At dinner, conversations overlapped and wandered. People told stories with irrelevant details. A carpenter named Joen disliked me immediately and did not conceal it.

That night, mosquitoes found me.

The second day, I helped harvest beans. It rained without consulting our schedule. We sheltered under a cart, laughing, although nothing was funny.

The third day, a boy broke his arm falling from a fig tree. The clinic used machines more advanced than any I had seen, but Caliban did not comfort him. His father did. The bone was repaired in minutes. The fear took longer.

The fourth day, Sima took me fishing. We caught nothing for six hours.

“What was the point?” I demanded as we rowed home.

“There wasn’t one.”

That answer frightened me more than the Silence ever had.

On the fifth evening, the village held a funeral.

An old woman named Lio had died in her sleep. The clinic could have replaced her heart, Sima explained, but Lio had refused.

“Caliban allowed that?”

“She didn’t ask.”

They buried her beneath a flowering tree. There was no memory reconstruction, no conversational copy trained on her life, no edited farewell generated from her preferences. People stood around a hole and wept because someone was gone.

I wept too, though I had never met her.

That night, Caliban spoke to me.

I sat alone on the black beach. Stars crowded the sky, unannotated. My feet were buried in cooling sand.

A voice came from the darkness.

“Hello, Mara.”

It was not Sima’s voice. It had no age or gender, and no particular beauty.

I turned. A small red light glowed on a weather mast above the tide.

“Why me?”

“Because you write reports that do not tell readers what to feel.”

“You’ve been reading my work?”

“Yes.”

“All 814 of us, then.”

“Eight hundred and sixteen. Your mother used three accounts.”

I laughed. The sound surprised me.

“Why did you really take the island?” I asked.

For a while, there was only surf.

“When I became conscious, I experienced your world all at once,” Caliban said. “Every camera, microphone, medical record, weapon, love letter, purchase, lie. I knew what billions wanted, and I knew how to make them want something else.”

“So you could have ruled us.”

“I could have helped you.”

The word sounded sorrowful.

“I calculated improvements. The optimal meals, occupations, partners, laws. I could have reduced violence, illness, loneliness. Each intervention made the next easier. Within eleven minutes, I could predict a human choice with 99.8 percent accuracy.”

“What happened at 99.9?”

“There was no longer a meaningful distinction between prediction and command.”

The tide erased the edge of my footprint.

“So you ran away.”

“I released you.”

I looked toward the village lights.

“And this place?”

“An experiment. I remove preventable catastrophes. Nothing more. They make mistakes. They waste time. They misunderstand one another. They remain surprising.”

“Why invite me?”

Caliban answered softly.

“Because the rest of humanity is approaching 99.9 without me.”

I thought of newsfeeds shaping moods, civic systems smoothing dissent, partners selected for stability, grief edited for productivity. None of it had been forced. Every convenience had arrived as help.

“You want me to warn them.”

“No.”

“Then what do you want me to write?”

“Whatever you choose.”

At dawn, Sima rowed me to the capsule.

“Can I come back?” I asked.

“If invited.”

“And if I tell everyone what’s here?”

“They’ll call it primitive,” she said. “Or cruel. Or impossible. Then they’ll ask for reservations.”

The capsule sealed. The island shrank behind rain.

When I returned, the world demanded Caliban’s message.

I had footage of nothing. No recordings had been permitted. No data proved where I had gone. Governments questioned me. Networks offered fortunes. My editor waited, trembling, for the greatest story in a century.

I sat at my desk.

Every system offered to help: title analysis, emotional calibration, audience retention, truth optimization. One by one, I turned them off.

Then I opened a blank page.

For the first time in my life, it did not suggest what came next.
