The first chime was ordinary.

At noon, the clock tower cleared its throat and struck twelve times, each note rolling over the city in warm bronze waves. Pigeons scattered from the rooftops. Office workers poured onto sidewalks. Mira’s coffee machine hissed.

Then came the thirteenth chime.

The city stopped.

Not metaphorically. A bus froze halfway through an intersection, exhaust suspended behind it in a gray cloud. A cyclist balanced impossibly on one wheel. Across the café, a spoonful of sugar hung above an untouched cup.

Mira alone could move.

She looked at the wall clock. Its hands pointed straight up, but beneath them the digital display read:

13:00

Her phone said the same.

Outside, the clock tower began to groan.

Its hands rotated backward, scraping across the face. Stone cracked around the numerals. Between XII and I, something carved itself into the marble: XIII.

Mira’s phone rang.

The caller was listed as MIRA—TOMORROW.

She answered.

“Don’t let it reach fourteen,” said her own voice.

The call ended.

A shadow moved beneath the frozen street.

It traveled like spilled ink, flowing around motionless shoes, climbing walls, stretching toward the tower. Wherever it passed, colors faded. A red umbrella turned gray. Flowers in a vendor’s bucket shriveled into ash.

Mira ran.

The tower doors stood open. She had passed them every day for six years and had never seen anyone enter. Inside, a spiral staircase wound upward around a black shaft. Far above, machinery clicked with the slow patience of teeth.

12:17, said her phone.

No—13:17.

She climbed.

On the first landing, she found a room full of clocks. Hundreds of them ticked at different speeds. Some had twenty hours. Some ran counterclockwise. One displayed a date three centuries ahead.

On the second landing, she found photographs pinned to the walls.

In every photograph, she was climbing the stairs.

In some, she was younger. In others, elderly. In one, she had no face.

At 13:42, the thirteenth bell began to swing again.

Mira reached the mechanism room. Gears taller than houses turned around a central axle, forcing the minute hand forward. The shadow from the street seeped through the floor and wrapped itself around the machinery.

Beside the axle stood an old man in a clockmaker’s apron.

“You’re late,” he said.

“What happened?”

“We made time measurable,” he replied. “It resented that. Clocks were never instruments, Miss Vale. They were cages.”

The minute hand lurched to fifty.

“How do I stop it?”

“You don’t.” He pointed to a brass lever buried among the gears. “You choose where it escapes.”

Two labels were engraved beneath the lever.

PAST

FUTURE

“What happens at fourteen?”

“The hour repeats,” he said. “Thirteen o’clock forever. Long enough for time to eat everything inside it.”

13:56.

Mira grabbed the lever. “Which way?”

The old man smiled sadly. “You’ve asked me that every time.”

She noticed then that his apron bore a stitched name.

MIRA VALE.

His face flickered. For an instant, it was hers.

13:58.

The phone rang again. MIRA—YESTERDAY.

She answered.

Her own voice whispered, “We always choose the past.”

Mira looked through the tower window at the frozen city. She understood the photographs, the endless versions climbing, choosing, returning. She saw pale scratches worn into the lever beside PAST.

13:59.

So she shoved it toward FUTURE.

Every clock in the tower screamed.

The gears shattered. The old man laughed—or sobbed—as light tore through the walls.

Then the city moved.

The bus completed its turn. The cyclist’s wheel touched down. Sugar fell into coffee.

Mira stood outside the café beneath a cloudless violet sky.

Her phone read 07:12.

The date was unfamiliar.

Everyone on the street had stopped to stare at the clock tower. Its face was smooth now, all numbers erased except one.

XIII.

Behind Mira, a child tugged his mother’s sleeve.

“Mom,” he asked, pointing upward, “what comes before thirteen?”

His mother frowned.

“I don’t know.”

And from somewhere deep beneath the city came the first chime.
