Mara had buried everyone twice before the angel found her.

The first time, she buried her husband beneath the pear tree because the churchyard was full of soldiers. She wrapped him in the blue blanket they had shared for thirty winters and dug until the shovel split her palms.

The second time, eighty years later, she buried the pear tree.

It had grown enormous, fed by rain and Thomas and time. When lightning opened it from crown to root, Mara cut the blackened wood into pieces and carried them to the stove. The fire smelled faintly sweet.

“Forgive me,” she whispered as the last branch burned.

After that, she stopped counting burials.

Children. Grandchildren. Their children. Friends who began as infants in her arms and ended as bones beneath stones. Villages changed names. Roads straightened. Forests retreated from iron rails. Every few decades, suspicion drove her elsewhere, and she became her own daughter, then her own niece, then a widow from some province no one had visited.

She learned to make grief small enough to carry.

Then, one autumn evening, an angel knocked on her cottage door.

Mara knew him at once. Not because he had wings—he did not—but because the rain refused to touch him. Drops curved around his shoulders and rejoined beneath his boots.

“I have no food,” Mara said.

“I have not come to eat.”

“No money either.”

“I have come to give.”

“Then you have the wrong house.”

The angel smiled with the exhausted patience of someone accustomed to being welcomed. He was beautiful in a way that irritated her: golden hair, clear skin, eyes like polished coins.

“May I enter?”

“No.”

He entered.

Mara sat beside the stove, where a pot of turnips simmered. She did not offer him a chair. He took one anyway.

“Three hundred and twelve years ago,” he said, “you pulled a dying stranger from a river.”

Mara stirred the pot. “Did I?”

“You remember.”

She did. A spring flood. A young man caught between branches, his robes rich, his face blue. She had waded in while others shouted that the current would take her. She had dragged him onto the bank and beaten water from his lungs.

Later, feverish, he had held her wrist and whispered words in a language she did not understand.

“You saved one of my kind,” said the angel. “In gratitude, he blessed you.”

“He cursed me.”

“He granted immortality.”

“I noticed.”

The angel’s smile faltered.

Mara ladled turnips into a wooden bowl. “Was he drunk?”

“He was injured.”

“Was he stupid?”

“He was young.”

“That is the same answer.”

The angel folded his hands. “A blessing of that magnitude requires review after three centuries. I am here to ask how you have used the gift.”

Mara ate a turnip. It needed salt.

“I have spent it.”

“On what?”

“Living.”

“Yes, but to what purpose?”

Mara looked at him then.

Behind his flawless face she saw an official, a clerk of heaven expecting an account: kingdoms advised, plagues cured, libraries written, wars prevented. He wanted immortality to have made her grand.

“I planted beans,” she said.

The angel blinked.

“I delivered babies. Mended nets. Sat with the dying when their families were afraid. Once, I taught a dog to close a door.”

“Surely you have done more.”

“I learned six languages and forgot four. I warned a duke not to invade the north. He invaded anyway. I showed a physician how to wash his hands. He claimed the idea as his own, then died when a horse kicked him.”

The angel leaned forward. “With endless life, you could accumulate wisdom beyond any mortal. You could guide nations.”

“Nations do not enjoy being guided by old women.”

“You could reveal yourself.”

“They would burn me.”

“They cannot kill you.”

“Burning still hurts.”

For the first time, the angel looked uncertain.

Rain whispered against the roof. Mara finished her turnips and placed the bowl aside.

“If this is a review,” she said, “review it. Then take it back.”

His eyes widened. “Take back immortality?”

“Yes.”

“It is the highest gift heaven can bestow.”

“Then heaven has never received it.”

The cottage became very quiet.

The angel rose and paced to the window. Outside, dead leaves clung to the mud. “Do you know how many beg for what you possess? Kings on their deathbeds. Mothers with sick children. Scholars with unfinished work.”

“Give it to them.”

“It cannot be transferred.”

“Then remove it.”

“I do not know if that is permitted.”

Mara laughed. Her voice sounded rusty. “Three hundred years, and the first honest thing one of you has said to me.”

The angel turned sharply. “One of us?”

“The man in the river visited once. Fifty years after the blessing. He expected thanks.”

“What happened?”

“I broke his nose.”

Despite himself, the angel smiled.

Mara stood. Her knees did not ache; they had not ached since the river. She hated them for it.

She opened a small chest beside her bed and removed a child’s red mitten, a tarnished wedding ring, a soldier’s brass button, a ribbon faded nearly white. Beneath them lay hundreds of scraps: locks of hair, letters, tiny portraits, names written so they would not vanish.

“This is immortality,” she said. “Not wisdom. Not power. Inventory.”

The angel touched the red mitten.

“My youngest,” Mara said. “Elsie. She lived seven years.”

“I am sorry.”

“No, you aren’t. Not yet.”

He withdrew his hand.

Mara shut the chest. “When you have watched mountains change shape, perhaps you may tell me what eternity means.”

The angel stood silent so long that the fire collapsed into embers.

At last he said, “There may be a way.”

Hope was painful. Mara had abandoned it a century ago, and its return was like blood rushing into a frozen hand.

“What way?”

“A life freely given cannot be reclaimed. But a gift may be completed.”

“Completed?”

“Your immortality preserved your life because you risked it for another. The blessing was shaped by that act. If you freely risk your life again—knowing death is certain, seeking no reward—the circle may close.”

“May?”

“I am young,” he admitted.

Mara studied his perfect, embarrassed face.

Then the church bell began ringing.

Not the hour. Alarm.

She opened the door. Beyond the fields, orange light pulsed against the clouds. The village mill was burning.

Mara ran.

People formed a line from the well, passing buckets toward the flames. The miller stumbled in the road, screaming that his daughter was inside. The roof groaned. Sparks spun upward like stars returning to heaven.

Mara did not pause.

Heat struck her at the doorway. Inside, smoke erased the world. She wrapped her sleeve across her mouth and crawled, calling the child’s name.

A faint cry answered from above.

The stairs were burning.

Mara climbed them.

Each step opened beneath her feet. Fire took her dress, her hair, her skin. Pain became the whole universe, but she climbed until she found the girl curled beneath a table. Mara seized her, carried her to the window, and threw her into the waiting arms below.

The roof fell.

In the final instant, Mara felt the blessing break.

Death came gently, smelling of rain and pearwood smoke.

Beyond it stood Thomas, holding a blue blanket.

“You took your time,” he said.

Mara smiled and stepped into his arms, while somewhere far behind her, an angel knelt in the ashes and finally learned what mortals meant when they prayed for mercy.
