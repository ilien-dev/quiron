The summons arrived three weeks after I killed Daniel Voss.

I nearly laughed. Then I read the case number.

The prosecutor described Voss’s death with clinical precision: one blow to the temple, delivered in his kitchen sometime between ten and midnight. No sign of forced entry. The accused, Marcus Bell, had argued with Voss at a bar that evening. His fingerprints were on a whiskey glass beside the body. A neighbor had seen him leave the building.

All true.

Marcus had left at ten fifteen.

I arrived at eleven.

During jury selection, the judge asked whether any of us knew the victim or defendant. I said no. She asked whether anything might prevent us from judging the case fairly.

I thought of Voss opening his apartment door, his smile collapsing when he saw me.

I said no again.

They seated me as Juror Eight.

The trial lasted four days. Marcus looked smaller each morning, folded inside a suit that belonged to someone broader. His public defender argued that the investigation had stopped the moment police found a convenient suspect.

She was right.

The prosecutor displayed photographs of Voss’s kitchen. There was the marble counter. The copper pan hanging crookedly. The dark stain where Voss had fallen.

And there, circled in red, was the murder weapon: a bronze horse from his desk.

I had wiped it twice.

Apparently, twice had been enough.

No fingerprints. No blood on Marcus’s clothes. No witness placing him there after ten fifteen. Only anger, opportunity, and a frightened neighbor who admitted, under questioning, that she wasn’t wearing her glasses.

In the jury room, eleven people voted guilty.

I voted not guilty.

“A man is dead,” Juror Four said.

“That doesn’t make Bell the killer.”

“He threatened him.”

“People threaten people every day.”

“You seem awfully sure,” Juror Nine said.

I felt twelve pairs of eyes, including the bailiff’s through the glass.

“I’m sure the state didn’t prove it.”

For six hours, I dismantled their certainty piece by piece. The timeline was imprecise. The witness unreliable. The forensic evidence absent. I spoke calmly because panic sounds like guilt.

By evening, the vote was ten to two.

The next morning, eight to four.

On the third day, only Juror Four remained.

He stared at Marcus through the narrow window in the door. “If we let him walk and he did it, that’s on us.”

“No,” I said. “If we imprison him without proof, that’s on us.”

The words tasted strange in my mouth.

At four seventeen, we returned a verdict of not guilty.

Marcus collapsed against his attorney. His mother screamed. The judge thanked us for our service.

Outside the courthouse, rain glossed the steps. I stood beneath the stone archway, breathing air that felt cleaner than I deserved.

“Juror Eight.”

Marcus waited behind me, alone.

“You saved my life,” he said.

I told him the evidence had saved him.

He shook his head. “No. You did.”

For one absurd second, I imagined confession. Not from remorse exactly, but from the unbearable weight of his gratitude.

Then he stepped closer.

“Daniel kept records,” he whispered.

My skin went cold.

“He recorded every meeting. Every threat. Everyone he was blackmailing.” Marcus watched my face carefully. “Police found the recorder yesterday. My lawyer got a copy.”

Rain hammered the courthouse roof.

On the night I killed him, Voss had said no one would believe me. He had smiled when he said it.

Marcus smiled now.

“The last voice on it is yours.”

Behind us, the courthouse doors opened.

Two detectives emerged.

Marcus tucked his hands into his pockets. “I wondered why you fought so hard.”

The detectives started down the steps.

I had spent three days teaching twelve strangers that suspicion was not proof.

Now, for the first time, I understood how little comfort that was.
