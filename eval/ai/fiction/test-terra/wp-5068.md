Sir Alden smelled smoke three miles before he saw the tower.

This encouraged him. Smoke meant fire, and fire meant the king had told the truth about the dragon. After six days in the saddle, Alden was relieved to find that at least one part of the quest had been accurately described.

The tower rose above a pine forest, its pale stones blackened around the upper windows. At its foot stood a painted wooden sign.

**NO SWORDS PAST THIS POINT.  
SHOW STARTS AT EIGHT.**

Alden looked at the sun. It was nearly eight.

He tied his horse to a tree, left his sword beside her, and approached the tower door. From within came a heavy, rhythmic thumping. He knocked.

The thumping stopped.

A hatch slid open, revealing one brown eye beneath an eyebrow with a silver ring through it.

“You on the list?” a voice asked.

“I am Sir Alden of Westmere. I have come to rescue Princess Elowen.”

The eye disappeared. Alden heard a sigh, followed by the scrape of bolts. The door opened.

The man behind it wore a red velvet coat, a gold chain, and a pair of boots so white that Alden suspected sorcery. He was broad-shouldered and bald, with a neatly trimmed beard and a tattoo of a flame curling up his neck.

“Name’s Dragon,” he said. “Come in before the mosquitoes eat you.”

Alden remained on the threshold. “Are you the dragon?”

“Depends who’s asking.”

“Sir Alden of Westmere.”

“Then yes.”

Dragon led him into a circular room lit by candles and crowded with benches. A dozen villagers sat facing a small wooden stage. On it stood two musicians, one holding a lute, the other a drum. A girl no older than sixteen was arranging sheets of parchment on a lectern.

Alden stared at her. He knew the princess from the coins in his purse, though the engraver had given her a considerably sterner expression.

“Elowen?” he said.

She looked up. “Oh. Father sent another one.”

Dragon took Alden’s arm and steered him toward an empty bench. “Sit down. You can rescue her at the interval.”

Alden sat.

The first performer was a farmer who sang a ballad about an unusually clever turnip. The second was a young woman whose poem made three people cry, including the farmer. Then Dragon stepped onto the stage.

The drummer struck a beat. Dragon began to speak in time with it, his voice rising and falling over the drum and lute. Alden had never heard anything quite like it. The words came quickly, each line catching the next, until the room was nodding along.

Dragon rapped about the king’s new tax on grain, the broken bridge that had taken a year to repair, and the palace feast held to celebrate its completion.

By the end, even Alden was nodding. He stopped when Dragon mentioned the king by name.

At the interval, Elowen brought him a cup of cider.

“Did my father tell you I was imprisoned?” she asked.

“He said a dragon had locked you in a tower.”

“He did lock me in,” she said. “I kept leaving the door open, and the mosquitoes were getting in.”

Dragon passed them carrying a tray of meat pies. “She’s terrible about doors.”

Elowen lowered her voice. “Father sent me here after I spoke against the grain tax at court. He said I needed quiet and reflection. Dragon was supposed to keep me out of trouble.”

“I was hired as a tutor,” Dragon called over his shoulder. “Nobody mentioned trouble.”

“He taught me to write verses,” Elowen said. “Then we invited the villagers to hear them. The smoke is from the kitchen chimney.”

“And the blackened windows?”

“Last week’s pies,” Dragon said.

Alden considered this. “Your father promised me a hundred gold pieces to bring you home.”

Elowen’s face fell. “Do you need the money?”

“My mother’s roof fell in during the spring rains.”

“Oh.” She glanced toward the stage. “Would you wait until I’ve performed?”

It was a small request. Alden agreed.

Elowen’s hands shook as she stepped before the audience. She unfolded her parchment, then folded it again and set it aside.

Her verse began haltingly. She spoke of the tax collectors coming after a poor harvest, and of a boy she had met whose family had sold their plough to pay them. As she found the rhythm, her voice grew steadier. By the final line, the room was silent.

Then someone struck the side of a bench. Others joined in, until the tower rang with applause.

Alden looked around at the villagers. The boy might have been among them. He wondered how many had roofs in need of repair.

The next morning, he returned to the palace alone.

“The princess is safe,” he told the king. “But I could not bring her home.”

The king’s face darkened. “The dragon stopped you?”

“No, Your Majesty. The door was locked.”

“You’re a knight! Break it down!”

“I tried. I also tried the window, but it was very high.”

The king dismissed him without a coin.

Three days later, a cart arrived at Alden’s cottage carrying roof tiles, timber, and Dragon. The villagers had sent the materials; Dragon had offered to help.

As they worked, Alden told him about the king’s expression when he mentioned the window.

Dragon laughed. “That’s a good line. Mind if I use it?”

“Go ahead,” Alden said. “Just don’t use my name.”

By the following week, everyone in the kingdom knew it.
