The first time I saw the man accused of my husband’s murder, he was standing at a bus stop with a bag of oranges.

I was driving home from the police station, where I had spent three hours describing the intruder who had pushed past me in the dark. The man at the stop wore a blue jacket like the one I’d described. He had a cut over his eyebrow. One of the oranges had escaped his bag and was rolling toward the curb.

He ran after it, caught it with his shoe, and looked up.

For a moment I thought he recognized me. Then he smiled, embarrassed, and held up the orange.

Three weeks later, his photograph appeared on the evening news.

His name was Daniel Ruiz. He had worked maintenance at the assisted-living home where my husband, Peter, volunteered. Police had found Peter’s watch in Daniel’s apartment, along with a blue jacket and a pair of work boots consistent with prints outside our house.

I knew about the watch. I had slipped it into Daniel’s open toolbox the afternoon after Peter died, when Daniel came to fix the lock Peter had broken on my mother’s door. I had seen an opportunity and taken it.

The boots surprised me. So did the jacket. By the time I learned Daniel had a record for burglary, I had begun to wonder how much of the case had made itself.

My summons arrived four months later.

I called the court and explained who I was. The clerk checked the case number and told me the summons was for a civil trial, not Daniel Ruiz’s. I slept for the first time in days.

Then, on Monday morning, the civil case settled. The judge sent our panel downstairs to join the pool for a criminal trial.

I recognized Daniel before I saw the prosecutors.

When the judge asked whether anyone knew the defendant, my hand stayed down. When she asked whether anyone had a connection to the victim, I raised it.

“I’m his widow.”

The courtroom went still.

At the bench, the lawyers spoke in low voices. The prosecutor wanted me dismissed. Daniel’s attorney asked for a recess. Through the glass doors, I watched Daniel sit with his hands folded, staring at the empty witness stand.

The judge called me back.

“Mrs. Bell, you should have been identified before you entered this courtroom. I apologize. You’re excused from jury service.”

I stood, but Daniel’s attorney raised a hand.

“Your Honor, may I ask Mrs. Bell one question?”

The prosecutor objected. The judge told him to make it quick.

“Mrs. Bell,” the attorney said, “do you remember Mr. Ruiz visiting your mother’s house the day after your husband died?”

My mouth dried.

“He repaired a lock,” I said.

“And did you see his toolbox?”

“Yes.”

“Was it open?”

“I don’t remember.”

He nodded and let me go.

I reached the courthouse steps before I understood what had happened. Daniel had told his lawyer about the repair. Perhaps he had remembered me hovering near the toolbox. Perhaps he had only described his day, and the lawyer had noticed that I was there.

That evening, the detective called.

“We need to talk about your mother’s lock,” she said.

I told her I was tired. She said she could come to me.

While I waited, I opened the kitchen drawer and took out the photograph I had hidden beneath the cutlery tray. Peter had taken it of my mother’s bruised arm, meaning to show a doctor how easily she injured herself. He’d sent it to me with a message: *She fell again.*

My mother had told me how she fell. Peter had grabbed her when she tried to leave the house, then shoved her against the banister. He had broken the lock to get inside after she changed it.

The detective arrived at seven. I put the photograph on the table between us.

“I killed him,” I said. “Daniel had nothing to do with it.”

The detective looked at me for a long time, then reached for her recorder.

This time, I told her everything.
