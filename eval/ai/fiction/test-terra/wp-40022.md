The box appeared on a Tuesday, between the library’s returns slot and the sign asking patrons not to leave donations after hours.

It was made of dark wood and had no hinges, seams, or lock. On top, someone had placed a small brass plaque:

**PLEASE DO NOT THINK ABOUT WHAT IS INSIDE.**

By nine o’clock, eleven people had stopped to look at it. By ten, someone had called the police. By eleven, the police had decided it was probably art.

Mara, the assistant librarian, encountered it on her lunch break. She read the plaque, thought immediately of a mouse wearing a crown, and felt a faint tug behind her eyes.

She stepped back. The tug stopped.

“Very clever,” she told the box.

She ate her sandwich across the street, watching people approach. A man in a suit touched the plaque and then stood perfectly still for almost a minute. When he moved again, he checked his watch, looked embarrassed, and hurried away.

Mara checked the time on her phone. He’d stood there for eight minutes.

She went back inside and asked her manager for the security footage.

“You think it’s dangerous?” he asked.

“I think it’s overdue for an explanation.”

The recording showed the man approaching at 10:42. He reached for the plaque at 10:43. At 10:51, he lowered his hand and walked away. Throughout those eight minutes, his face remained quite ordinary.

At 10:52, the box vanished.

At 10:53, it returned, accompanied by a man in a yellow raincoat.

The man examined the plaque, nodded with satisfaction, and disappeared.

Mara watched that part six times. The raincoat man didn’t walk out of frame. One moment he was there, and the next the camera showed the wall behind him.

At closing, she found him sitting on the library steps, eating a plum.

“You made the box,” she said.

He looked up. “Did you bring me a complaint?”

“I brought you a question.”

“Much better.”

Mara sat two steps below him. “What happened to the man who touched it?”

“He wondered what was inside.”

“For eight minutes?”

“Eight minutes here. A little longer where he went.” The man spat a plum stone into his palm. It turned into a beetle and flew away.

Mara looked at him. “Where did he go?”

“Inside the box.”

She thought of the mouse again. The tug returned, stronger this time, though she couldn’t see the box from the steps.

The man smiled. “Careful.”

“What are you?”

“A god. A trickster. An occasional patron of this library, though I dispute the late fee.”

“What do you want?”

“I collect questions. Yours was lovely, by the way. Very crisp.”

Mara put a hand to her forehead. “You’re stealing people’s time.”

“I give it back. Most of them enjoy themselves.”

“Most?”

“Some find what they’re looking for.”

He stood and brushed plum juice from his fingers. “The trap opens when someone imagines an answer. It closes when they find the thing they imagined.”

“And if they don’t?”

“They stay until they do.”

“That’s cruel.”

“It’s a box with a warning on it.”

He tipped an imaginary hat and vanished.

Mara went around to the returns slot. She meant to carry the box into the staff room, where she could put a real warning on it. She reached down, trying very hard to think of the weather.

Her fingers touched the wood.

She was standing in a long hallway lined with doors.

A sign above the nearest one read:

**MOUSE WITH A CROWN**

“Oh,” Mara said.

She opened it. Inside was a kitchen, warm and bright, with a tiny wooden throne on the table. A mouse sat upon it, wearing a crown made from a walnut shell.

It squeaked.

Mara stepped back into the hallway. Nothing happened.

She went in again. The mouse stood, climbed down from its throne, and offered her a crumb.

“Thank you,” Mara said. She took it.

The kitchen disappeared.

She was back beside the returns slot. The streetlights were on, and her phone showed that three hours had passed.

The next morning, Mara found a second plaque on the box:

**THE MOUSE APPRECIATED YOUR MANNERS.**

She went to the hardware store and bought a plastic tub, a padlock, and a roll of chain. She lowered the tub over the box without touching it and chained the handles to the library railing. Then she printed a sign:

**DANGEROUS. DO NOT TOUCH OR THINK ABOUT THE BOX UNDER THIS TUB.**

Within an hour, a crowd had gathered.

The first person to touch the tub was a teenage boy. Mara grabbed his arm before he could lift it.

“There’s a magic box under there,” she said. “It sends you somewhere until you find whatever you imagined was inside.”

The boy considered this. “Can I imagine money?”

“Yes, but—”

He pulled free, lifted the tub, and touched the box.

He stood motionless. A few people applauded.

Mara spent the next hour keeping everyone else away. The boy finally blinked and looked down at his empty hands.

“Did you find it?” she asked.

“Yeah. A whole swimming pool full.” He grinned, then noticed the crowd. “Does somebody have a towel?”

That evening, the god returned. Mara was waiting with a chair and a notebook.

“I’ve had thirty-seven questions today,” he said happily. “You’re a natural.”

“I have another one. What happens if someone can’t find what they imagined?”

“I told you. They stay.”

“I know.” She opened the notebook. “I wrote down what I imagined this morning. Something in your box that you haven’t found yet.”

For the first time, his smile faltered.

“That’s not a thing.”

“Isn’t it? You’re a god. You must know everything that’s in there.”

He glanced at the box.

Mara watched him try not to think.

“Don’t,” she said.

But his hand was already on the lid.

He vanished.

The box remained. Mara waited until the library closed, then called a locksmith, who helped her secure the plastic tub with a better chain. She put up a new sign that said **BROKEN GLASS**.

A week later, the plaque appeared on her desk.

**THIS IS TAKING LONGER THAN EXPECTED.**

Mara filed it under Lost and Found.

Every so often, she checked the box. It never moved, and nobody touched it. She hoped the god would find what he was looking for one day. She was curious what it might be.
