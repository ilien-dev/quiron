Jimmy first mentioned it when we were eight, standing beside the storm drain where his baseball had disappeared.

“My dad knows everything,” he said.

“Then ask him where my bike went.”

My bike had been stolen three days earlier. Jimmy went home and came back with his father, a thin man in a paint-spattered T-shirt. Mr. Bell crouched beside us.

“It’s behind the old laundromat,” he said. “The blue one with the broken lock. A boy named Marcus took it, but he’s sorry.”

It was there. Marcus’s apology, when I confronted him, was less convincing, but he handed over the bell he’d kept.

After that, everyone on our street had a question for Mr. Bell. He knew who had knocked over Mrs. Alvarez’s birdbath and what was wrong with the Hendersons’ furnace. He knew where my mother’s wedding ring had rolled: beneath a floorboard, through a gap no wider than a pencil.

He never made a show of it. He’d pause as if listening, then answer in the same mild voice he used to ask for the salt.

When I was twelve, I asked him how he knew.

“I don’t know how,” he said.

I pointed out that this meant he didn’t know everything.

He smiled. “You’re right.”

But the next morning he found me outside and said, “I do know how. I just can’t explain it in words either of us would understand.”

By high school, Jimmy had stopped bringing friends home. People still came to the door: neighbors, strangers, reporters. One man drove from another state to ask where his brother was buried. Mr. Bell told him gently, and the man sat on the porch for a long time afterward.

Jimmy sat with him. He was good at that.

The summer we graduated, Jimmy’s mother got sick.

The doctors called it aggressive. They talked about treatments and odds, and Jimmy listened with his hands folded so tightly that his knuckles turned white.

After the appointment, I found him in my backyard.

“He knows,” Jimmy said.

I knew what he meant. “Did you ask?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

He picked at a splinter in the fence. “Because if he says she dies, then she dies. And if he says she lives, I’ll spend every day waiting for him to be right.”

I wanted to tell him that hope was still possible either way. But I’d seen Mr. Bell find things no one else could find, answer questions no one else could answer. So I sat beside Jimmy until the porch light came on.

His mother began treatment. Mr. Bell drove her to appointments, learned which foods she could keep down, and held her hair when she was sick. He planted tomatoes that spring because she liked watching them ripen from the kitchen window.

One evening I helped Jimmy water them. Mr. Bell came outside carrying three glasses of lemonade.

“You could tell us what to do,” Jimmy said as his father handed him a glass.

“I am telling you what I know will help,” Mr. Bell said.

“Everything?”

Mr. Bell looked toward the house. Through the window, his wife was asleep in her chair, a blanket across her knees.

“Everything useful.”

Jimmy set his glass down hard enough to spill it. “That’s not your choice.”

“No,” his father said. “It’s yours. You can ask me anything.”

Jimmy opened his mouth, then closed it.

For a while, the only sound was water running into the soil. Then Mr. Bell said, “The tomatoes need another minute.”

His wife died in October. She was home, with Jimmy holding one hand and Mr. Bell holding the other.

At the funeral, people told Jimmy how brave his father had been. Jimmy accepted their condolences, though afterward he told me he didn’t want to hear the word *brave* again.

That night, he and I sat on his front steps. Mr. Bell came out with his coat on.

“I’m going to walk to the store,” he said. “Do you boys want anything?”

Jimmy shook his head. His father started down the path, then stopped at the gate.

“Dad?”

Mr. Bell turned.

“Did you know, when you married her?”

“Yes.”

Jimmy looked down at his hands. “Did you know she’d be happy?”

Mr. Bell came back and sat beside him.

“Yes,” he said. “Very.”
