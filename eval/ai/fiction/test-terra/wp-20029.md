The first thing I learned about Atlantis was that Plato had mistranslated the word *sink*.

The second was why they had let him.

I found the city in a signal buried beneath the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. My team thought we were mapping magma chambers. Every eleven minutes, the seismographs registered a pulse too regular to be geological and too faint for anyone to have noticed before.

We lowered a probe into a fissure. At six thousand meters, its lights revealed a wall of black stone, smooth as glass. When the probe touched it, the wall lit up.

My monitors filled with symbols. Then the symbols arranged themselves into English.

**IS THE SKY QUIET?**

I told the others it was a software fault and sent them home. Then I typed:

**Yes.**

The reply took eleven minutes.

**HOW LONG?**

I thought of the telescope feeds I checked every morning: nebulae, asteroids, the patient dark between stars.

**As long as we’ve been listening.**

**THAT IS NOT THE SAME THING.**

A map appeared. At first I thought it showed galaxies. Then I recognized the outline of our solar system, and understood that the bright points surrounding it were much closer.

There were thousands of them.

I asked what they were.

**EARS.**

The messages came slowly after that. The system seemed to be drawing power from somewhere deep beneath the ridge, gathering enough between transmissions to answer each question.

Atlantis had been a civilization of builders. They had crossed oceans in hours, cured diseases by editing living cells, and learned to draw energy from the heat beneath the earth. When they discovered the listeners, they tried to speak to them.

For eighty-three years, nothing answered.

Then a city vanished.

Not burned. Not flooded. Every living person in it disappeared, leaving meals on tables and water running from fountains. Their animals remained. So did the people who had been asleep.

Atlantis sent soldiers to the empty city. The waking ones vanished too.

Eventually, they understood: the listeners followed thought. Not ordinary thought, scattered and private, but the particular shape of a mind trying to understand them. Each answer made that shape clearer. Each act of study helped them find another mind.

Sleep protected people because dreams were too tangled to follow. Distance offered no protection at all.

The Atlanteans built their greatest machine beneath the ocean, using the weight and noise of the sea to scatter the signal of human thought. Then they moved everyone they could into the cities around it and sealed them below.

The machine had been running ever since.

**Why tell me?** I typed.

The cursor blinked for eleven minutes.

**YOU OPENED THE WALL.**

I checked the probe feed. Its metal arm was still pressed against the stone. Around it, the illuminated symbols had begun to spread.

**CLOSE IT.**

**I CANNOT. YOU MUST FORGET WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED.**

I stared at the words. My hands were shaking too badly to type.

The system displayed a diagram of a human brain. A small cluster near the center glowed red. Beside it were instructions for building a device from components in our lab.

**THIS WILL REMOVE THE MEMORY.**

I called the project director instead.

She answered on the third ring, sleepy and irritated. I told her to come to the lab alone. Before she could ask why, the line went dead.

Her name remained on the screen. I called back. Her phone rang until it went to voicemail.

I drove to her house. The front door was unlocked, and her car was in the driveway. Upstairs, her husband lay in bed, breathing softly. On the floor beside him was her phone.

I returned to the lab and built the device.

It took four hours. Twice I nearly called someone for help, but I kept seeing her phone on the carpet. When the device was finished, I set it beside the terminal and destroyed the backups of the probe data. I wiped the drives, then broke them open and dropped their platters into the furnace.

That left the wall. I sent the probe one final command, telling it to reverse out of the fissure. It pulled its arm free. The symbols faded, and the wall went black.

I wrote myself a note.

*The survey failed. Do not return to these coordinates.*

I put it on the keyboard, placed the device against my temple, and pressed the switch.

I woke on the lab floor with a headache and no memory of the previous day. The note was there, along with a dismantled piece of equipment I couldn’t identify.

The director was missing. The police questioned all of us. I told them what I knew, which was very little.

Three months later, I found this account in my desk drawer, handwritten on pages torn from a lab notebook. I recognized my handwriting, though I couldn’t remember writing it. The last line read:

*If you find this, put it back. Don’t make a copy.*

I should have listened.

I typed it out because I wanted someone else to tell me whether it could be true.

Since I started, my phone has rung twice. Both times, the caller ID showed my own number.
