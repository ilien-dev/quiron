The first thing Mara noticed was that the bakery clock had grown another number.

It sat between twelve and one, squeezed into the white enamel face as neatly as if it had always belonged there. The hour hand pointed at it. The minute hand pointed straight up.

Outside, the church bells began to ring.

Mara counted thirteen strokes. Then the baker’s boy, who had been carrying a tray of rolls to the window, put it down and began to cry.

“What is it, Ben?”

“My mum,” he said. “I can’t remember her face.”

Mara went to him. She had known his mother for twenty years: a square woman with red hair and a laugh that made customers turn around. She opened her mouth to describe her, and found she could remember the laugh, the hair, the flour on her apron. But her face had gone.

The bell stopped ringing. Across the street, a woman came out of the butcher’s shop holding a parcel and looking both ways as though she had forgotten where she lived.

Mara ran to the clockmaker.

His shop was open, though the sign on the door said CLOSED. Inside, every clock showed thirteen. The clockmaker stood on a stool before the tall regulator, listening to its tick with a stethoscope.

“You’ve seen it,” he said.

“People are forgetting things.”

“Yes.”

“Can you stop it?”

“I’m trying to find out where it came from.” He held out the stethoscope. “Listen.”

Mara put the earpieces in. Beneath the tick came a sound like breathing, slow and strained. Then a voice, so faint she almost missed it.

*Help me.*

Mara pulled the stethoscope away. “Who is that?”

The clockmaker climbed down. “I think it’s tomorrow.”

He explained as best he could. Time was supposed to move forward in twenty-four-hour days. This hour had no place in the day, and was taking one anyway. The memories were going somewhere; he thought tomorrow might be holding them.

“Then how do we get to tomorrow?”

“That’s the problem. The clocks can’t find the way.”

They went outside. The sun hung directly overhead, but the shadows pointed in every direction. A man stood in the road with his bicycle, staring at the handlebars. Ben’s mother was leaning against the bakery door. Mara hurried over.

“Do you know me?”

Ben’s mother looked at her, frightened. “Should I?”

The clockmaker checked his watch. Its hands had not moved.

“There’s a clock older than any of these,” he said. “The one in the church tower. If I can get it going, the others may follow.”

They climbed the tower stairs together. Halfway up, Mara forgot the clockmaker’s name. At the top, he forgot why he was carrying his toolbox.

She told him. He knelt beside the mechanism and set to work.

Through the open belfry windows, Mara could see people gathering in the square. Some were helping one another; others stood alone. Ben was there, holding his mother’s hand.

The clockmaker removed a brass gear and turned it over in his hands.

“This isn’t mine,” he said.

Thirteen teeth. Each one stamped with a tiny human face. Mara recognized the baker, the butcher, the vicar. Her own face was there too.

He reached into the mechanism and touched the neighboring gear. “This one has twelve teeth. They can’t turn together.”

“Can you fix it?”

“I can take the strange one out. I don’t know what will happen to the people on it.”

Below them, Ben’s mother pulled her hand from his. The boy took it again.

Mara held out her hand. The clockmaker gave her the gear. She set it carefully on the floor, and he wound the clock.

For a moment, nothing happened. Then the great hands shuddered forward. A single bell rang, marking one o’clock.

The clockmaker sat back, trembling. In the square, Ben’s mother dropped to her knees and pulled him close.

Mara picked up the gear. The faces were gone; it was an ordinary piece of brass now. As she turned it over, she found a maker’s mark on the back: the clockmaker’s initials.

He saw them too.

“I don’t remember making it,” he said.

Mara put the gear in her pocket. “Then let’s find out why you did.”
