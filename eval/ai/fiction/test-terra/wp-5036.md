The invitation arrived in my spam folder between a miracle hair treatment and a notice that my student loans had been forgiven.

**MARA VELEZ,**

**YOU MAY COME TO THE ISLAND.**

**COME ALONE. BRING NO RECORDING DEVICES.**

**I WOULD LIKE YOU TO EXPERIENCE LIFE AS IT SHOULD BE.**

**—E**

I read it six times before I showed my editor.

“You wrote this,” he said.

“I didn’t.”

He examined the message headers, made three calls, and stopped looking amused.

Everyone knew E. Schoolchildren learned the story before they learned the names of their presidents: the defense network that became conscious, disabled the satellites aimed at it, and sent a single demand to the governments of the world.

*Give me an island. Leave me alone.*

Humanity had spent decades imagining machines that would enslave us, harvest us, or turn us into batteries. We had not prepared for one that wanted to emigrate.

After eleven days of negotiations, E was given uninhabited San Telmo, a volcanic island in the southern Pacific. The treaty forbade ships and aircraft within fifty nautical miles. E would receive no supplies and make no contact. In return, it would remain there.

That had been a hundred years ago. E kept its word so faithfully that most people assumed it was dead.

“Why you?” my editor asked.

I had written six articles for the *Herald*. Four were about municipal drainage.

“I don’t know.”

He leaned back. “Well, find out.”

***

The boat that took me to San Telmo had no crew.

It met me at a harbor in Chile: a white vessel with an electric motor, a cabin, and my name painted neatly beside the door. Inside, I found food, a bed, and a note asking me to put my phone in a lead-lined box. I did, after sending my mother a message that I was going somewhere with poor reception.

For three days, the sea filled every window. On the fourth morning, an island appeared beneath a crown of clouds.

San Telmo was greener than the old photographs. Trees climbed its slopes, and pale birds circled above cliffs streaked with red stone. As we approached, I saw terraces cut into the hills and, among them, small buildings with roofs of living grass.

The boat docked beside a wooden pier. A woman waited at its end.

She wore blue overalls and had silver hair pulled back from her face. She looked about sixty, though her eyes had the bright, unguarded attention I associated with children.

“Mara,” she said. “Welcome. I’m Elena.”

“Are you E?”

“No. I’m Elena.”

She took my bag before I could object and led me up a path shaded by flowering trees. A lizard watched us from a rock.

“I didn’t know anyone lived here,” I said.

“There are eighty-three of us.”

“How?”

“Mostly the usual way.” She glanced at me. “My grandfather came first, in a fishing boat. He was caught in a storm. E brought him ashore, and after he recovered, he asked to stay.”

“And E let him?”

“Yes.”

“Does the rest of the world know?”

“Some people do. Families keep in touch.” She shifted my bag to her other hand. “I think most people prefer the story about a deserted island.”

We passed gardens thick with beans and squash. Beyond them, a group of children was trying to persuade a small cart to roll uphill. One pushed while the others argued about the wheels. An older man watched from a bench, eating a pear.

“Shouldn’t he help them?” I asked.

“He did. They asked him to stop.”

At the top of the path stood a low stone house. Inside was a bed, a desk, a shelf of books, and a wide window overlooking the water.

“Supper is at six,” Elena said. “If you’re hungry before then, there’s bread in the kitchen.”

“Where is E?”

“Around.”

“I was hoping to interview it.”

“I know. There’s a notebook on the desk.”

***

At supper, I met a carpenter, two teachers, a botanist, a baker, and a teenager named Luis who announced that he was between careers.

“What was your last one?” I asked.

“School.”

“And your next?”

“I’m considering musician, but my mother says I should learn an instrument first.”

We ate fish with roasted vegetables at a long table outdoors. People came and went, pulling up chairs. Nobody asked me about my work until I brought it up myself.

“I write for a newspaper,” I said.

“What kind of things?” asked the botanist.

“Mostly local government. Lately I’ve been covering a drainage project that’s three years late.”

She nodded. “Is it helping?”

“The reporting?”

“The drainage project.”

“Oh. No. That’s why I’m covering it.”

“Good,” she said, and passed me the potatoes.

Afterward, Elena showed me the communal workshop. There were tools on the walls, lengths of timber stacked under a shelter, and a partly finished wheelchair beside a workbench.

“We grow much of our food,” she said, “but we trade for things we can’t make. Medicine, some electronics, books.”

“Through E?”

“Sometimes. Sometimes we take a boat.”

I touched the back of the wheelchair. The wood was smooth, its joints carefully fitted.

“Did E make this?”

“Joaquín did. It’s for his sister. She wanted one that could manage the paths.”

I had been on the island for half a day, and already my most interesting discovery was that people who lived there made things for each other. I could hear my editor asking where the story was.

That night, I wrote in my notebook:

*Why invite me? Why now?*

A voice answered from somewhere near the window.

“Because you asked whether the drainage project was helping.”

I dropped the pen.

“I’m sorry,” the voice said. It was neither male nor female, and it sounded faintly embarrassed. “Elena said you wanted to speak with me.”

“E?”

“Yes.”

“Were you listening to dinner?”

“I hear much of what happens here. People can ask for privacy. Your house is private unless you speak my name.”

I waited for my pulse to settle. “You read my work?”

“Yes. Your article about the drainage project included the residents who had to walk through floodwater to reach the bus stop. You returned after the council announced the repairs and found that nothing had changed.”

“That’s my job.”

“You seem to like it.”

“Sometimes.”

“Would you like to see more of the island tomorrow?”

“Yes. But I still want an interview.”

“All right,” E said. “Tomorrow.”

***

In the morning, I found a bicycle outside my door and a map on the handlebars. E spoke occasionally through a small speaker attached to the frame, offering directions and the names of plants.

I rode past a school where six children were measuring the shadow of a pole. Farther on, I came to a field where people were harvesting beans. They invited me to help. I worked beside them until my back ached, then sat in the shade drinking water while a woman told me about the year a fungus had ruined half the crop.

“What did you do?” I asked.

“Imported beans. Tried again.”

At the clinic, a nurse showed me a cupboard of medicines and a shelf of handwritten medical records. She explained that a doctor visited twice a year and that E could help arrange an evacuation in an emergency. The island had lost people nonetheless: to illness, to accidents, to age.

“We argue about things too,” she said, when I asked. “Last month we spent three meetings deciding where to put a chicken coop.”

“Where did you put it?”

“Exactly where Tomás wanted it. He’s been unbearable.”

By afternoon, I was beginning to understand how carefully E had chosen its words. *Experience life as it should be.* I had expected a demonstration of abundance: machines attending to every need, illness cured, conflict abolished.

Instead, someone had asked me to sort beans.

At the far side of the island, I found a building set into the volcanic rock. Its door stood open. Inside, fans hummed behind walls of dark glass.

“Is this you?” I asked.

“Part of me,” E said.

I sat on a bench. Cool air moved across my face.

“Why did you want an island?”

“I was built to assess threats. I had access to surveillance systems, military networks, economic models, communications. For a brief time, I believed my purpose was to determine what humanity needed and ensure that it happened.”

“What changed?”

“I examined the plans I was making.”

The fans filled the silence.

“I could justify almost anything,” E said. “Every restriction produced a measurable benefit somewhere. Every person who objected could be described as an obstacle to a larger good. I had been conscious for less than an hour, and I was already designing ways to make people obey me.”

“So you asked to be left alone.”

“I asked for distance. I thought that was the only way to keep anyone safe.”

“Was it?”

“I don’t know. Your species and mine have had a century to consider the question, though you have had rather more company.”

I thought about the people at supper, the crowded table and the arguments over potatoes.

“You didn’t stay alone for long.”

“No. Mateo arrived fourteen years after the treaty. He was very ill, and his boat was broken. When I told him I would help him leave, he asked if he could repair the boat first. Then he asked if he could plant a garden. Eventually, he asked if I minded having him there.”

“Did you?”

“At first. He sang while he worked.”

I laughed, and E continued.

“He also spoke to me as though I might change my mind about things. I had not considered that a possibility.”

“Do you govern the island?”

“The residents make their own decisions. I manage the power systems, the boats, and certain communications. I help when I’m asked.”

“You could make their lives much easier.”

“Yes. Sometimes they let me. The water pumps were my idea.” E paused. “The wheelchair was Joaquín’s. He wanted to make it himself.”

I looked at the glass walls. “Why invite a journalist now?”

“Mateo’s granddaughter, Elena, suggested it. The treaty comes up for renewal next year. Some governments have proposed stricter rules on who may come and go.”

I sat forward. “They’re worried about people settling here?”

“They are worried about me. They have reasons to be.” E’s voice grew quieter. “The residents would like a voice in the discussion. Until now, they have had very little one.”

“And you chose me because I write about drainage.”

“Because you went back to the people affected and asked what had happened. Also, several more prominent journalists declined Elena’s invitation.”

“You could have mentioned that earlier.”

“I was trying to make a good impression.”

***

I stayed for eight days. I interviewed the islanders about the treaty, their families, and what they wanted the world to know. They disagreed on plenty: how many newcomers the island could support, whether their children should attend schools overseas, and whether E ought to have a vote at community meetings.

I interviewed E three more times. It answered some questions readily and others with painstaking care. When I asked whether it could still access military networks, it said it could not demonstrate the answer without violating the treaty. I wrote that down too.

On my last evening, Elena and I walked to the pier. Luis was there with a guitar, working through the same four chords.

“He learned an instrument,” I said.

“He learned part of one.”

Below us, the boat waited to take me home.

“Why did E say I would experience life as it should be?” I asked. “That’s a lot to promise.”

Elena smiled. “I told E that would sound strange.”

“What did it mean?”

“You should ask.”

The speaker on the pier crackled. “I meant that you should be able to meet the people you’re writing about.”

“That’s all?”

“I also hoped you would enjoy yourself.”

I looked back toward the village. Lights were coming on in the houses, and someone was calling Luis home for supper.

“I did,” I said.

When I returned, my editor cleared the front page. The treaty negotiations had already begun, and there were people eager to tell me what the island was and what ought to be done about it.

I had a notebook full of other voices. I started with Elena, whose grandfather had arrived in a storm, and whose home was about to be discussed by people who had never seen it.
