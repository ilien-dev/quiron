The first time Mara died, she was trying to save a tree.

It was a foolish tree, according to her husband. A pear tree that had never produced a decent pear, planted too close to the house by a woman who had died before either of them moved in. Its branches scraped the bedroom window whenever the wind came from the east.

Still, when the storm split its trunk, Mara went out with rope.

She remembered the rain. She remembered pulling the ladder beneath the broken limb and thinking that, if she could just bind it until morning, perhaps the wound would close. Then lightning found the highest thing in the garden.

When she woke, she was lying in wet grass. The ladder had burned. The tree was a blackened stump.

Her husband found her there at dawn, naked and shivering, with the rope fused into a knot beside her.

The doctors called it a miracle. Mara called it a shame about the tree.

***

For twenty-three years, nothing else happened.

Mara and her husband grew older. They raised two children and buried a dog. Her husband’s hair went white at the temples, and Mara’s stayed brown. People said she was lucky. She bought gray dye and learned to complain about her knees.

Then a lorry struck her car on the way home from the market.

She woke in a hospital refrigerator, beneath a sheet that smelled faintly of bleach. When the attendant opened the drawer, she sat up and apologized.

Her husband came to collect her. He held her so tightly that she heard something crack in his back.

At home, she told him about the gray dye.

“I know,” he said.

“You knew?”

“I found the bottles. I thought you’d tell me when you were ready.”

She asked whether he had noticed anything else.

“The cut on your hand, when you broke that glass. It was gone by supper.” He touched her cheek. “I didn’t know what to say.”

They sat together until the sky brightened. In the morning, he made tea and asked if she still wanted to visit their daughter on Sunday.

***

Her husband died in his sleep at seventy-eight.

Mara found him with one hand tucked beneath his cheek, the way he had slept since she’d known him. She lay beside him for an hour, trying to remember whether she had said goodnight.

She had. She remembered, eventually. He had asked her to close the curtains, and she had said goodnight as she did it.

At the funeral, an old woman approached her beside the grave.

“You have it,” the woman said.

Mara looked at her. “Have what?”

“The gift.”

The woman wore a coat too thin for the weather. Her face was deeply lined, but her eyes were clear and alert. She held out a gloved hand.

Mara ignored it. “Who are you?”

“Someone who has been looking for you.”

They walked beyond the mourners, past graves sunk unevenly into the earth. The woman told Mara that a handful of people had been given this life. They healed. They did not age beyond the day of their choosing, though none of them had done the choosing.

“Why me?” Mara asked.

“I don’t know who makes the choice. I only know it always falls on someone who didn’t want it.”

“Then take it back.”

“I can’t.”

“You found me to tell me that?”

“I found you because I was alone for a very long time. I thought you might want to talk to someone.”

Mara stared at her until the woman gave her an address, written on the back of a receipt. She put it in her pocket.

***

Her children grew old. Mara moved away, then returned under another name to care for her son when his hands began to shake. He knew her, though she had told him she was a distant cousin. One afternoon he called her Mum while she was helping him button his shirt.

“I thought you’d be angry,” she said.

“I was,” he said. “For a while.”

“Why didn’t you say anything?”

He looked down at the buttons. “I didn’t want you to leave again.”

She stayed until he died.

Her daughter lived another eleven years. Mara saw her often, introducing herself to the grandchildren as a friend. When her daughter became ill, Mara sat beside her bed and read aloud from a book they had both loved when she was small.

The receipt had long since worn through along its folds. Mara had copied the address into a notebook. After her daughter’s funeral, she found the notebook and went to the house.

A young man answered the door. He told her his grandmother had died twenty years earlier.

“She used to talk about a friend she was hoping would visit,” he said.

Mara thanked him. She walked to the end of the street and sat on a wall until it grew dark.

***

Centuries passed. Mara worked as a baker, a nurse, a surveyor, a teacher, and, once, for forty miserable years, an accountant. She learned that cities could vanish and languages could change while people still found ways to be unkind to one another.

She learned, too, that children would always bring injured creatures to whoever seemed least likely to laugh.

In a town by the sea, a girl arrived at Mara’s door carrying a gull wrapped in her coat. Its wing hung at a terrible angle.

“My father says it’s no use,” the girl said.

“Your father may be right.”

The girl’s face crumpled.

“But let’s have a look.”

They made a splint from thin strips of wood. For six weeks, the gull lived in a box beside Mara’s stove and screamed whenever either of them came near. The girl visited every day after school, bringing scraps of fish and reports about her classmates.

When the wing had healed, they carried the box to the beach. The gull climbed out, bit Mara’s thumb, and flew away.

“Will it remember us?” the girl asked.

“I don’t know.”

The girl considered this. “I’ll remember it.”

She came back the next day without an injured animal. She wanted to know if Mara would teach her to bake.

***

Mara met the second immortal in a place that had once been a train station. The roof was gone, and trees grew between the platforms. A man sat on a bench, turning a coin over and over in his fingers.

He watched her pass.

“You’re one too,” he said.

She stopped. “How can you tell?”

“I’ve been following you for three days. You fell off a roof yesterday.”

“I slipped.”

“From a roof.”

She sat beside him. He had the frightened, exhausted look she remembered from her own face, long ago in a hospital mirror.

“How long?” she asked.

“Seven years.”

“Does anyone know?”

“My sister did. She died last month.”

He pressed the coin into his palm. For a while they listened to the leaves moving overhead.

“Is there a way to end it?” he asked.

Mara thought of the woman at her husband’s funeral, standing in the cold with an address written on a receipt.

“I haven’t found one,” she said.

The man nodded, staring at the tracks.

“I have a room nearby,” Mara said. “There’s a chair that folds out into a bed. The stove is temperamental, but I know how to make it work.”

“I don’t know you.”

“My name is Mara. I make good bread.”

He almost smiled.

She stood and waited while he turned the coin once more. Then he put it in his pocket and followed her home.
