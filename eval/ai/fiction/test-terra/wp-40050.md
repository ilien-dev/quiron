The first president I told asked for a chair.

That was President Keene, in 1977. We were in a windowless room beneath the Treasury, with a red folder between us. He’d been in office eleven days.

“Are you certain?” he asked.

“Yes, sir.”

“How long?”

“Since Washington.”

He sat down. He read every page, including the appendices, and asked for a pencil. For three hours he made notes in the margins. Then he closed the folder and said, “We’ll need to increase the food supply.”

That was how I learned a president could be afraid and useful at the same time.

My name is Daniel Mercer. Officially, I spent forty-two years with the Secret Service, mostly in protective intelligence. Unofficially, I was the man who introduced new presidents to the thing beneath the country.

There are forty-eight doors to its chamber, one in each contiguous state. The oldest is in Virginia. The newest is under a truck stop outside Las Vegas, behind a freezer that has never contained ice cream. Each is guarded by people who believe they’re guarding something else.

The thing calls itself America, though it had another name before there were people here to hear it. It is older than the mountains and large enough that no one has seen all of it. It has slept beneath this land for a very long time.

We feed it memories.

Not the ones you’d expect. It has no taste for war or triumph. Give it a man’s recollection of winning the World Series and it will let it drift back to him by morning. What it likes are small kindnesses: a stranger carrying groceries, a child sharing the last orange, a nurse sitting with someone who has no family.

A memory given freely lasts it years. A stolen one turns sour.

Keene established the voluntary donation program. Donors were told their memories would help with a classified national security project. Most signed without reading the forms. I used to hate that. Later, I met some of the people who’d read every word and signed anyway.

President Bell, in 1981, laughed when I told him.

Then I showed him a jar containing a memory of his mother singing while she washed his hair. He went white.

“You took that from me?”

“No, sir. You gave it, when you were six. Your mother worked for the program.”

He held the jar to the light. The memory moved inside it, gold and slow, like a fish turning in deep water.

“Can I have it back?”

I told him yes. He held it for a long time, then put it back on the table. “Ask me again when I’m old,” he said.

President Armitage demanded to see the creature. We took him through the Virginia door, down a staircase cut into stone that sweated warm water. At the bottom, an eye opened.

Armitage saluted it.

The eye closed. On the drive back, he asked whether anyone had tried talking to it. By the end of his term, he had visited eleven times.

President Vale wanted to weaponize it. I won’t pretend otherwise. He asked how much of a foreign capital it could swallow and whether we could make it wake on command.

The creature answered him itself. Every clock in the White House stopped at 3:17. For one minute, Vale forgot his daughter’s name.

He never asked again.

The last president I briefed was Elena Torres. She listened without interrupting, then opened the folder and turned past the diagrams to the donor records.

“These people lose their memories?”

“They do, ma’am.”

“Even the ones they might need?”

“We ask them to choose carefully.”

She read for a while. “I see a lot of donors who needed the money.”

There was a payment option. I’d helped set the rates.

Torres ordered a review before authorizing the next collection. I argued that the creature couldn’t wait through a review.

“Then let’s go and ask it,” she said.

We went to Virginia. The eye opened, and she told it what we’d been doing. For a long time nothing happened. Then the warm water on the walls began to run, carrying a thought into both our heads: *I can wait.*

The review lasted six months. The creature slept. When we asked people to give without payment, many still did. We also began returning the memories of donors who wanted them back.

I retired that year. On my last day, I went down alone and offered it a memory: my wife laughing as we danced badly in our kitchen.

The eye opened. It remembered her too. I’d given it the same memory once before, when she was dying and we needed the money.
