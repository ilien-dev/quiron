**The Slow Burn**

The Archmage Voss had been monologuing for eleven minutes when he noticed that neither of his captives was looking at the other.

"...and so, with the Heart of Ashkara, I shall unmake the sun itself!" He paused. Waited. Gestured grandly at the two figures chained to the obsidian pillars. "Any thoughts?"

Captain Mara Delacroix, sword-arm bruised, chewed her lip. "Is the sun-unmaking reversible?"

"No."

"Then I'm against it." She turned to the man beside her. "Tobias, do you have a plan, or are we doing the thing where I yell and you apologize to the guards?"

"I have a plan," said Tobias Wren, cartographer, second-class adventurer, owner of one good coat. "It's a bad plan. I need you to hit that lever with your boot, and I need you to not ask me how I know about the lever."

"Fine."

"Fine."

Voss frowned. This was not going how the prophecy had described. The prophecy, which he had read nine times, was quite clear: *two souls bound by fate, drawn together by peril, their love the key that would unlock the Heart.* He had arranged the whole kidnapping around this. He'd even set the lighting.

"You do understand," Voss said, "that you are in mortal danger. Together. Chained *very close* to one another."

"Yes," said Mara.

"Does that not stir anything in you?"

"My wrists, mostly. These manacles are cheap."

Voss lowered his staff. Behind him, his lieutenant, a hulking creature named Gorb, shifted uncomfortably. Gorb had read the prophecy too. Gorb had put money on this.

"Perhaps," Voss tried, "you've never noticed how the torchlight catches her hair, Cartographer."

Tobias squinted at Mara. "It's a nice color. Like a fox. Mara, you look like a fox."

"Thank you. You look like a man who's about to be very smug about a lever."

"I do not."

"You absolutely do."

They grinned at each other, easy and bright, the way you grin at someone who has seen you at your worst and still shares their rations. Voss waited for the grin to curdle into longing. It did not. It stayed exactly what it was: two friends, mildly delighted by one another.

"This is *impossible*," Voss muttered. "The texts were unambiguous."

"Maybe you read it wrong," offered Tobias.

"I read it nine times!"

"Which translation?"

Voss opened his mouth. Closed it. "...The Valdrian."

Tobias winced with real professional sympathy. "Oh, *no*. Nobody uses the Valdrian. It's riddled with errors. The word they render as 'love' is *kavesh*, which in the older dialect means..." He glanced at Mara. "Help me out."

"Mutual regard," said Mara. "Or a really good trade agreement. It's contextual."

"A trade agreement," Voss whispered.

"Or a *sworn alliance*," said Tobias. "Depending on the verb ending."

Gorb made a small, wounded sound. "I had fifty crowns on the kiss."

"Everyone did," Voss snapped. "The entire *dungeon* had a pool." He pressed his fingers to his temples. "Do you have any idea how much I spent on the candles?"

"They're lovely candles," said Mara kindly.

"Don't be nice to me, I'm the villain!"

"You're a *bad* villain," Mara said, "but you're consistent, and I respect that."

"Mara," Tobias murmured. "The lever."

"Right."

She hooked her boot toward the iron lever half-hidden behind the pillar. Voss, realizing his mistake far too late, lunged. But Gorb, who had lost fifty crowns and his faith in the old texts, stepped quietly into his path and said, "Sorry, boss. Morale's been low."

The lever clanked down. The chains fell open like startled birds. Mara caught Tobias's elbow as the floor shuddered, steadying him without thinking, and he caught hers in return, and neither of them thought anything of it at all.

Voss, defeated, sat down heavily on the steps of his own altar. The Heart of Ashkara dimmed to a dull, disappointed amber. Without the prophecy's key, it was just a very large, very ornate rock.

"Well," said Mara, cracking her knuckles. "That's the sun saved. Drinks?"

"Drinks," Tobias agreed. "You're buying."

"I bought last time."

"You bought *one* time."

"It was a *big* time."

They wandered toward the exit, bickering happily, shoulder to shoulder, perfectly content.

Voss watched them go. Gorb sat down beside him and offered him a flask.

"For what it's worth," Gorb said, "I think it's a good friendship."

"It's a *wonderful* friendship," Voss said bitterly. "That's the problem. Nobody wrote a prophecy about that."

Somewhere above them, the sun, which had never once been in danger from the romantic subplot, rose on schedule.
