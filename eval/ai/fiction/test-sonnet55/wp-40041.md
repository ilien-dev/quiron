**The Reliquary of the Small Gods**

Dr. Imara Voss brushed the last of the silt from the find and held her breath.

"Tell me you're seeing this," she said into her collar mic.

Her field assistant, Teo, lowered himself into the trench beside her. Three hundred meters of compacted landfill strata pressed down above them, a layer cake of the Late Disposable Age. They had been sifting through it for eleven weeks, and until now it had given them nothing but fragments: brittle polymer shards, corroded wire, the occasional shining scrap of something that had refused to rot.

This was different. It was intact.

A slab of black glass, thin as a communion wafer, fitted into a frame of pale metal. One face was perfectly smooth and dark. The other bore a single circular depression and, near the top, a tiny cluster of lenses, three of them, set in a raised square like a many-eyed creature peering from its burrow.

"A mirror," Teo said. "Ceremonial. Look how black it is."

"Too thin. Too precise." Imara turned it over. Along the edge ran a seam of microscopic ports, and on the back, a bitten fruit had been etched in the metal. "The fruit has a bite taken out of it. That's deliberate. A symbol of knowledge, maybe. Forbidden fruit."

"A priestly object, then."

"Look at the wear." She tilted it under her lamp. The glass was webbed with fine cracks, spidering out from one corner. Yet the fracture was clearly old and clearly *tolerated*. Someone had kept using it. "They cared for it, but they didn't replace it. Whoever owned this carried it everywhere. The metal is polished smooth at the edges, here, where a hand would curve around it. And the circular depression is worn almost flat from a thumb."

Teo whistled softly. "Every day. For years."

"Several times an hour, I'd guess." She pointed to the dark face. Beneath the cracks, ghostly smudges overlapped in swirls and arcs, the residue of countless touches. "Look at the pattern. Always the same motions. A downward drag. A pinch. A tap. Like a liturgy."

They carried it to the field lab, where the generator hummed and the dust-filters sighed. Imara set the slab on a cradle and began the long work of documentation. By the third day, she had assembled a theory.

"The Ancients had a god of the pocket," she announced to the assembled team. "Not one god. Millions. Every person owned one, a personal oracle, carried against the body. Its altar was the glass. They touched it upon waking. They touched it before sleep. They touched it at meals, at funerals, in the presence of their own children."

"Why?" asked a graduate student.

"To be seen, I think. Look at the lenses. Three eyes facing outward, one facing inward. They *watched themselves* in it. The glass went dark and returned their own face." She paused. "And the wear patterns suggest they spent more hours with this object than with any person in their lives."

A silence settled over the lab.

"There's more." She had saved it. Along the bottom edge, the ports included one narrow slot, and the lab's technician had managed to coax a trickle of power through it, as one might drip water onto a dying man's lips. For nine seconds, the glass lit.

It showed a small grid of colored squares. Beneath them, a number floated in a red circle: **2,847**.

"Unread messages?" Teo guessed. "Prayers?"

"Petitions," Imara said quietly. "Thousands of them, unanswered. And look." She zoomed the image. In the corner, a small photograph glowed: a child, perhaps four years old, laughing with frosting on its chin. The lock-screen of the oracle. The thing its owner had chosen to see first, every time.

The glass flickered and died.

Nobody spoke. The generator hummed.

"We assumed they worshipped it," Teo said at last. "But they didn't, did they?"

Imara looked at the cracked black slab, at the worn place where a thumb had rested a million times, at the little ghost of a laughing child.

"I think," she said, "they were just trying to hold on to everyone they loved. All at once. All the time. And it was never quite enough."

She covered the artifact with a clean cloth, gently, the way one covers a sleeping person, and wrote in her field notebook:

*Specimen 4471. Function: unknown. Significance: profound.*

Then, after a moment, she crossed out *unknown* and wrote nothing in its place.
