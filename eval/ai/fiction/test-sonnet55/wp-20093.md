**The Renewal Clause**

The man on my porch wore a charcoal suit and held a manila folder, and he smelled faintly of struck matches. It was my twenty-first birthday, 4:47 in the afternoon, and I had a cake in the oven.

"Dana Okafor?" he said. "I'm here about the contract."

"I don't have a contract. I'm not buying anything."

"You bought something in 2010." He smiled, apologetic. "Can I come in? It's easier to show you."

I should have shut the door. But he had the kind of face that made you feel rude for suspecting him, and I was curious, and the cake needed another twenty minutes anyway.

He spread the papers across my kitchen table. At the bottom of the last page was a signature in purple crayon. The D was backwards. Next to it was a drawing of a horse, or possibly a dog.

"That's not a legal signature," I said.

"You were five. We accepted it." He tapped the page. "You'd lost your balloon at the county fair. Big red one. You were sitting behind the funnel cake stand, sobbing, and I asked what I could do to help."

A memory moved somewhere deep in me, like a fish turning under ice. Red balloon. Sticky hands. A kind man crouched down to my height, who'd said, *What if I could get it back?*

"You got me a balloon," I said slowly.

"I got you *the* balloon. It had drifted four miles. I made some arrangements." He shrugged. "In exchange, you gave me your soul on a twenty-one-year lease. Very standard. Terms are on page two."

I read page two. It was, unfortunately, in plain language. *Party of the Second Part shall receive one (1) red balloon, retrieved. Party of the First Part shall receive custody of Party of the Second Part's soul upon her twenty-first birthday.*

"Custody," I said.

"Eventually. But there's a renewal option." He brightened. "That's why I'm here. Most people just want to renew. Another twenty-one years, same terms, and you keep your soul a while longer."

"What's the catch?"

"There's a small fee."

"How small?"

He turned to the last page. The number was not a number of money. It was a list. *One (1) memory of first love. The ability to taste cinnamon. Three (3) years from the end of her life, to be selected at our discretion.*

"Absolutely not."

"Cinnamon's overrated."

"I like cinnamon!" I said. "I'm *making* a cinnamon cake!" Behind us, the oven hummed. "And you can't take years off the end of my life for a balloon."

"It was a very good balloon."

I put my head in my hands. When I looked up, he was watching me with something that wasn't quite malice. It was closer to professional patience, the face of a man who had done this ten thousand times and still hoped, each time, for a little drama.

"Okay," I said. "Let's talk about the original contract. I was five."

"Yes."

"A minor."

His smile flickered.

"Contracts with minors are voidable. I took Business Law last spring. A minor can disaffirm any contract, before or shortly after reaching majority." I stood up. I was almost enjoying myself. "I'm twenty-one today. Which means this is the moment. So I'm disaffirming."

"The Infernal Code doesn't recognize—"

"Does it recognize *anything*? Because you accepted a crayon drawing of a horse as a signature. Either you're bound by standards of consent or you're not. If you're not, you're a guy who tricked a crying child at a fair, and I'd love to hear how that plays in your own offices."

The room got warm. The light through the window bent oddly, and for a second his shadow was the wrong shape, too tall, with too many angles. Then it settled. He sighed and closed the folder.

"It was a dog, wasn't it?" he said. "The drawing."

"It's a horse. I was very clear about that at the time."

"Of course it was." He rubbed his eyes. "You know, I get one or two of these a decade. Kid signs, grows up, finds a loophole. It's always the same loophole. Management keeps saying we should stop contracting with minors, and then the quarterly numbers come in and nobody listens."

"So we're done?"

"Not quite." He slid a single new sheet across the table. "I can't keep your soul. But the balloon was a real service rendered. Quantum meruit. You'd owe me its fair value."

I read the sheet. *One (1) red balloon, approximate value: $1.50.*

I stared at him. He shrugged, almost sheepish.

I dug through my purse, found two singles, and slapped them on the table. "Keep the change."

He pocketed the money, tucked the folder under his arm, and stood. At the door he paused. "Happy birthday, Dana. For what it's worth, the cake smells wonderful."

"It's cinnamon."

"I know," he said gently. "That's why I asked about the fee."

Then he was gone, and the porch smelled of matches, and the timer on the oven began to chime.

I took the cake out and ate the first slice standing at the counter, slowly, tasting every bit of it.
