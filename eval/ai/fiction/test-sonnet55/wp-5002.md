**The Fine Print**

The waiting room of Temporal Makeover Inc. smelled like lemon cleaner and regret. I took a number. Forty-one. They were serving nine.

I had six days and nineteen hours left, which was the whole problem.

"Next," said the clerk, though no one had moved. He had the flat eyes of a man who'd heard every story twice. His nameplate read DENNIS, TIER II ADJUSTER.

"I need to undo Tuesday."

"Everybody needs to undo Tuesday." He slid a tablet across the counter. "Rules. Read them, initial them."

I knew them already. You could change what *you* did. Never what anyone else did. No resurrections, no lottery numbers, no stock tips, nothing that touched a death. The Seventh Day Clause: nothing older than a week. And the big one, printed in red: *No alteration may create a paradox of causality or avert a Registered Fatality.*

"I just want to take a different road home," I said.

Dennis raised an eyebrow. "Different road. That's a Class C. Four thousand."

"Fine."

"Reason for modification?"

I'd rehearsed this. "Traffic."

He studied me. Then he typed something, and the tablet chimed a soft, disapproving note. "Interesting. Our system flags that Tuesday at 5:52 p.m., on Delmar Avenue, there's a Registered Fatality."

My mouth went dry. "Is there."

"A pedestrian. Marguerite Oyelaran, sixty-eight. Struck by a gray sedan." He looked up. "Your plate number is attached to the sedan, sir."

The room had gone very quiet, or maybe I'd stopped hearing it.

"That's why I want a different road," I said. "If I'm not on Delmar, she's not hit."

"If you're not on Delmar, she's not hit *by you*." Dennis said it gently, the way you explain something to a child. "The Registered Fatality is a fixed point. The Bureau doesn't care who. If you vanish from Delmar, the universe fills the slot. A delivery van. A cyclist. Same woman, same minute. We've run it ten thousand times."

"Then what's the point of any of this?"

"Mostly? People who forgot to buy their mother a birthday card." He almost smiled. "Request denied. I can refund eighty percent."

I looked at the red text again. *Avert a Registered Fatality.* Averting, I thought. Not *altering*.

"What if I'm not averting it?" I said slowly. "What if I'm there, and I'm the one who stops?"

Dennis paused. "Stops."

"Not to prevent it. I was texting. I looked up late. What if I looked up *on time*, braked, and she crossed in front of me, and she just... didn't die? That's not me dodging the slot. That's me changing a decision. My decision."

"That's the same thing."

"Is it? Your own rule says the change can't create a paradox. A paradox is if the fatality never happens and something depends on it. Does something depend on it?"

He didn't answer. His eyes went to the screen, and for the first time they weren't flat.

"Does it?" I pressed.

"I'm not authorized to discuss downstream dependencies."

"Dennis."

He lowered his voice. "Sir, the Bureau puts fatalities on the Registry because *something* hangs off them. Sometimes it's small. Sometimes a woman dies on Delmar and thirty years later a kid becomes a surgeon because of it. You don't get to know which. That's the whole design. If you knew, you'd pay to fix it. Or pay to leave it."

"So I'm supposed to let her die on the chance that it matters."

"You're supposed to accept that you don't get to weigh it." He tapped the red text. "Sometimes rules are the only thing standing between a person and the worst idea they ever had."

I thought about Tuesday. The buzz of the phone. The soft, sickening thump I'd driven away from, because I was a coward and the road was empty.

"Then I'll pay for the card," I said. "The birthday card. Change Tuesday so I stop at the scene. Call 911. Stay."

Dennis blinked. "That's... not averting anything. She's still struck."

"She's still struck. But I stayed."

He looked at me a long time. Then, quietly, he typed.

"Class C," he said. "Four thousand."

I paid it.
