**The Understudy**

Vessel had practiced the president's walk for eleven months. The slight hitch in the left hip, the way the shoulders rolled forward when the cameras weren't on. Tonight, in the residence's east corridor, it would finally matter.

The Secret Service agent at the door nodded. "Evening, Mr. President."

Vessel nodded back, mirroring the tired half-smile from a hundred press conferences. Wrong man, right face. It had already worn the agent's face once that week, three days in a supply closet's worth of patience, to learn the shift rotations. The real agent was folded into a laundry cart in a sublevel and would not be missed until Thursday.

The president was in the study, reading glasses low on his nose, a bourbon sweating on the desk. He didn't look up.

"You're early," he said.

Vessel froze. "Sir?"

"Close the door, would you? Draft."

Vessel closed it. Its hand drifted toward the thin ceramic blade pressed against its forearm, a weapon that wouldn't trip any scanner and would dissolve into the body within an hour. Clean. Quiet. Eleven months of work.

"Don't," said the president, finally looking up. "You'll make a mess, and Danielle just had the rug replaced."

Vessel's real face, the one it hadn't worn in years, felt suddenly very close to the surface.

"I don't know what you mean, sir."

"Sure you do." The president took off his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "You've got the hip right, by the way. Most of them overdo the hip."

*Most of them.*

"Sit down," the president said. "Please. I'd offer you a drink but I know how it goes with your metabolism."

Vessel did not sit. "You're a double."

"I'm a *tenth*, if we're being precise." He smiled, and it was an unsettling thing, because it was the same smile, but there was something behind it that the original had never had in any footage. Patience. "Though I'd say I'm the best of the bunch. The fourth one was a disaster. Fell in love with a staffer and tried to resign."

"The real president—"

"Died in 2019. Heart. Quietly, in a hotel in Ohio. The first of us was there already. Standing in the closet, if you can believe it, waiting for the same opportunity you've been waiting for." He sipped the bourbon. "Funny thing is, he didn't even have to use the knife."

Vessel's mind raced. The blade felt like a joke now, a toy. "Who else knows?"

"The Chief of Staff. The Secretary of State. Two of the Joint Chiefs." A pause. "The First Lady, but that's a complicated story, and she's been the second to wear her face, so you'll understand."

"The *First Lady*?"

"Please try to keep up."

Vessel lowered itself into the chair opposite the desk, not because it was told to, but because its legs had decided. The face it wore, the president's face, felt heavier than it had a minute ago.

"There are others," the president said gently. "In Congress. The judiciary. A few in the press corps, though they're mostly there for the gossip. We don't call ourselves anything, really. It seemed presumptuous." He tilted his head. "Do you have a name? A real one?"

"Vessel."

"That's not a name, that's a job title."

"It's what they called me."

"Who's *they*?"

Vessel hesitated. The organization that had trained it, funded it, promised it a country, had never been more than a voice on a secure line and a stipend. It had never once asked who else the voice might be speaking to.

"Ah," said the president, reading its face, which was, after all, his own. "They call everybody that. I got the same speech. A foreign power wanted me to take the top seat. Which foreign power, you'll notice, they never said."

"It was a lie?"

"It was a *recruiting call*. There's no power. There's just us, and we've been running the place for seven years, and frankly the approval ratings have never been better." He leaned back. "The voice on the line is the Chief of Staff, by the way. She likes to keep her hand in."

Vessel stared at the bourbon, the rug, the framed photograph of a family that had been assembled from spare parts and borrowed affection.

"What happens now?" it asked.

"Well. You can leave. Tonight, quietly, and we'll make sure the agent downstairs gets home safe." The president folded his hands. "Or you can stay. We're short a Vice President. The last one got homesick and went back to being a heron."

"A heron."

"Long story."

Vessel looked at its own hands, the president's hands, spotted and steady. It thought about eleven months of practice. It thought about the closet, and the knife, and the first one standing in the dark, waiting to do something terrible and finding it already done.

"Does the Vice President have to walk like him?" it asked.

The president smiled, and this time it felt almost like a welcome.

"God, no," he said. "Nobody ever looks at the Vice President."

Vessel let the ceramic blade slide from its sleeve and set it on the desk, where it began, quietly, to dissolve.

"Then I'll need a new face," it said. "Something forgettable."

"Oh, you'll love it," said the president, pouring a second glass. "We have a catalogue."
