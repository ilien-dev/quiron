**Freefall**

I got the words when I was born, the way everyone does. They sat in neat black cursive along my left ribs, just under the bra line, where I could hide them and where I'd have to explain them to every doctor I ever met.

*Are you ready to die?*

My mother cried when she saw them. My father said they were probably a mistake, that ink sometimes smudges. They weren't a mistake. I took one look at them in the mirror at fourteen and understood that somewhere in the world was a person who would meet me and open with a threat.

I narrowed it down over the years. Assassin. Cult recruiter. Very intense dentist. My friend Priya, whose own mark read *Sorry, is this seat taken?*, said I was catastrophizing. "Maybe it's a hospice thing," she said. "Maybe they're really good at their job."

"That's worse."

"Maybe they're a goth."

By twenty-nine I'd stopped dating, mostly. It's hard to enjoy a first date when you're waiting for the guy to lean across the table and say the words. I once left a perfectly nice man at an Italian restaurant because he said "Are you ready to order?" and my whole body went rigid. He had said *order*. I knew that. I left anyway.

So I played it safe. I took the accounting job with the beige office, bought the sensible car, and kept a fire extinguisher in every room of my apartment. I flossed. I was ready for almost everything except the one sentence I was sure was coming.

Then Priya turned thirty and decided, with the cruelty of a best friend, that her gift to herself was skydiving. And that mine was coming along.

"Absolutely not," I said.

"You're not jumping. You're being *strapped* to a professional and *he* jumps. You just fall along with him."

"That's the same thing, Priya."

"It's tandem. Please. I need you there when I scream."

---

The airfield was a flat gray stretch outside town, with a hangar, a windsock, and a plane that looked older than I was. Priya hugged me in the parking lot. I signed a waiver that used the word *death* four times in the first paragraph, which felt unsubtle.

"Your instructor's name is Dev," said the girl at the desk. "He'll be out in a sec."

I stood in the gear room holding a jumpsuit like a hostage. Posters of smiling people mid-air covered the walls. Priya was across the room being fitted for a harness, already shrieking with joy. My ribs itched, the way they always did when I was nervous.

"Hi, you must be Nora."

I turned around.

He was maybe thirty-two, sunburned across the nose, with dark curly hair flattened by a headset and a clipboard tucked under one arm. He had the loose, easy posture of someone who spent his days stepping out of airplanes. He was smiling, and it was a good smile.

"I'm Dev. I'll be your tandem partner today. Before we go up, I have to run you through the safety briefing, and I'm required to ask you the final question." He glanced at the clipboard, then at me, and his smile turned a little wry. "Sorry, it's a corporate thing. They think it's funny."

My heart was a trapped bird.

"Are you ready to die?"

The room went quiet in my head. Somewhere very far away Priya whooped.

My ribs didn't burn or glow, which I'd half expected from all the movies. They just went warm, the way skin does when someone puts a hand over it. I stood there for a long moment.

"Is that a joke?" I managed.

"It's the dark-humor portion of the waiver. People usually laugh." He studied my face, and his own changed. "You're not laughing."

"No."

"Was it too much? I can skip it, I don't have to—"

"I've been waiting for you to say that my whole life."

I hadn't meant to say it out loud. Dev went still, the clipboard slipping a little in his grip.

"Say that again?"

I pulled up the hem of my shirt, right there in the gear room with the posters watching. The cursive sat on my ribs, black and patient, thirty years old. He looked at it for a long time. Then, very slowly, he set the clipboard on a bench and rolled up his left sleeve.

On the inside of his forearm, in the same handwriting, it said:

*Oh, no. I'm so sorry, I didn't mean to scare you.*

I read it twice and then I started laughing. It wasn't graceful. I laughed until I had to put a hand on the bench.

"I did say that," Dev said faintly. "Just now. Almost exactly. I said it in my head, anyway, when your face went white."

"Thirty years," I wheezed. "I thought you were going to be an *assassin*."

"I'm a skydiving instructor."

"I know that *now*."

He was laughing too by then, helplessly, one hand over his mouth. When we finally ran out of breath he looked at me, and I saw he was nervous in a way he'd never be in the sky.

"We don't have to go up," he said. "We could get coffee. I could tell you I'm sorry for the next several decades."

I thought about the beige office, the fire extinguishers, and every dinner I'd walked out on. For thirty years I had braced for a sentence like a blow, and it had turned out to be a bad joke from a man with sunburn and kind eyes.

"Dev."

"Yeah?"

"Are *you* ready?"

He grinned slowly. "Always."

---

We went up anyway.

The plane shuddered and roared, and Priya squeezed my hand and screamed something I couldn't hear. Dev clipped himself to my back and I felt his heartbeat through the harness, fast, matching mine. The door rolled open on a sky so enormous it didn't look real.

"Last chance," he shouted over the wind. "Ready?"

My whole life I'd thought that question meant an ending. But standing at the edge with his arms around me and the world laid out below like something I was allowed to have, I understood it could mean the opposite.

"Ready!" I yelled.

We fell.
