# Night Shift

The first time one of them saved my life, I assumed it was an accident.

I was pinned in a gas station bathroom outside Tulsa, nine weeks into the end of the world, holding a fire axe I didn't know how to swing. Seven biters leaned on the door, and the hinges were giving. I had made my peace, mostly.

Then the pressure stopped. Through the gap I heard wet, ugly sounds. When I finally opened the door, a man in a ruined mechanic's jumpsuit stood over the others, gray-skinned, one eye clouded white. Seven bodies lay at his feet. He looked at me, and I raised the axe.

He lowered his head. It wasn't an attack posture. It was almost a nod. Then he shuffled out into the sun and stood at the edge of the lot, facing the road like a man waiting for a bus.

After that, I started noticing.

The horde in Wichita parted around me like water around a stone. A woman in a tattered wedding dress wrenched a rusted gate open so I could pass, then held it shut behind me against the others. Once, feverish from bad water, I woke in a church basement with a dozen of them standing in a silent ring around my cot. A dented can of peaches sat by my hand. I'm not saying a zombie put it there. I'm saying nobody else was around.

I tried to work out why. Maybe it was my smell, or something in my blood. Maybe I was immune and they sensed it. I tested theories the way you do when you're alone. I walked in the open, then caked in mud, then in a dead man's coat. Nothing changed. They protected me anyway, the way you'd cup a hand around a candle.

I didn't find out why until Denver.

St. Anne's Hospital was still standing, and I'd come looking for antibiotics. The lobby doors swung shut behind me, and I saw hundreds of them packed shoulder to shoulder in the dim light. I thought I had finally been stupid enough to die.

They didn't move. They just looked at me with all those clouded eyes. Then an old man in a hospital gown, an IV stand still dragging from his wrist, stepped out of the crowd.

He held out his hand, slowly, palm up.

I stared at it, and something shook loose in me, because I knew that gesture. I had made it ten thousand times.

Before the end, I was a traveling hospice nurse. I took thirteen-week contracts in whatever town was short-staffed, which meant eleven states in six years. I always took the night shift, because nobody wants it. The people who come apart at three in the morning are the frightened ones. The day staff have gone home, and the families have gone to sleep or couldn't bear to stay. So I stayed. I sat by the bed and slid my hand under theirs, palm up, so they could hold on or let go as they needed. I told them the same thing every time.

*I'm here. You don't have to be alone.*

I never thought of it as anything special. It was just the job, the part of it that wasn't charts and morphine.

Standing in that lobby, I understood that I had never known how many of them there were. Some of these people might have been mine, the ones who died with my hand under theirs and woke up into this. The rest might have learned it from them. Maybe whatever is left in a mind like that travels the way a rumor does, or a lullaby. They don't remember my name or my face. They remember that one person, in the worst hour, stayed.

My hands were shaking. I put the axe down on the lobby floor, stepped forward, and laid my palm under the old man's.

His fingers closed around mine, cold as a doorknob in winter, and so gentle.

"I'm here," I said. My voice cracked on it. "You don't have to be alone."

A sound moved through the room. I'd call it a sigh, though they had no breath to make one. The crowd drew back, row by row, until a path ran clear down the length of the lobby to the pharmacy doors.

The old man walked me to them himself, and he never let go of my hand.
