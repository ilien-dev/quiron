**Door Closed**

The first thing I noticed was the heat, and the second was the quiet.

Fire stretched in every direction under a ceiling the color of an old bruise. The air smelled like struck matches. I turned in a full circle. No screaming, no crowds, no one at all.

"Hello?" My voice went nowhere.

I sat on a rock that was hot but, strangely, not hot enough to burn. I'd been there an hour or a year when I heard footsteps, unhurried, boots on cinders.

He wasn't what I expected. No horns, or if he had them, he'd left them at home. He wore a rumpled gray suit with the tie loosened, like a man at the end of a double shift, and carried a clipboard. When he saw me, his whole face lit up.

"Finally," he said. "The first one. Do you know how long I've been waiting?" He clicked a pen. "So. Who are you? What did you do? And how did you die? Take your time. I have, as it happens, nothing but."

"Daniel Okafor," I said. "Hospice billing coordinator, Columbus, Ohio. I died on a Tuesday, carrying groceries up the stairs. Heart attack, I think."

He wrote it down. "And what did you do?"

"Isn't that supposed to be your department?"

"I like to hear it said." He smiled, not unkindly. "It's different when it's said."

I looked at my hands. "I didn't murder anyone. I want to say that first."

"Noted."

"When our mother was dying, she wanted to come home from the hospital. It would've meant someone staying with her full time. I had a promotion coming. My brother Tobi had kids. We kept saying we'd figure it out, and we kept not figuring it out, and she died in a facility on a Thursday afternoon while I was in a budget meeting. Tobi called. I saw the phone buzz and let it go to voicemail, because I was presenting." I stopped. "That's the main one."

The Devil said nothing. The pen had stopped moving.

"There's more, but it's small. I told people I'd call and didn't. I was short with a waitress once and thought about it for eleven years. I never told Tobi I was sorry, because then we'd have to talk about it. Then I died on some stairs holding a bag of oranges."

"Eleven years," the Devil said quietly. "About the waitress."

"Yes."

He set the clipboard down and rubbed his eyes. "Want to know something funny, Daniel? This place is enormous. I built it for everybody. Every liar, every thief, every tyrant. I figured the line would wrap around the universe." He gestured at the endless fire. "And nobody comes. Do you know why?"

"Because they're in Heaven?"

"Because they forgave themselves." He said it like a confession. "Or somebody did it for them, or they just got tired of carrying it. Even the awful ones, more of them than you'd think, found some way to set it down at the end. A word, a look, a stranger holding their hand. The way out is never locked. It's barely a door."

"Then why am I here?"

"Because you're the first person in all of time who walked in on his own and shut it behind him."

I thought about that. The fire crackled, politely.

"The trouble with my work," he went on, "is that it's very hard to punish someone who's already doing a better job than I could. Eleven years over a waitress. Honestly, I'm a little intimidated."

I laughed, and it surprised me, a cracked, wet sound. "So what happens now?"

"Now?" He tore the page off the clipboard and let it burn away in his fingers. "Now I make you an offer. You tell me about your mother. Not what you did. Her. What she was like. I'll listen, because I'm very good at it, and nobody's ever asked me to."

"And then?"

He shrugged, and for a second he looked ancient and tired and almost hopeful. "Then we walk to the door. You go through it whenever you're ready. I'd just like the company until then."

I looked out at the endless, empty fire. Somewhere far off, faint as a phone buzzing in a pocket, something was ringing.

"She made the best jollof," I said. "Every Sunday."

The Devil sat down beside me on the rock, and listened.
