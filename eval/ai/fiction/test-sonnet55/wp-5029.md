# The Piper's Debt

The first bed was empty in March. By June, forty-one more were.

Nobody in Wapping could say how it was done. Windows latched, doors barred, a dog asleep across the threshold, and in the morning only cold sheets and a smell of crushed mint and wet earth. The magistrates blamed gypsies. The parish blamed the magistrates. The mothers blamed no one; they simply stopped sleeping, and sat in the dark listening for pipes.

Captain James Hook heard of it in a tavern, from a woman who had no business being brave enough to say it to him.

"They say you fear nothing," she said. Her son was seven. His name was Tom. "They say you've sailed past the edge of every chart."

"They say a great deal." Hook turned his tankard with the iron curve of his left hand. "Most of it flatters me."

"Then be flattered, and go."

He went. Not for gold, though he told the crew there would be gold. He had been a boy once, small in a place that did not want him, and he suspected that whatever took children was only a creature terrified of time. He knew something of that terror.

The *Jolly Roger* sailed second star to the right and straight on till the compass spun like a drunkard, and then the sea went green and warm and smelled of pine.

The island rose from the mist like something breathing.

Hook came ashore with twelve men and a lantern. Somewhere ahead a pipe was playing, a bright, reckless tune that made boots want to dance. Smee clapped his hands over his ears and wept.

"Wax, Mr. Smee," Hook said gently. "I told you to press it deeper."

They found the children in a hollow ringed with mushrooms: forty-two of them, barefoot, laughing, leaves tangled in their hair. Their eyes were wrong. Too bright, too green, as if something stood behind them looking out. A few had gone translucent at the edges, like candle flames seen through paper.

On a throne of root and antler sat a boy.

He looked fifteen. He had looked fifteen, Hook suspected, when Rome was a village. He wore ivy and a grin, and small horns shone in the firelight.

"A grown-up!" the boy said, delighted. "Look, everyone, one came all this way! Are you here to play?"

"I'm here to collect," said Hook.

"They don't want to go." Pan tilted his head. "Ask them."

Hook knelt before a girl of six. "Child. Do you know your name?"

She frowned, and for a moment the green dimmed. "Mary," she whispered. "Mary Ellis."

"Your mother is waiting, Mary Ellis."

"Don't be tedious." The pipes flared, and the girl's face smoothed over, bright as glass. "Mothers are always waiting. It's what they're for."

Hook stood. Steel was useless here; he had known that crossing the reef. What did a thing like this fear?

*Time.*

He sat down on the moss, which astonished everyone.

"You're very young," Hook said.

"I'm the oldest thing alive!"

"No. You've been fifteen for three thousand years. You've never had a birthday that mattered." He let that settle. "These children will be forty-two lanterns in your forest, and in a century you'll have forgotten their names, as you've forgotten all the rest. That's your forever. One afternoon, repeated until it rots."

The pipe faltered.

"I'm going to die, boy," said Hook. "Soon, probably. Bad hand, worse lungs. But Tom's mother will say her son's name on her last day on earth, and so will his grandchildren. That's what I have that you don't. Not magic. Consequence."

Pan's grin had gone strange, a thing wearing the shape of a smile. "You're lying."

"Pirates do. Not about this."

The silence lasted a long time. A child began to cry, then another, and it was the most human sound Hook had ever heard, because it was the sound of someone remembering they were homesick.

Pan stood. Beneath the ivy he looked, suddenly, very tired.

"Take them," he said. "They're dull when they cry."

They sailed at dawn, forty-two children asleep in hammocks and coils of rope, the green draining from their eyes like tide from sand.

Smee found the captain at the stern, watching the island dissolve.

"He'll just take more, Cap'n."

"Yes," said Hook. "And I'll come again."

He said it lightly. But behind them, thin as smoke, the pipes began to play, and for the first time they sounded less like a threat than a lullaby.

In Wapping, three weeks later, a woman sat awake by a candle out of habit. The door opened on a small, sunburnt boy with leaves in his hair, who said, "Mum, I had the strangest dream," and she did not ask which one. She only said his name, again and again, until it was worn soft as a coin. Captain Hook, who wanted no thanks, was not there to hear it.
